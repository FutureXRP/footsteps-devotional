# PART ONE

## Chapter One

The road was gone in the morning.

Not blocked. Steadfast had seen the road blocked twice, and both times there had
been something to look at. A slide of rock. A fallen tree. A place where a river
had decided differently. This was not that. He came out of the lodging with his
pack on and his boots laced, and he looked at the place where the road had been
for the nineteen years he had walked it, and there was grass there. Old grass.
Grass that had been growing a long time.

He stood in the doorway.

Then he walked out to where it should have been and put his boot on the ground and
pressed, as though the road were under the turf and could be found by pushing. He
went twenty paces north and tried again. He went forty paces south. He got down
and put his hand flat on the grass. He felt nothing but grass, and cold, and the
small movement of something living underneath.

The keeper of the lodging came out and stood with him and said nothing.

"It was here," Steadfast said.

"I know it was."

"I walked it in."

"I know you did. I watched you come."

"Then where is it."

The keeper looked out at the grass with the expression of a man reading something
in a language he only half has.

"I have seen this four times," he said. "Not here. Elsewhere. It is not the road
that moved."

Steadfast waited.

"It is the country," the keeper said. "The country has turned underneath it.
There is no walking back to a thing that is still there."

---

He had been Steadfast for six of the nineteen years, and had been given it, which
is the only way anybody gets one.

Before that he had been called something ordinary that he had got from his mother
and had never thought about. What happened was that there was a winter with a lot
of weather in it, and a stretch of the road above the ford went bad, and he walked
it anyway. Four times, in both directions, carrying for people who could not. At
the end of it a woman at the staging house said it in front of about a dozen
people, and it had stuck within a season.

He had been forty-one. He remembered being pleased in a way he was slightly
ashamed of, and going out afterwards and standing in the cold with his ears hot.

And then it had been his, and he had lived inside it, and it turned out to be a
good name to live inside because it told you what to do. When you did not know
what to do you did the steady thing. Everybody knew that was what you would do,
and after a few years you did not have to decide.

He was very good at the road.

That is not nothing, and he would like it said plainly, because everything that
came afterwards has a way of making it sound like it was not. He knew the water on
every stretch between the ford and the high crossing. He knew which of the
lodging-keepers watered the beer and which of them would sit up with a sick man.
He knew the six places where people came off it in the first fortnight, and why.
He could tell in about an hour of walking with someone whether they were going to
last, and he was right about it more often than anybody he ever met.

Nineteen years. He could have said what he was for in a sentence, and did, out
loud, to people, without any embarrassment at all.

---

He spent nine days looking for it.

That is the part he does not enjoy remembering. He went north along where it had
been, and then south. Then he did a thing he was ashamed of afterwards and went
out on a bearing at right angles for most of a day, on the theory that the road
might have been *displaced*, that it might be lying two miles east like a rope
kicked off a path. He stood at the end of that day in a field with cows in it.

He came back to the lodging every night because he did not know what else to do.
The keeper fed him and did not say anything about it, which Steadfast noticed at
the time and understood the size of much later.

On the fourth day he found the milestone.

It was where it should have been, on its side in the grass, half sunk, with the
figures still legible and moss in them. He got down and cleared it with his hands
and read it. It said what it had always said, which was a number and a place. Both
of them were still true and neither of them was any use, because there was no road
for them to be measured along.

He sat next to it for about an hour.

That was the first time he understood that the trouble was not that something had
been taken away. Everything was still there. The stone was there and the ford was
there and the high crossing was there, and the distances between them had not
changed by a yard. What had gone was the thing that joined them.

---

There were about thirty people at the lodging within the fortnight, and that was
when he made his mistake.

They came in off the country, in ones and twos, the way people do. Some of them
had been a day out and some of them a week. They came in with the same face on
them, all of them, and they came to him. Because of the name. Because he was the
one who had walked it nineteen years. Because a man in a bad hour will look round
a room for somebody who has done the thing longest.

And he told them it would come back.

He did not say it exactly like that. He was too careful for that, and had been
careful for nineteen years. What he said was that in his experience the road went
bad in patches and came good, and that he had walked the stretch above the ford
through a winter when everybody said it was finished, and that the sensible thing
was to stay put and keep together and not go off in weather on somebody's guess.

Every word of that was true, and every word of it was about a different thing. He
knew that within about ten days and went on saying it for six weeks.

He has thought about why. The answer is not complicated and does not improve with
looking at it. He said it because they were frightened and it steadied them. He
said it because saying it steadied him. And he said it because he was the man who
knew, and there is no way to stand in a room full of people who need you to know
and say *I have no idea* that does not cost you the only thing you have ever been.

## Chapter Two

They went anyway, over the winter, in the way that a thing empties rather than
ends.

Two of them went south in the first month and were sensible about it and said so,
and asked him to come. Four went to look for the high crossing. A family went back
east to a place they had come from. One man, who was not well, went out on his own
in a hard week in the second month and did not come back and was not found. There
is nothing to say about that except that Steadfast had told him the day before to
sit tight and wait.

By the thaw there were nine of them left, and none of the nine believed him.

Nobody was cruel about it. That was somehow the worst part. There was no scene.
Nobody stood up and said *you have been telling us this for four months and you do
not know*, and he sometimes wished afterwards that somebody had, because it would
have been quicker. What happened instead was that they stopped asking him things.
He noticed it over about three weeks. He would come into the room and a
conversation would go on rather than stopping and turning to him, and it took him
some time to work out that this was not rudeness.

He was not the man who knew. He had not been for months. They had all understood
it before he had, and had gone on being kind to him while he caught up.

---

The map came in the spring, with a man who had six of them.

He was pleasant and unhurried and did not oversell it. He spread one on the table
at the lodging and it was beautiful. Inked in three colours on good hide, with a
scale, and a compass rose in the corner drawn by somebody who could draw, and the
road on it as a double line running north and south, confident, unbroken, with the
lodging-houses along it as little squares.

Steadfast looked for a long time at the square with his own lodging's name on it.

"Have you seen it," he said.

"I have people out."

"That is not the question I asked."

The man did not take offence, which was somehow worse. He said, in the same
unhurried way, that no one man could walk all of it. That this was what surveying
was. That he marked the stretches he was less sure of in a paler hand, look, and
that he would rather sell an honest map with three doubtful sections on it than no
map at all, because people were going out on nothing.

And that, Steadfast thought, was true. That was the trouble with all of it. It was
true.

He did not buy the map. Two of the nine did.

He watched them go with it a week later. He stood at the door of the lodging and
did not say one word to either of them about what he had stood in on a cold
morning in the doorway behind him. He had spent four months telling people things
that steadied them, and had run out of any appetite for the sound of his own voice
being certain.

That is the whole of the reason. He would like it to have been a better one.

---

Three people asked him to come with them and he said no to all three.

The two who went south in the first month asked him honestly, and he said no
because he still believed the road would come back and thought them foolish for
going. That is the only one of the three he does not blame himself for, because at
least he had a reason he believed at the time.

The family going east asked in a general way, at a meal, in front of the children,
and he said no in a general way. Later he thought that the woman had meant it, and
had asked in front of the children so that it would be easier to accept.

And the keeper asked him, at the end, in the summer, when there were four of them
left and the lodging was going to close.

"Come with us," he said. "My brother has a place at the coast. There is room and
there is work and it is a long way from any of this."

"No."

"Why not."

And Steadfast could not answer him, and stood there. The keeper waited a long
time. Longer than was comfortable. Long enough that Steadfast understood
afterwards that it had been on purpose.

"All right," the keeper said. "Then take some food."

---

He went west because of the milestone.

That is the honest reason and it is not a good one. The figures on the stone were
a number and a place, and the place was west, and it had been the last true thing
he had touched. When the lodging closed and there was nowhere to be, he found that
his feet had an opinion.

He knew what was west. Everybody did. There was settled country for a while and
then there was not, and then there was a stretch of stone and grey scrub that had
a name in the way weather has a name. Nobody crossed it, because there was nothing
on the other side worth crossing to, and the people who had gone into it were
people who were not coming back.

He walked for eight days through country where there were still farms.

He was fed twice and turned away once, and slept in a barn with the owner's
knowledge and in two more without it. On the eighth day he passed a boy on a gate
who asked him where he was going, and he said west, and the boy said there was
nothing west, and he said he knew, and walked on. He heard the boy call something
after him that he did not turn round for.

Then the farms stopped.

There was no border. He noticed the ground going hard under him over about two
days, and the hedges giving out, and then one morning he came up out of a dip and
there was grey scrub in front of him as far as he could see in every direction,
low, ankle-high, going nowhere.

He stood at the edge of it for most of an hour.

Then he stepped into it, and that was the whole of the ceremony.

---

Four months.

He would come to say it in months for a long time after he should have stopped,
and there is not a great deal to say about them, which is itself the truest thing
about that country.

He learned what it had in it. Stone. Scrub that broke like old bread. Water in
seams, if you knew to look at the underside of a slope. He did not know, for the
first three weeks, and nearly died of not knowing, and then met a man who showed
him in about four minutes and would not stay to be thanked.

He tried to make fire twice. Both times he got a smell like scorched hair and a
handful of ash that blew off before it was cool. The second time he sat and looked
at his hands for a while, and then packed up and went on.

He met fourteen people in four months. Every one of them told him their account
inside a quarter of an hour, usually before he asked. Where they had come off. How
long they had been out. What they had been before. He understood that this was
what people did out there, and did it himself, and heard himself doing it, and
carried on doing it because there was nothing else to give anybody.

They asked his name a dozen times and he said it a dozen times.

That was the part he could not have explained to anybody, then or afterwards. Not
the thirst. Not the ground. Not the cold. That a dozen times in four months a
stranger in a grey country asked a man what he was called, and a man who had not
been steadfast about one single thing since a morning in a doorway heard himself
say the word out loud, in the ordinary way, like a fact, and got away with it.

---

On the last day of it he smelled smoke.

He was four months in by then and had stopped expecting anything, which is a thing
that happens to a man's face before it happens to his mind. He stopped where he
was and turned his head and could not credit it, because there was nothing in that
country that would take a flame and hold it. He had tried. He knew.

He stood for a while and it did not go away.

So he went towards it. Not quickly, because he had learned by then what quickly
cost, and not with any hope in particular, because hope was not a thing he had
carried in some weeks. He went towards it the way you go towards a sound in the
night: to find out, and to stop wondering.

It took him the rest of the afternoon.

And at the end of it there was a fold in the ground he had to come up over, and he
came up over it, and stopped.
