# THE WASTE

### Second installment — draft

---

## Seventeen

The cold came, and the garden went to almost nothing, and he learned that this was
normal and that she had been feeding him out of a store he had not known existed.

It was under the floor. She lifted three boards by the stove and there was a pit
lined with flat stones, and in it beans dried on the cane, and roots in sand, and
a hard grey cheese she had made from nothing he could identify and would not
discuss.

— How long does that last.

— Until it does not.

— That is not —

— It lasts to the middle of the cold if I am the only one, she said, and put the
boards back down.

He stood there while she swept the dust back over the joins with her foot.

— How long with two.

— Steadfast.

— How long with two.

— We will find out, she said. — Which is not the same as short. Go and get the
grey wood in before it gets damp.

He went and got the grey wood in. He was angry for two days in a way he could not
have justified to anybody, least of all himself, because she had never once
suggested he go, and because he had eaten from that pit every day for four months
without asking a single question about where any of it came from.

---

They worked the fourth side through the cold.

It went slowly, because the stone had to come from further out each week and
because the ground was hard and the days were short. He got better. He stopped
lifting everything. Somewhere in there he found that he could stand over a stone
with his hands at his sides and know, the way you notice you have learned a road.

She noticed before he did.

— You did not lift that.

— No.

— Hm, she said, and went back to her own end.

He was absurdly pleased for the rest of the day and told himself he was not.

---

## Eighteen

The woman came in the middle of the cold and was the easiest guest either of them
ever had.

That was the first thing about her and it went on being the thing. She came over
the fold at an ordinary walk in the middle of the morning, and stopped at the line
of laid stones where the fourth side was going to be, and looked at the garden, and
said: — May I come in through the wall or do I go round.

Grieve said: — There is no round.

Her name was Maud. She was perhaps forty-five. She ate what was put in front of
her and no more, and made no performance of any of it, and asked after the beans
like a woman asking after somebody's children.

She stayed two days.

She noticed the gutter on the first afternoon and stood under it with her head
back, working out the pitch of it, and asked who had done the repairs, and said
the third one was clever. She was out in the rows before Steadfast on the second
morning, working, and she worked fast and low and did not need telling anything.
She had done it before, she said. At home. A long time ago. She did not say where
home was and neither of them asked, because that was the house they kept.

On the second evening she took the bucket apart and mended the strap, which had
been going for a month and which Steadfast had been meaning to do. It was a proper
job. She sewed it back with a curved needle out of her own pack, and made the seam
sit flat, and put a patch under it where the wear was going to come next.

She told a story about a dog. Grieve laughed out loud, which Steadfast had heard
twice in eight months and had both times had to look at the fire.

---

She asked one thing, late, when the pot was off and the three of them were sitting.

— How far is it to anywhere from here.

Grieve answered her the way she answered everything, which was fully and without
decorating it. Nine days to a line of pale stone if you were sensible and routed by
water. Past the pale stone she did not know, because she had never been. East was
nothing and stayed nothing for as long as anybody had ever gone into it. South was
where most people came from and there was no reason to think it had improved.

Maud listened to all of it with her chin on her hand.

— Right, she said.

And that was the whole of it, and they went to bed, and Steadfast went over that
conversation many times afterwards and could not find anything in it.

---

In the morning she was packed and standing in the gap.

Grieve gave her the bundle. Bread, the hard cheese, a stoppered flask, and the
thing about not drinking the flask on the first day, and Maud said — I know, and
Grieve said — I know you know, and they both did the small laugh people do.

— Go well.

— Thank you for the two days.

She went out the way she had come, north-west, up over the fold, and she did not
turn round, and Steadfast went back to the wall.

---

He found the knife at the middle of the morning, clearing the table.

It was under the edge of the cloth. It was a good knife, better than anything in
the house — a working blade with a horn handle worn down to the shape of a hand
that was not his and not Grieve's — and he picked it up and turned it over and had
got as far as the door with it before the second thought arrived, which was the
needle-case, which was also there, on the shelf, where she had put it down after
the bucket.

He came back in with the knife in his hand.

— She has left her knife.

Grieve was at the stove.

She turned round, and she looked at the knife, and she did not say anything at
all, and the not saying anything went on a beat too long — one beat, no more — and
then she was sitting on the floor by the door pulling her boots on.

— She will be two hours off, said Steadfast. — I will go. I am quicker —

— Stay here and keep the fire up.

— Grieve —

— Keep the fire up, she said. — Put the green stuff on it. I want it seen.

Then she was out through the gap in the fourth side and going up the fold at a
pace he had not known she had.

---

He kept the fire up.

He built it stupidly big and put green stuff on it in armfuls, wet scrub and the
tops off the bean canes, and stood back and watched the column go up straight
because there was no wind, and then went and got more, and burned in one afternoon
what they would ordinarily have burned in nine days. He knew what he was doing to
the store of wood. He did it anyway. Twice he went up onto the fold and looked
north-west at nothing, and came back down, and put more on.

Grieve came in at dark.

She came in alone, and shut the door, and stood with her back against it for a
moment before she got her boots off.

— Well?

— She had two hours on me, Grieve said, — and she is faster than she looks.

She ate standing at the stove and did not sit down. She did not say anything else
about it that night, or in the week after it, or ever.

---

He asked her one question, at the table, a day later, and she answered it.

— How did you know.

— I did not know.

— You knew when I picked the knife up.

— I knew when you picked the knife up, she said. — That is not knowing. That is
being told.

He turned his cup round.

— Two days, he said. — She was here two days and she mended the bucket.

— Yes.

— And you did not —

— No, said Grieve.

That was the end of it. She got up and went out to look at the fourth side and
what he had done to the wood pile, and did not say anything about that either.

The knife stayed. She used it, every day, for everything, in the ordinary way,
because it was the best knife in the house and she was not a woman who would have
a good knife in a drawer. Steadfast could not look at it for about a month. Then
he stopped noticing it, which was worse, and he noticed that he had stopped
noticing it, which was worse again.

---

## Nineteen

The fourth side was two stones from finished on a grey afternoon when he
understood what finishing it meant.

He was carrying, and she was seating, and there was a gap the width of a forearm
left in the line, and he set the stone down beside it rather than handing it over
and stood up and looked at the whole thing. Four sides. A closed shape on the
ground with a house in it.

— What happens after this.

She did not look up. — After what.

— After the wall.

— The gutter needs doing before the rain. And the second row is finished, so that
comes out and goes over.

— No, he said. — After.

She sat back on her heels and wiped her wrist across her forehead and left dirt
there, and looked at the gap.

— There is always something.

— That is not what I asked.

— I know what you asked, she said.

She got up. She did not take the stone from him. She went and got the bucket and
started away toward the house with it and then stopped about six feet off, which
he noticed, because it was the distance she had stood from the man in the autumn.

— You have been asking me that since the first week, she said. — You have just
been asking it about the road.

Then she went in, and he stood in the gap in his own wall with a stone in his
hands and could not work out whether he had been understood or dismissed, and
found that he wanted very badly for it to be dismissed, because the other thing
was worse.

He did not put the stone in. He left the gap.

She did not mention it. It stayed open through the cold, one forearm's width, and
they both walked past it every day and neither of them said anything about it at
all, and that was the first thing they had that was not spoken about.

---

## Twenty

The girl came in the thaw.

She was young, which was the first thing, and she was not far gone, which was the
second. She came up out of the fold at a walk, not a stagger. She had a pack that
was still packed. She had been out twelve days.

— Twelve days, Grieve said. — You are quick.

— I ran the first three.

— Everyone does.

She ate like someone who had not yet learned to be ashamed of eating, and she
talked, and what she said joined up. Her name was Ash. She had been on the road
six years. She was going to cross the Waste and get to the other side of it,
where she believed there was something, and she asked them both, straight out,
across the table, whether they knew what was over there.

Grieve said no.

Steadfast said no, and heard how his own voice did it — flat, quick, closing —
and did not like the sound.

Ash slept in the lean-to. Steadfast lay by the stove and listened to the nothing
and did not sleep much.

---

In the morning she was up before either of them, packed, standing out by the gap
in the fourth side with her boots on.

— You are going, Grieve said, from the door.

— Yes.

— Which way.

— That way, said Ash, and pointed at the flat grey nothing to the east, and she
said it the way you would name a street.

Grieve nodded and went and got a cloth bundle from inside and brought it out and
put it in the girl's hands. Bread and the hard cheese and a stoppered flask.

— Two days of it, she said. — Do not drink the flask on the first day no matter
what you tell yourself about the second.

— Thank you.

— Go well.

And she stepped back.

Steadfast said: — Wait.

Both of them looked at him.

He had not known he was going to say it. He heard it come out and heard it land
and there was a silence of the kind that has weight, and Grieve did not move at
all, which was her giving him the chance to make it a smaller sentence.

He did not take it.

— It is fifteen days across at the narrowest and you are not carrying fifteen.
There is nothing east. I came from the east. There is nothing there but more of
it, and you will be four days out with an empty flask and no shade and no —

— Steadfast, Grieve said.

— She is nineteen.

— Twenty-two, said Ash.

— You are nineteen or you are twenty-two and either way you are going to die out
there, he said, and his voice had gone up and he could not get it down. — Stay
until the rains. Stay six weeks. Six weeks and you go with full water and you go
knowing where the ground rises, and we can tell you where the ground rises,
because she knows and she will tell you if you ask —

— Steadfast.

— Ask her, he said to the girl. — Ask her what is out there. She knows and she
will not say it unless you ask, that is how she does it, she will stand six feet
off and let you walk into it —

He stopped.

Nobody said anything for a while. A long while. The girl looked at the ground and
then at Grieve, and something passed between the two of them that he was not part
of and did not have the standing to be part of, and then Ash picked her pack up
off the stones.

— I would like the rise, she said to Grieve. — If you know it.

Grieve told her. It took about a minute. Third fold, keep the burnt scrub on your
left, do not follow the flat because the flat is longer, there is a seam under the
lip of the fourth rise that will give you a cup if you are patient with it.

Ash listened, and repeated the parts back, and went.

They watched her out to the top of the fold and over. She did not turn round.

---

## Twenty-One

He waited for it all day and it did not come.

She did the beans. She did the gutter joint that had been on her list since the
cold. She asked him at midday to bring the long-handled thing from the lean-to and
he brought it, and she said thank you, and that was the whole of it.

By evening he could not stand it.

— Say it.

— Say what.

— That I had no right.

She was scraping the pot. She put it down.

— You had no right, she said. — There. Does that help?

— No.

— No, she said. — It never does.

She sat down across from him and he saw that she was tired in a way she had not
been that morning, and understood that she had been carrying it all day too, and
had simply been carrying it without making him watch.

— She was going to ask me anyway, she said. — She had it in her hand. She was
going to ask me at the gap and I was going to tell her, and she would have gone
with it and it would have been hers.

— And now.

— And now she has it because a man shouted. And she took it anyway, which was
well done of her, because most of them would not have.

— That is the difference? That is the whole difference? Who says it first?

— Yes, Grieve said.

— That is a very fine distinction to hang somebody's life on.

— Yes, she said. — It is.

And she let that sit, and did not decorate it, and he wanted her to and she would
not.

— I have thought about very little else for eleven years, she said. — I have got
it wrong in both directions. I have watched two people walk east who I could have
stopped by putting my hand out, and I did not put my hand out, and I do not know
what happened to either of them and I never will. And I made one man stay four
years, and I know exactly what happened to him, because I had to watch it every
day at this table.

She turned the cup round.

— The ones I let go, I have to imagine. The one I kept, I do not have to imagine.
That is the only reason I do it this way. Not because it is right. Because I have
found out which of the two kinds of not-knowing I can carry.

Steadfast sat with that.

— That is not a principle, he said at last.

— No.

— You said it like a principle. In the autumn. You said it to me like it was a
principle and you had worked it out.

— I know.

— Why.

— Because you had been here three weeks, she said, — and you wanted there to be
one.

---

## Twenty-Two

They did not talk much for a while after that.

It was not a rupture. That was almost the worst of it — there was no scene to
have, nothing to apologise for that had not already been said plainly on the same
evening. They worked. They ate. She asked him to pass things and he passed them.
Some evenings they sat out for the orange hour the way they had, and it was fine,
and it was not the same, and he could not have said what the difference was except
that he was now aware of being a person who was in her house.

He thought about Ash constantly and did not mention her once.

He caught himself counting. Fourteen days. Nineteen days. Twenty-six. He did not
know what number would mean she was across or what number would mean the other
thing, and there was nobody to ask, because the only person who would know was the
person he was not going to ask.

On the thirty-first day he came in from the wall and Grieve was sitting at the
table doing nothing, which she never did.

— Thirty-one, she said.

He stood in the door.

— You have been counting.

— Since the morning she went.

He came in and sat down. Neither of them said anything about the number or about
what it meant or about whether there was a number that meant anything at all.

After a while she got up and put the pot on.

---

## Twenty-Three

He put the stone in on a still day at the end of the thaw.

He had not planned to. He was moving the last of the far pile and it was in his
hands and he was standing at the gap, and there was no reason not to and no ceremony
about it, and he turned it twice and found the face and set it in and it seated
first time without a sound.

Then he stood back and looked at the wall.

It went all the way round. Four sides, waist high, closed, with the house inside
it and the rows inside it and the barrels at the back, and his own section bulging
out of the north run like a swallowed thing, ugly, holding.

He waited to feel the thing he had expected to feel.

What came instead was so plain that he had to sit down on the wall he had just
finished. There was nothing left to build. There was the gutter, and the rows, and
the store under the floor, and the eleven days, and all of it would go round again
next year and the year after, and it was good, all of it was good, and it was
going to be the same.

He looked at the flat grey nothing to the east where the girl had gone.

Grieve came out with the basket on her hip and saw him sitting on the wall and
saw the wall, and stopped.

She did not say well done. She did not say anything for long enough that he looked
up at her.

— Right, she said.

— Right.

— That is that, then.

And she went past him to the rows and started work, and did not look at the fourth
side again that day, and he sat on the closed wall in the middle of the afternoon
and did not move for a long time.
