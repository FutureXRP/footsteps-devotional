# THE WASTE

### First installment — draft

---

## One

He smelled the smoke a long time before he found it, which frightened him, because
there was nothing in the Waste to burn.

He had been four months in it by then and had learned what it held.
Stone. A grey scrub that came up to the ankle and broke like old bread when you
stepped on it. Water in seams, sometimes, if you knew to look at the underside of
a slope. Nothing that would take a flame and hold it. He had tried, twice, and got
a smell like scorched hair and a handful of ash that blew off before it was cool.

So he followed the smoke because it was impossible, and came up over a fold in the
ground, and stopped.

It was a garden.

That was the thing he could not get past, standing at the edge of it with his hand
still on the strap of his pack. Beans up canes. Something low and green in rows,
four rows, with the soil between them dark and turned. A wall of stacked flat
stones about waist high running all the way round, which someone had carried there
one at a time from somewhere he could not see. And at the back of it a house, a
real one, with a roof and a chimney and a door.

A door. In the Waste. A thing that could be shut.

The wall was not finished. It ran three sides and stopped, and the fourth side was
open to the scrub with a line of stones laid out along it, spaced, waiting, like
something interrupted.

— You can come in, she said, without turning round. — You have been standing there
a while.

She was at the far end of the rows with her back to him, doing something to the
beans. He had made no sound he was aware of.

He came in through the gap in the wall because there was no gate, and the ground
under his boots changed, and that was what undid him — not the green, not the
smoke. The ground. It had been worked. It gave slightly. He had been walking on
stone for four months and had stopped noticing until it stopped.

---

She fed him.

She did not ask him anything until he had eaten, and she did not watch him eat. He
ate badly and fast and had to stop twice.
There was a stew of something with beans in it and there was bread, and the bread
was the thing that broke his composure, though he got through it without her
seeing.

Then she sat back and looked at him with her hands round a cup.

— How long have you been out?

— Four months.

— Four months, she said.

— Yes.

— You still say it in months.

She said it without any cruelty, the way you would tell a man he had cut himself
and not noticed, and Steadfast sat in her warm house with her food in him and felt
the floor go.

— What do they call you?

He had been asked that eleven times in four months and had answered it eleven
times without thinking, and he opened his mouth to do it again and could not get
the word out in front of her.

She waited.

— Steadfast, he said.

— Is that yours?

— It was given.

— By people who had watched you do something.

— Nineteen years of it.

She nodded, and did not smile, which he had been afraid she was going to do.

— All right, she said. — Steadfast.

She used it after that in the ordinary way, every day, for everything. Pass me
that, Steadfast. The light is going, Steadfast. He flinched at it for weeks and
she never once made anything of the flinch.

— How long have you been here, he said.

— Eleven years.

He waited for the rest of it, the account, the how and the why, because everyone
he had met in four months had given him theirs within a quarter of an hour,
usually before he had asked. She drank her tea.

— Eleven years, he said.

— Yes.

— And you have not —

— No.

She put the cup down and stood and started clearing, and he understood that the
conversation was not being refused, exactly. It was being deferred to a time when
it would be less interesting to both of them.

---

## Two

He meant to stay two nights.

He said so, that first evening, and she said that was fine and showed him where he
could sleep, which was a lean-to on the north side of the house with a straw
mattress and a blanket that smelled of the smoke. He lay in it and listened to
nothing — the Waste made no sound at night, no insects, no wind in anything — and
in the morning she was already out and there was porridge on the stove and a note
of the kind you leave for somebody you are not worried about.

*Water in the barrel. Do not use the second barrel.*

He used the first barrel. Then he stood in the doorway and looked at her working
in the rows and could not think of a reason to leave that day rather than the
next.

By the fourth morning she put him on the wall.

— Can you lay stone?

— No.

— Good. Neither could I.

She showed him. It was less like building than like a puzzle with no picture: you
put your hand on a stone and you did not pick it up yet, you turned it where it
lay and looked at the face it presented, and if the face was wrong you left it and
tried another, and the whole art of it was in not lifting anything until you were
sure. Lifting was the expensive part. She could tell from six feet whether a stone
would seat.

He was terrible at it. He lifted everything. By noon his back had a hot wire in it
and his section had a bulge in the middle where he had forced two stones to sit
together that did not want to.

— That will come down, she said, going past with a bucket.

— I can take it out.

— No. Leave it. It will come down on its own or it will not, and either way you
will learn more from watching it than from me telling you.

It did not come down. He watched it for days, expecting it, and it stood, ugly,
out of line with everything on either side of it, a bulge in an otherwise honest
wall.

---

The water was the thing he could not work out.

There was no well. He looked. There was no spring, no seam in the rock anywhere
within an hour of the house, and he walked the ground for an hour in every
direction on the sixth day, partly to see and partly because he had begun to want
a reason to be useful and had not found one.

He came back and she was sitting on the step.

— There is no water here, he said.

— No.

— Then where.

She got up and took him round the back of the house, and it was so simple he was
angry about it for an hour afterwards. The roof was pitched to a gutter of split
wood. The gutter ran to the barrel. Beside the barrel was the second barrel, the
one he was not to use, and beside that a run of flat stones set into the ground
sloping to a sunk basin lined with clay.

— It rains eleven days a year here, she said. — Sometimes fourteen.

— Eleven days.

— You have to be very good at eleven days.

He looked at the gutter, which was old and had been repaired in four places, each
repair a different wood.

— And the second barrel.

— Is for when I have got the first one wrong.

---

## Three

He asked about the road on the ninth day. He had been building up to it and made a
poor job of the approach.

— When you came out. Did yours go the same way, or —

— Steadfast.

— I only mean, if it was the same, then someone must —

— Steadfast.

She was kneeling in the second row with her hands in the dirt and she did not
stop what she was doing.

— I am not going to talk about the road.

— Why not.

— Because you want me to help you find it, and I cannot, and after we have talked
about it for a week you will be angry with me for not being able to. I would
rather skip that part. I have done it before and it always goes that way.

She pulled something and shook the soil off it and set it in the basket.

— How many times, he said.

— You are the ninth.

That stopped him. He had been thinking of himself as a weather event.

— What happened to the other eight.

— Six went on. Two went back. One of the two who went back came through again
about a year later going the other way and did not stop, and I have thought about
that one more than the other eight together.

— Why.

— Because he saw the smoke, she said. — And chose not to come and stand at my
wall. And I have never worked out whether that was strength or the other thing,
and I would like to know, and I never will.

She sat back on her heels then and looked at him properly for the first time
since the meal.

— That is the last question about the road you get for a while. Have it back if
you want to spend it differently.

— No, he said. — That was a good one.

— It was, she said, and went back to the row.

---

## Four

The visitor came in the third week.

Steadfast saw him first, from the wall, and did not recognise what he was looking
at for a moment because the man was moving so slowly. He came across the scrub
from the north in a diagonal, stopping often, not the stop of a man resting but
the stop of a man who has forgotten mid-stride what he is doing and has to wait
for it to come back.

Grieve was beside him before he had said anything.

— Fill the pot, she said. — Not the good bread. The other one.

— Do you know him?

— No.

She went out to meet him past the line of laid stones, and Steadfast watched her
do a thing he thought about for a long time afterwards. She did not help him. She
walked out and stopped six feet short of him and stood, and said something he
could not hear, and waited. The man said something. She waited again. It went on
like that for a while, the two of them standing in the open with a gap between
them that she did not close, and then the man came forward on his own feet and she
turned and walked in beside him without touching him.

The man ate. He did not talk much and what he said did not join up. He had been a
builder, or his father had. He had a daughter. There was a thing about a horse
that started twice and did not finish either time. Grieve asked him nothing except
whether he wanted more, four times, and each time he did.

He slept in the lean-to and Steadfast slept on the floor by the stove and did not
mind.

In the morning the man was gone. He had taken nothing, which Steadfast checked,
and was ashamed of checking.

— Will he be all right?

— No, she said.

— Then why did you not —

— Say it.

— Make him stay.

Grieve was scraping the pot out and she stopped and held it against her hip.

— Because he did not ask, she said. — And because the last time I made someone
stay who had not asked, he stayed four years and it was not a kindness, it was me
being unable to watch someone walk off into it. He hated me by the end and he was
right to. Now I feed them and I let them go and I am wrong about half of them and
I do not know which half.

She went back to the pot.

— That is the honest answer. There is a nicer one and I could give it to you but
you would use it later on someone who deserves the honest one.

---

## Five

The rain came at the end of the fifth week.

She was up before it, in the dark, and he woke to her moving and came out and
found her on the roof with a rag in each hand doing something to the gutter joints
by feel. There were no clouds he could see. The sky was the same nothing it always
was.

— How do you know.

— My hip. And the beans go quiet. Get the second barrel round.

It rained for nine hours. He had not seen rain in four months and had forgotten
that it made noise, and the noise on the roof was so loud they had to raise their
voices in the house. The gutter took it. The first barrel filled in two hours and
the run-off went down the flat stones to the basin and the basin filled and the
overflow from that went out along a channel she had cut into the rows so the water
walked itself down between the beans.

She stood in the doorway with her arms folded, wet to the shoulder, and watched
the channel work, and she was so plainly happy that he had to look away.

Around the fourth hour a joint went on the north side and the water started coming
off the roof in a sheet instead of into the gutter, and she was up the ladder
before he had the door open. He went up after her. It was two of them on a wet
roof with a bad joint and no light, and she showed him where to put his hands and
he put them there and they held it, physically held the wood together, for the
better part of an hour until the rain slackened enough for her to get a wedge in.

They came down black with wet. She was laughing. It was the first time he had
heard it.

— That is eleven days, she said. — That was one of them.

Then she looked at the wall, at the ugly bulged section he had built, running with
water, standing.

— Huh, she said.

That was all. She went in to get dry.

---

## Six

He stopped counting in months somewhere in the second season and did not notice
until she said something.

They were sat on the step at the end of a day. The light in the Waste went orange
about an hour before it went, and it was the only time the country was anything
other than grey, and they had got into the habit of being outside for it without
having discussed it.

— You have been here a while now, she said.

— Since the rain.

— No. Before that.

— Since a while before that, he said.

She smiled at the middle distance.

— There, she said.

— There what.

— Nothing. You did it right.

He worked it out about a minute later and sat with it. He did not feel that he had
arrived anywhere. He felt as though something had been taken from him quietly
while he was looking at the beans.

— Is that good, he said.

— It is neither.

— That is not an answer.

— No, she said. — It is not.

And then, because he had learned by then that she gave you the real thing if you
let the silence run rather than filling it, he let the silence run.

— It happens, she said eventually. — To everyone who stays past the first winter.

— That is not an answer either.

— No.

The light went. She got up and knocked the dirt off the back of her skirt.

— The wall, she said. — Tomorrow. I want the fourth side up before the cold, and
you are quicker now, and you still lift everything.

— I do not lift everything.

— You lift everything.

She went in. He sat a minute longer in the dark and found that he was thinking
about the fourth side of the wall and what stones were near it and how many days
it would take them, and that he had not thought about the road since some morning
he could not now identify.

He noticed that.
