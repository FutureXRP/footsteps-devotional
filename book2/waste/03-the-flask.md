# THE WASTE

### Third installment — draft

---

## Twenty-Four

The second year was easier, and that was the problem.

He knew the work now. He knew that the beans went in when the ground took a thumb
to the second knuckle and not before, and that the third row drained badly and
wanted the shallow end, and that the grey wood had to come in a fortnight earlier
than seemed necessary. He stopped being told things. Some time around the middle
of it he noticed that she had stopped watching him work, which was the highest
thing she had ever said to him and she had not said it.

The rains came late.

He knew they were coming before she did, or at the same time — he could not tell,
because when he came out that morning to say so she was already on the roof.

— The joints, he said.

— The north one.

— It will be the north one again.

— It is always the north one.

They did the north joint. Then it rained for nine hours and the gutter took it and
the barrel filled in two hours and the run went down the flat stones to the basin
and the basin filled and the overflow walked itself down between the rows, and
nothing went wrong, and there was nothing for either of them to hold together on a
wet roof in the dark.

They stood in the doorway and watched it work.

— Good, she said.

— Yes.

And it was good. That was the whole of it. It was good and it had been good last
year and it would be good next year, and he stood there in the doorway of a house
that was not his, dry, fed, competent, watching a system he now understood do
exactly what he had known it would do.

He did not say much for the rest of that week, and she did not ask.

---

## Twenty-Five

He found himself keeping a list.

Not on paper. In his head, in the evenings, and it was a list of reasons to be
there, and he did not notice he was keeping it until he noticed he was checking
it.

The wall was done. That one had gone in the spring.

The store had been dug out and relined the summer before; that one had gone too.

He had cut and hung a proper door for the lean-to, which was the last thing on it
of any size, and he had done that in four days at the end of the cold, and it hung
well, and every time he went through it now he thought: that is done as well.

By the end of the second year the list was: she is here.

He turned that over on the step one evening and found that it did not frighten him
the way he had expected. What
frightened him was the sentence underneath it, which he got to about a week later
and which was: *and I have stopped asking whether that is a reason.*

He had walked the King's Road nineteen years. He had known what he was for on
every single one of those days, and had been able to say it in a sentence, and had
said it to people, out loud, without embarrassment.

He could not now say what he was for. He could say what he did. He could say it in
enormous detail — he could tell you the drainage of the third row and the fortnight
of the grey wood and the exact face of every stone in eighty feet of wall — and
none of it added up to an answer, and the not-adding-up had stopped bothering him
some time in the previous year without his permission.

That was the thing that frightened him. Not that the road had gone. That he had
stopped looking at the place where it had been.

---

## Twenty-Six

Corr came in the second summer and was the worst of them.

He came over the fold in the afternoon at a good pace for a man of sixty, and
stood in the gap that was no longer a gap, and looked at the beans and the rows and
the house and the smoke going up out of the chimney, and said:

— Well. Somebody has done all right for themselves.

He was not half dead. He was not anything. He had food in his pack and a fat on
him that nobody four months out of the south has, and it came out over the meal
that he had been three weeks on the edge of the country and not in it, working
south along the fringe where there were still people, and had come up for a look, and he came in and sat down at
the table without being asked twice and ate a great deal and said the cheese was
strong.

He asked how much was in the barrel. He did not like the answer.

— And you have not dug.

— There is no water under this ground, said Steadfast.

— Men say that about most ground.

He asked what they did about the cold. He asked whether the beans were worth the
work, given the water. He looked along the north run of the wall from where he was
sitting, and found the bulge inside a minute, which nobody else ever had, and said
— Who did that one, and Grieve said — He did, and Corr laughed for a while.

Steadfast washed the pot with his back to the room and found that he was in a
state he had not been in for two years and did not have anything to do with it.

---

Then Corr said, with his mouth full: — How did you end up out here, then.

And Grieve told him.

— I came out of the south with my sister. She died on the sixth day. I sat down
where I was and did not get up for a while, and while I was sitting there it
rained, and I was still there when it rained again.

Corr chewed.

— And you never went on.

— No.

— Seems a waste, he said. — Is there more of that bread.

There was more of the bread.

---

He stayed four days, which was two more than anybody had, and he was work the
whole time. He did not lift anything. He watched Steadfast lift things and told
him about a better way that he had seen once. He wanted his water at the times he
wanted it. On the third evening he explained the road to them both — where it had
gone and why and whose fault it had been — for the better part of an hour, and it
was all nonsense, and Grieve listened to the whole of it and asked him two
questions that let him go on longer.

On the fifth morning he went.

He got to the wall and turned round with his pack on and looked back at the roof,
and said: — Your north gutter joint is going. Not this year. Next.

Then he went over the wall and away south, and he was right about the joint; they
had it off within the week and the wood behind it was soft as bread.

---

Steadfast said it that night and could not stop himself.

— You should not have answered him.

— Which part.

— About your sister. He was rude to you at your own table and he was rude about
the wall and he ate a fortnight of the store and he called your life a waste with his
mouth full, and you sat there and told him.

— He asked, said Grieve.

She said it the way she said the beans wanted the shallow end.

Steadfast put his hands flat on the table.

— I have been here two years.

— Yes.

— Two years. And I did not know you had a sister.

Grieve put the cup down and looked at him, and he saw her decide not to make it
easier for him, and he watched her do it.

— No, she said. — Nobody has asked me.

— I would have asked.

— Yes, she said. — Probably.

She took the cups to the basin.

He sat at the table for a long time after she had gone through to the other side of
the room, and what he did with the time was go back over the two years looking for
the place where he could have asked, and what he came out with instead was that he
had asked her a great many questions in two years and that every single one of
them had been about him.

He did not ask her about the sister. Not that week, and not in the month after it,
and not on the last morning, and he was never afterwards able to give himself a
reason for that which sounded like anything except the true one.

---

He did ask her about the number, a few days later, out at the rows, because it was
easier.

— That is four since I came.

— Yes.

— Is that a lot.

— It is a wave, she said. — There was one about six years ago and then almost
nothing for four years and then this.

— Why.

— I do not know. I only know it is not steady.

She went on down the row on her knees.

— Do not make anything of it, she said. — I did, the first time.

---

## Twenty-Seven

He asked her at the end of the summer, badly, in the middle of doing something
else.

They were lifting the second row, which was finished, and the work was on hands
and knees and slow, and he said it into the dirt without looking up because he had
worked out that he would not be able to say it to her face.

— Come with me.

Grieve did not stop working.

— No.

He sat back on his heels.

— I have not said when.

— No is not about when.

She went on pulling. He watched her hands do it, which he had watched a thousand
times, and found that he was angry in the particular way you are angry with
somebody who has answered too quickly, even though he knew she had not answered
too quickly, she had answered in eleven years.

— You have not thought about it.

— Steadfast.

— One night. Think about it for one night and then say no and I will never —

— I have thought about it every time somebody has come over that fold, she said.
— Thirteen times. And the answer has been the same thirteen times and it was not a
different question when it was you.

That landed harder than she had meant it to. He saw her see it land.

— That was not — she said, and stopped, and started again. — The answer being the
same does not mean the question weighed the same. It weighed more.

She sat up and put her wrists on her knees.

— Ask me why. You have never asked me why. You have asked me about the road and
about the water and about what is east, and you have never once asked me why I
stay.

He asked her why she stayed.

— The smoke, she said.

He waited for the rest.

— That is it. That is the whole of it. There is nothing here that burns. If I go,
in a year there is no smoke over this fold, and the next one who comes out of the
south four months in and half dead walks past a mile east of here and never knows
he walked past anything.

— Someone else would —

— No, she said. — In eleven years, nobody else has. I have been the only fire in
this country for eleven years and I have counted thirteen people, nine of them
before you, and if you take the thirteen off the number and think about how many
did not come close enough to see it, that is what I do instead of sleeping.

She said it flatly, without any grandeur at all, the way a woman tells you what
her job is.

He said: — Then let me stay.

And she said: — No.

That was the one that took his breath. He had expected a fight about going. He had
not prepared anything for this.

— That makes no sense. You have just told me the whole thing runs on somebody
being here. I am here. I am good at it now, you have said so, you have —

— I made a man stay once, she said.

— That is not what this is.

— No. It is the other one. He asked and I said yes. I have thought for a long time
about which of those two was worse and I have not got anywhere with it, and I am
not doing either of them again.

— That is not your decision.

— It is exactly my decision, she said. — You are asking me for a reason to stay.
That is what let-me-stay is.

— That is not what I —

— It is. And I will not give you one, because you will take it, and it will hold a
year and possibly five, and then one morning you will be standing in that doorway
watching the water go down the rows and you will work out that the reason was mine
and not yours.

She rubbed the dirt off her palms, one and then the other.

— I have seen that face. I have looked at that face across this table for four
years.

She picked up the basket.

— If you stay, stay because you have found something here that is yours. Not
because I said the smoke needs two.

Then she took the basket in, and left him on his knees in the second row with the
whole afternoon in front of him.

---

## Twenty-Eight

It could not be unasked.

That was the discovery of the following weeks, and it was worse than a quarrel
would have been, because there was nothing to repair. She was not cold to him. She
did not avoid the subject; twice she raised things adjacent to it herself, easily,
to show that it could be raised. They worked. They ate. The orange hour still
happened.

But he now knew the answer to a question he had spent two years not asking. Before,
the days had simply been the days. Now each one was a day on which he had chosen to stay, and the choosing
was in the room, and it was in the room for her too, and neither of them could put
it back.

He caught himself once, in the third week, doing the arithmetic on the store. Two
of them, the cold coming, the second year's yield down on the first because of the
late rains. He stopped himself and was ashamed, and then a day later did it again.

He knew then that he was going. He did not admit it for a month.

---

## Twenty-Nine

He told her on a morning that had nothing to recommend it.

— I am going to go.

Grieve was at the stove. She did not turn round straight away, and when she did
her face had nothing on it that he could read, and he understood that this was not
because she felt nothing but because she had had a month to decide what her face
would do.

— When.

— Before the cold. So there is time.

— Yes, she said. — There is time.

She thought.

— Not before the rains. Go after. You want to go out with the seams full behind
you, and the fourth rise will hold a cup for a week after and not longer.

— All right.

— And not the flat. Everyone goes on the flat because it is flat.

— Not the flat.

— No.

She turned back to the stove.

That was the whole conversation. He had built about a dozen versions of it over the
month and none of them had been that one, and he stood in the middle of the room
for a while afterwards with his hands at his sides.

---

The rains came and went. He worked the last four weeks harder than he had worked
any four weeks in the two years, and she let him, and neither of them said what
that was.

He relaid the whole north run of the wall. He did not ask. He took it down in
sections and set it properly, and it took eleven days, and when he got to his own
piece — the bulge, the two forced stones, the ugly swallowed thing in the middle
of eighty feet of honest wall — he stopped with his hands on it.

— Leave that, she said, from the rows. She had not appeared to be watching.

— It is the worst thing here.

— Leave it.

He left it.

---

## Thirty

She packed him two days of food.

He watched her do it, standing in the door of the house with his pack already on,
and she did it exactly the way she had done it for the woman in the cold and for
the girl in the thaw, the same cloth, the same three things, and neither of them
said that and both of them knew it, and he had to look at the doorframe for a
while.

Bread. The hard cheese. A stoppered flask.

— Two days of it, she said, and put it in his hands. — Do not drink the flask on
the first day, no matter what you tell yourself about the second.

— I know.

— I know you know. I am saying it.

— Yes.

She wiped her hands down her front.

— Third fold, keep the burnt scrub on your left. The seam under the lip of the
fourth rise will give you a cup if you are patient with it. After that you are past
where I know and I have nothing for you.

He knew the words. He had heard them said twice already in this yard, to a woman
who had left a knife on the table and to a girl standing where he was standing with
her pack still packed, and he held the bundle and waited for Grieve to say
something about that.

She did not.

He could not tell whether she had spared him, or whether there was simply one way
out of that country and she had only ever had the one thing to give anybody.

— Thank you, he said.

— There is a line of pale stone about nine days out, if you get that far. Cross
it and stop looking behind you, because from there the ground reads differently
and looking behind you will put you wrong.

He nodded. He could not do anything else.

They stood there. The wall went all the way round them, waist high, closed, and
the smoke went up straight because there was no wind.

— Grieve, he said.

— No, she said. — Not on the step. Whatever it is, it will come out wrong on the
step and you will carry the wrong version of it for a year. Go on.

So he went.

He got to the gap that was not a gap any more, the place in the fourth side where
the stone had gone in, and had to step over the wall like everyone else, and it
was waist high and he was not young and it was undignified, and behind him he
heard her make a small sound that might have been a laugh.

He got up onto the fold and did not turn round until the top of it.

She was already back in the rows. She was not watching him go. He had known she
would not be and had wanted to check, and having checked, he understood that she
had gone back to the rows early on purpose, so that when he looked he would see
exactly this, and be able to go.

— Steadfast, she called, without straightening up. — The flask.

— On the second day.

— Good.

And that was the last thing she said to him, and it was about water, and it was
the kindest thing anyone had said to him in two years.

He went down the far side of the fold and the smoke went out of sight behind him
about a quarter of an hour later, and he stopped once to look for it and could not
see it, and after that he did not stop again until the light went.
