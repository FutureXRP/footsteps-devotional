# Draft — ninth installment

---

## Fifty-Five

It took him a hundred and four days and he was fifty-nine years old, and there
were four occasions on it when he thought he had made a mistake that would kill
him, and one of them nearly did.

He will not be interesting about the journey. He had Petch's board and it was good
for the first three weeks and then he was past where he had told Petch anything.
He had Ivo's flask, which was better than the old one, and he took it out at a seam
under a lip nine days west of the garden and held it up to the light at the angle,
and did not laugh this time.

He came at the country from the wrong side, which is to say from the side he had
left by, and everything he had been told to look for he had been told to look for
going the other way. The seam under the fourth rise took him two days to find
because he was reading the ground reversed. The third fold he walked past twice.

And then on the ninety-ninth day he came round a shoulder and there was a stand of
burnt scrub on his right hand, and he stopped so fast he hurt his back.

He stood and looked at it for a while, on the right, where it had always been on
the left.

Then he sat down on his pack in the middle of nowhere and put his hands over his
face for a minute, and got up, and went on.

---

## Fifty-Six

He smelled the smoke on the afternoon of the fourth day after that, and it
frightened him, though by then there was nothing frightening about it.

The garden had a second wall.

That was the first thing, from the top of the fold. The old wall was there, waist
high, the whole way round, four sides, and running out from the north-east corner
of it was a second enclosure about half again the size with a lower wall and rows
in it he did not recognise. There were more canes. There was a lean-to on the
south side that had not existed, with a proper door on it, hung well.

And there was a second building, small, thirty yards off, with its own chimney and
smoke coming out of it.

He stood on the fold for longer than he meant to.

She was in the far rows and she had seen him. He could tell, because she had
stopped, and had straightened up with a hand in the small of her back, and was
standing there facing him, and had been for some time.

He came down.

She came out through the gap that was not a gap any more — there was a gate in it
now, hung on two straps of leather, standing open — and walked out from her own
wall onto the open ground, and stopped.

Six feet.

Twenty-one years of it, the distance she had put between herself and everyone who
had ever come over that fold, and he knew the exact number of paces and he stopped
at it out of pure habit, at the place, on his side of it, because that was what he
had been taught by watching.

And Grieve looked at him standing there at her own distance, and made a sound that
was almost a laugh and was not, and crossed it, and got hold of him.

She was not a large woman and she was sixty and she nearly had him over.

---

They did not say anything for a while. Then she stood back and looked at him
properly and said:

— You are grey.

— Yes.

— Steadfast, you are —

She stopped. She had said it in the ordinary way, the way she had said it four
hundred times over two years, pass me that, the light is going, and it had come out
of plain habit before she had thought about it, and she heard it, and he watched
her hear it.

— No, she said. — Sorry.

— It is all right.

— It is not. I knew that before you left and I said it every day anyway.

— You said it every day because I could not, said Steadfast. — I worked that out
about four countries ago and I have wanted to tell you since.

Grieve looked at him for a moment.

— Well, she said. — Come in. There is somebody in the house and I do not want to
leave him long.

---

## Fifty-Seven

His name was Aldo and he was very old and he was not entirely there.

He was in the house, in a chair by the stove that had been made for him — the joints
were wrong, it was a chair somebody had built without much idea how — with a
blanket over his knees, and he looked up when they came in and said something
pleasant and slightly beside the point, and Grieve answered it as though it had
been the point.

She fed him. She fed him *by hand*, some of it, cutting things up small and putting
them into his hand and putting his hand where he could find his mouth, and talking
the whole time in a low continuous way that did not require answering.

Steadfast stood in the door of the house he had lived in for two years and watched
a woman who had once let a girl walk east into nothing with a flask and a bundle
because the girl had not asked her to stop.

He got it, then, and it went through him like cold water.

There had never been a method. He had spent nine years turning a distance and a
silence into a doctrine, and building a whole practice out of it, and getting it
wrong with a young man over a map. She had stood off from the man in the autumn
because that man needed her to stand off. She had let Ash go because Ash was
twenty-two and packed and going. She had fed Steadfast first and asked nothing
because he had been four months out and could not have answered anything.

And she was cutting an old man's food up small because that was what was in front
of her now.

— How long has he been here.

— Two years in the spring. He came over the fold on his own feet and he could talk
then. He is somebody's father and I do not know whose and neither does he now.

— And you —

— I built him a chair, Grieve said. — It is a bad chair. I am not Ivo.

Steadfast opened his mouth and shut it.

— You do not know who Ivo is, he said.

— No, said Grieve. — But you said it like I would.

## Fifty-Eight

He told her on the second evening, out on the step, because he could not do it in
the house.

He had rehearsed it for a hundred and four days and every version had been wrong,
and what came out in the end was:

— There is a fire four days east of here.

Grieve did not say anything.

— On the hard ground where a cart can come within two days. There is a stone hut
with water in it and dry wood and food in a sealed pit, and a brazier on the roof
that whoever is living there keeps lit. Four households out of a valley eight
hundred miles from here take it a year each. The first one has been in it a year
and a half.

Still nothing.

— And there was already one, he said. — Forty miles north of where we built. A man
and two women at a seam. Three years before we got there and they had never heard
of anybody and neither had we.

He made himself stop.

Grieve sat with her hands round her cup and looked out at the dark where the Waste
was, and did not move for a long time, and Steadfast, who had learned to let a
silence run three beats past comfortable, let this one run as long as it wanted.

— Say the number, she said at last.

— What?

— The number they have had. At the hut. Say it.

— Nineteen, he said. — In eighteen months. The first household counted because I
asked them to.

Grieve put the cup down on the step.

Then she put her hand over her mouth and made a sound he had never heard out of
her and hoped never to hear again, and he did not move, and she got hold of the
edge of the step with her other hand and held on to it and cried the way people
cry who have been standing up for a very long time.

He did not touch her. That was not restraint and it was not method. He simply
looked at her and understood, correctly, that she was not crying about the
nineteen.

---

She said it herself, later, when she could.

— I have thought about that number every night for twenty-one years, she said. —
The one I do not have. The ones who came over that ground and did not come close
enough, and I have made them up, I have made up faces, I have made up a boy of
about fourteen and I have thought about him for eleven years and he does not exist,
I invented him because the number needed a face.

She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist and left dirt there, and he could
not look at her for a second.

— And you are telling me some of them are nineteen.

— Some of them are nineteen.

— And some of them are still the other thing.

— Yes, said Steadfast, because it was true and because she would have known if he
had softened it.

— Yes, said Grieve.

She picked the cup back up.

— Good, she said. — That is the right answer. If you had told me it was all of
them I would have thrown you off the fold.

## Fifty-Nine

He saw the wall on the third morning.

He had walked it the first evening in the dark and had not looked, or had not let
himself, and on the third morning he went out early because he always did and
walked the whole four sides in the flat light with his hands behind his back.

It was good. The north run had settled and been repointed. The gate was hung well.
The second enclosure was newer work and had been done by more than one pair of
hands, and he stood at the join for a while and could see exactly where the
sections changed.

And in the middle of the north run, in eighty feet of honest wall that had been
taken up and relaid at least once since he left, there was a bulge.

Two stones forced together that did not want to go, out of line with everything on
either side of it, ugly as sin, load-bearing.

She had taken the wall down and put it back up and left it in.

He stood in front of it for a long time. Then he put his hand flat on it, the way
he had put his hand flat on the grass in a doorway a thousand miles east of
there, and left it there a moment, and took it away.

He did not ask her about it. She did not mention it. Neither of them ever did.

## Sixty

The stranger asked on the fifth day and it was almost funny that it was him.

There was a man passing — not stopping, a lean quick man who took food and would
not sit down, going somewhere with his own reasons — and he was in the yard with
his pack on and he said to Grieve, jerking his chin at Steadfast:

— And him. What is he called.

Steadfast said: — I have not got one just now.

He said it the way he had said it to an eight-year-old on a kerb, flatly, without
any weight on it, and it was the fourth or fifth time he had said it in seven
years and it had stopped being difficult.

The lean man looked at him as though he were slightly simple.

Grieve said, without looking up from what she was doing: — He is the ninth.

The man waited for more, did not get it, decided it was not worth his trouble, and
went.

Steadfast stood in the yard.

— The ninth, he said.

— That is what you are. You are the ninth one who came over that fold and found
it. I have had thirty-one now. You are still the ninth.

She said it the way she said everything, as a fact about the arrangement of the
world that she had been carrying so long she had stopped noticing the weight of
it, and then she looked up and saw his face and stopped.

— Is that wrong?

— No.

— Because I can —

— No, said Steadfast. — No. Leave it.

And that was the whole of it, and it was not a ceremony, and nobody said anything
else about it, and by the end of that week it was what she called him and it did
not sound strange in her mouth at all.

## Sixty-One

He stayed eleven weeks.

He worked. Of course he worked. He did the gutter properly, with Ivo's four
questions in his head and Ivo's drawing in his hand, and it was better than her
gutter and she made him show her twice and then said, — Well, that is annoying, and
used it.

He took the old man out into the sun on the good days, chair and all, and sat with
him, and had long conversations with him that were about four different things at
once and did not need to be about anything.

He built forty yards of a new wall on the west side of the second enclosure and
did it properly, and Grieve watched him lay six stones without lifting any of them
and said nothing at all, and went inside, and he heard the pot go on.

And one evening she took a box out from under the shelf by the stove.

It was wooden and about the size of two hands and it had a lid that fitted. She
took it out, and sat with it on her knees in the chair by the fire for perhaps a
quarter of an hour, and did not open it, and put it back.

He did not ask. She did not offer. He thought about it for the rest of his life and
never knew, and that was correct, and he knew it was correct at the time.

---

## Sixty-Two

He said the other thing on the last evening, on the step, which was where she had
refused to let him say it nine years earlier.

— You would not let me say it, he said. — On the step. The morning I went.

— I remember.

— I have carried it since.

— I know you have, said Grieve. — That is why I would not let you. It would have
come out wrong and you would have carried the wrong version, and I would rather you
carried the true one for nine years than the wrong one for one.

— That is a very long game.

— I am sixty years old and I live in a hole, she said. — Long games are what I
have got.

He laughed. Then he sat with it for a while, and she let him, and the light went
orange the way it did an hour before it went.

— It was not thank you, he said. — That is what I have worked out. I had thank you
ready and it was not that.

— No.

— It was: I am sorry that I am going.

He looked at his hands.

— Not sorry that I went. I was right to go, and you were right that I had to have
my own reason, and I found one eventually and it took about four years. Sorry that
it was going to cost you and that I was going to do it anyway. Both of those at
once. I did not have anywhere to put both of those at once and I have spent nine
years finding out that you can just — hold them.

Grieve was quiet.

— Yes, she said. — All right. That is worth nine years.

And then, after a while:

— It did cost.

— I know.

— I am telling you because you have got old enough to hear it without turning it
into a reason to stay. It cost. It cost for about two years, badly, and then it
was just a thing that had happened.

— Yes.

— And I would not have had you stay, she said. — Both of those. You can hold
them.

---

## Sixty-Three

He went on a clear morning.

She packed him two days of food, the same cloth, the same three things, and
neither of them said anything about that either, and this time he laughed while
she was doing it and she said — what — and he said — nothing — and she said —
liar — and went on packing.

— The seam under the fourth rise, she said.

— I know.

— And the burnt scrub —

— On the left, going out. I know. I found it on the right coming in and I sat down
on my pack.

Grieve stopped what she was doing.

— Did you.

— For a minute.

— Huh, she said.

She put the bundle in his hands.

— Do not drink the flask on the first day, no matter what you tell yourself about
the second.

— I know.

— I know you know. I am saying it.

He got the bundle into the pack. Aldo was asleep in his chair in the sun with the
blanket over his knees and Steadfast had said goodbye to him already, twice,
because he would not remember the first one and possibly not the second.

At the gate Grieve said: — Are you coming back?

He thought about it honestly, which she deserved.

— I do not know, he said. — It is a hundred and four days and I am fifty-nine.

— That is not a no.

— It is not a no.

— Good, said Grieve. — Then I will not tidy the lean-to.

He went out through the gate and up onto the fold and at the top of it he turned
round, which he had not done last time.

She was standing in the gate.

Not in the rows. Not with her back to him. Standing in the gate with her hand up
against the post, watching him go, and when she saw him turn she lifted the hand
off the post and held it up, and kept it there.

He put his hand up.

Then he went down the far side and the smoke went out of sight behind him about a
quarter of an hour later, and he did not stop to look for it, because he knew
exactly where it was, and because there was a hut with a brazier on it four days
east, and a man and two women at a seam forty miles north of that, and none of
them knew about each other yet, and he was the only person alive who had been in
all three places.
