### PART ONE

## One

The road was gone in the morning.

Not blocked. Steadfast had seen the road blocked, twice, and both times there had
been something to look at — a slide of rock, a fallen tree, a place where a river
had decided differently. This was not that. He came out of the lodging with his
pack on and his boots laced and looked at the place where the road had been for
the nineteen years he had walked it, and there was grass there. Old grass. Grass
that had been growing a long time.

He stood in the doorway.

Then he walked out to where it should have been and put his boot on the ground and
pressed, as though the road were under the turf and could be found by pushing. He
went twenty paces north and tried again. He went forty paces south. He got down
and put his hand flat on the grass and felt nothing but grass, and cold, and the
small movement of something living underneath.

The keeper of the lodging came out and stood with him and said nothing.

— It was here, Steadfast said.

— I know it was.

— I walked it in.

— I know you did. I watched you come.

— Then where is it.

The keeper looked out at the grass with the expression of a man reading something
in a language he only half has. — I have seen this four times, he said. Not here.
Elsewhere. It is not the road that moved.

Steadfast waited.

— It is the country, the keeper said. The country has turned underneath it. There
is no walking back to a thing that is still there.

---

## Two

He had been Steadfast for seven of the nineteen years and had been given it, which
is the only way anybody gets one.

Before that he had been called something ordinary that he had got from his mother
and had never thought about. What happened was that there was a winter with a lot
of weather in it and a stretch of the road above the ford went bad, and he had
walked it anyway, four times, in both directions, carrying for people who could
not, and at the end of it a woman at the staging house had said it in front of
about nine people and it had stuck within a season.

He had been forty-one. He remembered being pleased in a way he was slightly
ashamed of and going out afterwards and standing in the cold with his ears hot.

And then it had been his, and he had lived inside it, and it had turned out to be
a good name to live inside because it told you what to do. When you did not know
what to do you did the steady thing, and everybody knew that was what you would
do, and after a few years you did not have to decide.

He was very good at the road.

That is not nothing and he would like it said plainly, because everything that
came afterwards has a way of making it sound like it was not. He knew the water on
every stretch between the ford and the high crossing. He knew which of the
lodging-keepers watered the beer and which of them would sit up with a sick man.
He knew the six places where people came off it in the first fortnight and why,
and he could tell in about an hour of walking with someone whether they were going
to last, and he was right about it more often than anybody he ever met.

Nineteen years. He could have said what he was for in a sentence, and did, out
loud, to people, without any embarrassment at all.

---

## Three

He spent nine days looking for it.

That is the part he does not enjoy remembering. He went north along where it had
been, and then south, and then he did a thing he was ashamed of afterwards and
went out on a bearing at right angles for most of a day on the theory that it might
have been *displaced*, that it might be lying two miles east like a rope kicked off
a path, and he stood at the end of that day in a field with cows in it.

He came back to the lodging every night because he did not know what else to do,
and the keeper fed him and did not say anything about it, which Steadfast noticed
at the time and understood the size of much later.

On the fourth day he found the milestone.

It was where it should have been, on its side in the grass, half sunk, with the
figures still legible and moss in them. He got down and cleared it with his hands
and read it and it said what it had always said, which was a number and a place,
and both of them were still true and neither of them was any use, because there was
no road for them to be measured along.

He sat next to it for about an hour.

That was the first time he understood that the trouble was not that something had
been taken away. Everything was still there. The stone was there and the ford was
there and the high crossing was there and the distances between them had not
changed by a yard. What had gone was the thing that had joined them.

---

## Four

There were about thirty people at the lodging within the fortnight and that was
when he made his mistake.

They came in off the country, in ones and twos, the way people do. Some of them
had been a day out and some of them a week. They came in with the same face on
them, all of them, and they came to him — because of the name, because he was the
one who had walked it nineteen years, because a man in a bad hour will look round
a room for somebody who has done the thing longest.

And he told them it would come back.

He did not say it exactly like that. He was too careful for that and had been
careful for nineteen years. What he said was that in his experience the road went
bad in patches and came good, and that he had walked the stretch above the ford
through a winter when everybody said it was finished, and that the sensible thing
was to stay put and keep together and not go off in weather on somebody's guess.

Every word of that was true and every word of it was about a different thing, and
he knew that within about ten days and went on saying it for six weeks.

He has thought about why, and the answer is not complicated and does not improve
with looking at it. He said it because they were frightened and it steadied them.
He said it because saying it steadied him. And he said it because he was the man
who knew, and there is no way to stand in a room full of people who need you to
know and say *I have no idea* that does not cost you the only thing you have ever
been.

---

## Five

They went anyway, over the winter, in the way that a thing empties rather than
ends.

Two of them went south in the first month and were sensible about it and said so
and asked him to come. Four went to look for the high crossing. A family went back
east to a place they had come from. One man, who was not well, went out on his own
in a hard week in the second month and did not come back and was not found, and
there is nothing to say about that except that Steadfast had told him the day
before to sit tight and wait.

By the thaw there were nine of them left and none of the nine believed him.

Nobody was cruel about it. That was somehow the worst part — there was no scene,
nobody stood up and said *you have been telling us this for four months and you do
not know*, and he sometimes wished afterwards that somebody had, because it would
have been quicker. What happened instead was that they stopped asking him things.
He noticed it over about three weeks. He would come into the room and a
conversation would go on rather than stopping and turning to him, and it took him
some time to work out that this was not rudeness.

He was not the man who knew. He had not been for months. They had all understood
it before he had, and had gone on being kind to him while he caught up.

---

## Six

The map came in the spring, with a man who had six of them.

He was pleasant and unhurried and did not oversell it. He spread one on the table
at the lodging and it was beautiful — inked in three colours on good hide, with a
scale, and a compass rose in the corner drawn by somebody who could draw, and the
road on it as a double line running north and south, confident, unbroken, with the
lodging-houses along it as little squares.

Steadfast looked for a long time at the square with his own lodging's name on it.

— Have you seen it, he said.

— I have people out.

— That is not the question I asked.

The man did not take offence, which was somehow worse. He said, in the same
unhurried way, that no one man could walk all of it, that this was what surveying
was, that he marked the stretches he was less sure of in a paler hand — look — and
that he would rather sell an honest map with three doubtful sections on it than no
map at all, because people were going out on nothing.

And that, Steadfast thought, was true. That was the trouble with all of it. It was
true.

He did not buy the map. Two of the nine did.

He watched them go with it a week later and stood at the door of the lodging and
did not say one word to either of them about what he had stood in on a cold
morning in the doorway behind him, because he had spent four months telling people
things that steadied them and had run out of any appetite for the sound of his own
voice being certain.

That is the whole of the reason. He would like it to have been a better one.

---

## Seven

Three people asked him to come with them and he said no to all three.

The two who went south in the first month asked him honestly and he said no
because he still believed the road would come back and thought them foolish for
going, and that was the only one of the three he does not blame himself for,
because at least he had a reason he believed at the time.

The family going east asked in a general way, at a meal, in front of the children,
and he said no in a general way, and later thought that the woman had meant it and
had asked in front of the children so that it would be easier to accept.

And the keeper asked him, at the end, in the summer, when there were four of them
left and the lodging was going to close.

— Come with us, he said. — My brother has a place at the coast. There is room and
there is work and it is a long way from any of this.

— No.

— Why not.

And Steadfast could not answer him, and stood there, and the keeper waited a long
time — longer than was comfortable, long enough that Steadfast understood
afterwards that it had been on purpose — and then said:

— All right. Then take some food.

---

## Eight

He went west because of the milestone.

That is the honest reason and it is not a good one. The figures on the stone were a
number and a place and the place was west, and it had been the last true thing he
had touched, and when the lodging closed and there was nowhere to be, he found that
his feet had an opinion.

He knew what was west. Everybody did. There was settled country for a while and
then there was not, and then there was a stretch of stone and grey scrub that had a
name in the way weather has a name, and nobody crossed it because there was nothing
on the other side worth crossing to, and the people who had gone into it were
people who were not coming back.

He walked for nine days through country where there were still farms.

He was fed twice and turned away once and slept in a barn with the owner's
knowledge and in two more without it. On the ninth day he passed a boy on a gate
who asked him where he was going and he said west and the boy said — there is
nothing west — and he said — I know — and walked on, and heard the boy call
something after him that he did not turn round for.

Then the farms stopped.

There was no border. He noticed the ground going hard under him over about two
days, and the hedges giving out, and then one morning he came up out of a dip and
there was grey scrub in front of him as far as he could see in every direction,
low, ankle-high, going nowhere.

He stood at the edge of it for most of an hour.

Then he stepped into it, and that was the whole of the ceremony.

---

## Nine

Four months.

He would come to say it in months for a long time after he should have stopped,
and there is not a great deal to say about them, which is itself the truest thing
about that country.

He learned what it had in it. Stone. Scrub that broke like old bread. Water in
seams if you knew to look at the underside of a slope, and he did not know, for
the first three weeks, and nearly died of not knowing, and then met a man who
showed him in about four minutes and would not stay to be thanked.

He tried to make fire twice. Both times he got a smell like scorched hair and a
handful of ash that blew off before it was cool, and the second time he sat and
looked at his hands for a while and then packed up and went on.

He met fourteen people in four months. Every one of them told him their account
inside a quarter of an hour, usually before he asked — where they had come off,
how long they had been out, what they had been before — and he understood that this
was what people did out there, and did it himself, and heard himself doing it, and
carried on doing it because there was nothing else to give anybody.

They asked his name a dozen times and he said it a dozen times.

That was the part he could not have explained to anybody, then or afterwards. Not
the thirst, not the ground, not the cold. That a dozen times in four months a
stranger in a grey country asked a man what he was called, and a man who had not
been steadfast about one single thing since a morning in a doorway heard himself
say the word out loud, in the ordinary way, like a fact, and got away with it.

---

## Ten

On the last day of it he smelled smoke.

He was four months in by then and had stopped expecting anything, which is a thing
that happens to a man's face before it happens to his mind, and he stopped where
he was and turned his head and could not credit it, because there was nothing in
that country that would take a flame and hold it. He had tried. He knew.

He stood for a while and it did not go away.

So he went towards it — not quickly, because he had learned by then what quickly
cost, and not with any hope in particular, because hope was not a thing he had
carried in some weeks. He went towards it the way you go towards a sound in the
night: to find out, and to stop wondering.

It took him the rest of the afternoon.

And at the end of it there was a fold in the ground he had to come up over, and he
came up over it, and stopped.

### PART TWO

## Eleven

It was a garden.

That was the thing he could not get past, standing at the edge of it with his hand
still on the strap of his pack. Beans up canes. Something low and green in rows,
four rows, with the soil between them dark and turned. A wall of stacked flat
stones about waist high running all the way round, which someone had carried there
one at a time from somewhere he could not see. And at the back of it a house, a
real one, with a roof and a chimney and a door.

A door. In the Waste. A thing that could be shut.

The wall was not finished. It ran three sides and stopped, and the fourth side was
open to the scrub with a line of stones laid out along it, spaced, waiting, like
something interrupted.

— You can come in, she said, without turning round. — You have been standing there
a while.

She was at the far end of the rows with her back to him, doing something to the
beans. He had made no sound he was aware of.

He came in through the gap in the wall because there was no gate, and the ground
under his boots changed, and that was what undid him — not the green, not the
smoke. The ground. It had been worked. It gave slightly. He had been walking on
stone for four months and had stopped noticing until it stopped.

---

She fed him.

She did not ask him anything until he had eaten, and she did not watch him eat. He
ate badly and fast and had to stop twice.
There was a stew of something with beans in it and there was bread, and the bread
was the thing that broke his composure, though he got through it without her
seeing.

Then she sat back and looked at him with her hands round a cup.

— How long have you been out?

— Four months.

— Four months, she said.

— Yes.

— You still say it in months.

She said it without any cruelty, the way you would tell a man he had cut himself
and not noticed, and Steadfast sat in her warm house with her food in him and felt
the floor go.

— What do they call you?

He had been asked that a dozen times in four months and had answered it a dozen
times without thinking, and he opened his mouth to do it again and could not get
the word out in front of her.

She waited.

— Steadfast, he said.

— Is that yours?

— It was given.

— By people who had watched you do something.

— Nineteen years of it.

She nodded, and did not smile, which he had been afraid she was going to do.

— All right, she said. — Steadfast.

She used it after that in the ordinary way, every day, for everything. Pass me
that, Steadfast. The light is going, Steadfast. He flinched at it for weeks and
she never once made anything of the flinch.

— How long have you been here, he said.

— Eleven years.

He waited for the rest of it, the account, the how and the why, because everyone
he had met in four months had given him theirs within a quarter of an hour,
usually before he had asked. She drank her tea.

— Eleven years, he said.

— Yes.

— And you have not —

— No.

She put the cup down and stood and started clearing, and he understood that the
conversation was not being refused, exactly. It was being deferred to a time when
it would be less interesting to both of them.

---

## Twelve

He meant to stay two nights.

He said so, that first evening, and she said that was fine and showed him where he
could sleep, which was a lean-to on the north side of the house with a straw
mattress and a blanket that smelled of the smoke. He lay in it and listened to
nothing — the Waste made no sound at night, no insects, no wind in anything — and
in the morning she was already out and there was porridge on the stove and a note
of the kind you leave for somebody you are not worried about.

*Water in the barrel. Do not use the second barrel.*

He used the first barrel. Then he stood in the doorway and looked at her working
in the rows and could not think of a reason to leave that day rather than the
next.

By the fourth morning she put him on the wall.

— Can you lay stone?

— No.

— Good. Neither could I.

She showed him. It was less like building than like a puzzle with no picture: you
put your hand on a stone and you did not pick it up yet, you turned it where it
lay and looked at the face it presented, and if the face was wrong you left it and
tried another, and the whole art of it was in not lifting anything until you were
sure. Lifting was the expensive part. She could tell from six feet whether a stone
would seat.

He was terrible at it. He lifted everything. By noon his back had a hot wire in it
and his section had a bulge in the middle where he had forced two stones to sit
together that did not want to.

— That will come down, she said, going past with a bucket.

— I can take it out.

— No. Leave it. It will come down on its own or it will not, and either way you
will learn more from watching it than from me telling you.

It did not come down. He watched it for days, expecting it, and it stood, ugly,
out of line with everything on either side of it, a bulge in an otherwise honest
wall.

---

The water was the thing he could not work out.

There was no well. He looked. There was no spring, no seam in the rock anywhere
within an hour of the house, and he walked the ground for an hour in every
direction on the sixth day, partly to see and partly because he had begun to want
a reason to be useful and had not found one.

He came back and she was sitting on the step.

— There is no water here, he said.

— No.

— Then where.

She got up and took him round the back of the house, and it was so simple he was
angry about it for an hour afterwards. The roof was pitched to a gutter of split
wood. The gutter ran to the barrel. Beside the barrel was the second barrel, the
one he was not to use, and beside that a run of flat stones set into the ground
sloping to a sunk basin lined with clay.

— It rains eleven days a year here, she said. — Sometimes fourteen.

— Eleven days.

— You have to be very good at eleven days.

He looked at the gutter, which was old and had been repaired in four places, each
repair a different wood.

— And the second barrel.

— Is for when I have got the first one wrong.

---

## Thirteen

He asked about the road on the ninth day. He had been building up to it and made a
poor job of the approach.

— When you came out. Did yours go the same way, or —

— Steadfast.

— I only mean, if it was the same, then someone must —

— Steadfast.

She was kneeling in the second row with her hands in the dirt and she did not
stop what she was doing.

— I am not going to talk about the road.

— Why not.

— Because you want me to help you find it, and I cannot, and after we have talked
about it for a week you will be angry with me for not being able to. I would
rather skip that part. I have done it before and it always goes that way.

She pulled something and shook the soil off it and set it in the basket.

— How many times, he said.

— You are the ninth.

That stopped him. He had been thinking of himself as a weather event.

— What happened to the other eight.

— Six went on. Two went back. One of the two who went back came through again
about a year later going the other way and did not stop, and I have thought about
that one more than the other eight together.

— Why.

— Because he saw the smoke, she said. — And chose not to come and stand at my
wall. And I have never worked out whether that was strength or the other thing,
and I would like to know, and I never will.

She sat back on her heels then and looked at him properly for the first time
since the meal.

— That is the last question about the road you get for a while. Have it back if
you want to spend it differently.

— No, he said. — That was a good one.

— It was, she said, and went back to the row.

---

## Fourteen

The visitor came in the third week.

Steadfast saw him first, from the wall, and did not recognise what he was looking
at for a moment because the man was moving so slowly. He came across the scrub
from the north in a diagonal, stopping often, not the stop of a man resting but
the stop of a man who has forgotten mid-stride what he is doing and has to wait
for it to come back.

Grieve was beside him before he had said anything.

— Fill the pot, she said. — Not the good bread. The other one.

— Do you know him?

— No.

She went out to meet him past the line of laid stones, and Steadfast watched her
do a thing he thought about for a long time afterwards. She did not help him. She
walked out and stopped six feet short of him and stood, and said something he
could not hear, and waited. The man said something. She waited again. It went on
like that for a while, the two of them standing in the open with a gap between
them that she did not close, and then the man came forward on his own feet and she
turned and walked in beside him without touching him.

The man ate. He did not talk much and what he said did not join up. He had been a
builder, or his father had. He had a daughter. There was a thing about a horse
that started twice and did not finish either time. Grieve asked him nothing except
whether he wanted more, four times, and each time he did.

He slept in the lean-to and Steadfast slept on the floor by the stove and did not
mind.

In the morning the man was gone. He had taken nothing, which Steadfast checked,
and was ashamed of checking.

— Will he be all right?

— No, she said.

— Then why did you not —

— Say it.

— Make him stay.

Grieve was scraping the pot out and she stopped and held it against her hip.

— Because he did not ask, she said. — And because the last time I made someone
stay who had not asked, he stayed four years and it was not a kindness, it was me
being unable to watch someone walk off into it. He hated me by the end and he was
right to. Now I feed them and I let them go and I am wrong about half of them and
I do not know which half.

She went back to the pot.

— That is the honest answer. There is a nicer one and I could give it to you but
you would use it later on someone who deserves the honest one.

---

## Fifteen

The rain came at the end of the fifth week.

She was up before it, in the dark, and he woke to her moving and came out and
found her on the roof with a rag in each hand doing something to the gutter joints
by feel. There were no clouds he could see. The sky was the same nothing it always
was.

— How do you know.

— My hip. And the beans go quiet. Get the second barrel round.

It rained for nine hours. He had not seen rain in four months and had forgotten
that it made noise, and the noise on the roof was so loud they had to raise their
voices in the house. The gutter took it. The first barrel filled in two hours and
the run-off went down the flat stones to the basin and the basin filled and the
overflow from that went out along a channel she had cut into the rows so the water
walked itself down between the beans.

She stood in the doorway with her arms folded, wet to the shoulder, and watched
the channel work, and she was so plainly happy that he had to look away.

Around the fourth hour a joint went on the north side and the water started coming
off the roof in a sheet instead of into the gutter, and she was up the ladder
before he had the door open. He went up after her. It was two of them on a wet
roof with a bad joint and no light, and she showed him where to put his hands and
he put them there and they held it, physically held the wood together, for the
better part of an hour until the rain slackened enough for her to get a wedge in.

They came down black with wet. She was laughing. It was the first time he had
heard it.

— That is eleven days, she said. — That was one of them.

Then she looked at the wall, at the ugly bulged section he had built, running with
water, standing.

— Huh, she said.

That was all. She went in to get dry.

---

## Sixteen

He stopped counting in months somewhere in the second season and did not notice
until she said something.

They were sat on the step at the end of a day. The light in the Waste went orange
about an hour before it went, and it was the only time the country was anything
other than grey, and they had got into the habit of being outside for it without
having discussed it.

— You have been here a while now, she said.

— Since the rain.

— No. Before that.

— Since a while before that, he said.

She smiled at the middle distance.

— There, she said.

— There what.

— Nothing. You did it right.

He worked it out about a minute later and sat with it. He did not feel that he had
arrived anywhere. He felt as though something had been taken from him quietly
while he was looking at the beans.

— Is that good, he said.

— It is neither.

— That is not an answer.

— No, she said. — It is not.

And then, because he had learned that she gave you the real thing if you let a
silence run rather than filling it, he let it run.

— It happens, she said eventually. — To everyone who stays past the first winter.

— That is not an answer either.

— No.

The light went. She got up and knocked the dirt off the back of her skirt.

— The wall, she said. — Tomorrow. I want the fourth side up before the cold, and
you are quicker now, and you still lift everything.

— I do not lift everything.

— You lift everything.

She went in. He sat a minute longer in the dark and found that he was thinking
about the fourth side of the wall and what stones were near it and how many days
it would take them, and that he had not thought about the road since some morning
he could not now identify.

He noticed that.

## Seventeen

The cold came, and the garden went to almost nothing, and he learned that this was
normal and that she had been feeding him out of a store he had not known existed.

It was under the floor. She lifted three boards by the stove and there was a pit
lined with flat stones, and in it beans dried on the cane, and roots in sand, and
a hard grey cheese she had made from nothing he could identify and would not
discuss.

— How long does that last.

— Until it does not.

— That is not —

— It lasts to the middle of the cold if I am the only one, she said, and put the
boards back down.

He stood there while she swept the dust back over the joins with her foot.

— How long with two.

— Steadfast.

— How long with two.

— We will find out, she said. — Which is not the same as short. Go and get the
grey wood in before it gets damp.

He went and got the grey wood in. He was angry for two days in a way he could not
have justified to anybody, least of all himself, because she had never once
suggested he go, and because he had eaten from that pit every day for four months
without asking a single question about where any of it came from.

---

They worked the fourth side through the cold.

It went slowly, because the stone had to come from further out each week and
because the ground was hard and the days were short. He got better. He stopped
lifting everything. Somewhere in there he found that he could stand over a stone
with his hands at his sides and know, the way you notice you have learned a road.

She noticed before he did.

— You did not lift that.

— No.

— Hm, she said, and went back to her own end.

He was absurdly pleased for the rest of the day and told himself he was not.

---

## Eighteen

The woman came in the middle of the cold and was the easiest guest either of them
ever had.

That was the first thing about her and it went on being the thing. She came over
the fold at an ordinary walk in the middle of the morning, and stopped at the line
of laid stones where the fourth side was going to be, and looked at the garden, and
said: — May I come in through the wall or do I go round.

Grieve said: — There is no round.

Her name was Maud. She was perhaps forty-five. She ate what was put in front of
her and no more, and made no performance of any of it, and asked after the beans
like a woman asking after somebody's children.

She stayed two days.

She noticed the gutter on the first afternoon and stood under it with her head
back, working out the pitch of it, and asked who had done the repairs, and said
the third one was clever. She was out in the rows before Steadfast on the second
morning, working, and she worked fast and low and did not need telling anything.
She had done it before, she said. At home. A long time ago. She did not say where
home was and neither of them asked, because that was the house they kept.

On the second evening she took the bucket apart and mended the strap, which had
been going for a month and which Steadfast had been meaning to do. It was a proper
job. She sewed it back with a curved needle out of her own pack, and made the seam
sit flat, and put a patch under it where the wear was going to come next.

She told a story about a dog. Grieve laughed out loud, which Steadfast had heard
twice in eight months and had both times had to look at the fire.

---

She asked one thing, late, when the pot was off and the three of them were sitting.

— How far is it to anywhere from here.

Grieve answered her the way she answered everything, which was fully and without
decorating it. Nine days to a line of pale stone if you were sensible and routed by
water. Past the pale stone she did not know, because she had never been. East was
nothing and stayed nothing for as long as anybody had ever gone into it. South was
where most people came from and there was no reason to think it had improved.

Maud listened to all of it with her chin on her hand.

— Right, she said.

And that was the whole of it, and they went to bed, and Steadfast went over that
conversation many times afterwards and could not find anything in it.

---

In the morning she was packed and standing in the gap.

Grieve gave her the bundle. Bread, the hard cheese, a stoppered flask, and the
thing about not drinking the flask on the first day, and Maud said — I know, and
Grieve said — I know you know, and they both did the small laugh people do.

— Go well.

— Thank you for the two days.

She went out the way she had come, north-west, up over the fold, and she did not
turn round, and Steadfast went back to the wall.

---

He found the knife at the middle of the morning, clearing the table.

It was under the edge of the cloth. It was a good knife, better than anything in
the house — a working blade with a horn handle worn down to the shape of a hand
that was not his and not Grieve's — and he picked it up and turned it over and had
got as far as the door with it before the second thought arrived, which was the
needle-case, which was also there, on the shelf, where she had put it down after
the bucket.

He came back in with the knife in his hand.

— She has left her knife.

Grieve was at the stove.

She turned round, and she looked at the knife, and she did not say anything at
all, and the not saying anything went on a beat too long — one beat, no more — and
then she was sitting on the floor by the door pulling her boots on.

— She will be two hours off, said Steadfast. — I will go. I am quicker —

— Stay here and keep the fire up.

— Grieve —

— Keep the fire up, she said. — Put the green stuff on it. I want it seen.

Then she was out through the gap in the fourth side and going up the fold at a
pace he had not known she had.

---

He kept the fire up.

He built it stupidly big and put green stuff on it in armfuls, wet scrub and the
tops off the bean canes, and stood back and watched the column go up straight
because there was no wind, and then went and got more, and burned in one afternoon
what they would ordinarily have burned in nine days. He knew what he was doing to
the store of wood. He did it anyway. Twice he went up onto the fold and looked
north-west at nothing, and came back down, and put more on.

Grieve came in at dark.

She came in alone, and shut the door, and stood with her back against it for a
moment before she got her boots off.

— Well?

— She had two hours on me, Grieve said, — and she is faster than she looks.

She ate standing at the stove and did not sit down. She did not say anything else
about it that night, or in the week after it, or ever.

---

He asked her one question, at the table, a day later, and she answered it.

— How did you know.

— I did not know.

— You knew when I picked the knife up.

— I knew when you picked the knife up, she said. — That is not knowing. That is
being told.

He turned his cup round.

— Two days, he said. — She was here two days and she mended the bucket.

— Yes.

— And you did not —

— No, said Grieve.

That was the end of it. She got up and went out to look at the fourth side and
what he had done to the wood pile, and did not say anything about that either.

The knife stayed. She used it, every day, for everything, in the ordinary way,
because it was the best knife in the house and she was not a woman who would have
a good knife in a drawer. Steadfast could not look at it for about a month. Then
he stopped noticing it, which was worse, and he noticed that he had stopped
noticing it, which was worse again.

---

## Nineteen

The fourth side was two stones from finished on a grey afternoon when he
understood what finishing it meant.

He was carrying, and she was seating, and there was a gap the width of a forearm
left in the line, and he set the stone down beside it rather than handing it over
and stood up and looked at the whole thing. Four sides. A closed shape on the
ground with a house in it.

— What happens after this.

She did not look up. — After what.

— After the wall.

— The gutter needs doing before the rain. And the second row is finished, so that
comes out and goes over.

— No, he said. — After.

She sat back on her heels and wiped her wrist across her forehead and left dirt
there, and looked at the gap.

— There is always something.

— That is not what I asked.

— I know what you asked, she said.

She got up. She did not take the stone from him. She went and got the bucket and
started away toward the house with it and then stopped about six feet off, which
he noticed, because it was the distance she had stood from the man in the autumn.

— You have been asking me that since the first week, she said. — You have just
been asking it about the road.

Then she went in, and he stood in the gap in his own wall with a stone in his
hands and could not work out whether he had been understood or dismissed, and
found that he wanted very badly for it to be dismissed, because the other thing
was worse.

He did not put the stone in. He left the gap.

She did not mention it. It stayed open through the cold, one forearm's width, and
they both walked past it every day and neither of them said anything about it at
all, and that was the first thing they had that was not spoken about.

---

## Twenty

The girl came in the thaw.

She was young, which was the first thing, and she was not far gone, which was the
second. She came up out of the fold at a walk, not a stagger. She had a pack that
was still packed. She had been out twelve days.

— Twelve days, Grieve said. — You are quick.

— I ran the first three.

— Everyone does.

She ate like someone who had not yet learned to be ashamed of eating, and she
talked, and what she said joined up. Her name was Ash. She had been on the road
six years. She was going to cross the Waste and get to the other side of it,
where she believed there was something, and she asked them both, straight out,
across the table, whether they knew what was over there.

Grieve said no.

Steadfast said no, and heard how his own voice did it — flat, quick, closing —
and did not like the sound.

Ash slept in the lean-to. Steadfast lay by the stove and listened to the nothing
and did not sleep much.

---

In the morning she was up before either of them, packed, standing out by the gap
in the fourth side with her boots on.

— You are going, Grieve said, from the door.

— Yes.

— Which way.

— That way, said Ash, and pointed at the flat grey nothing to the east, and she
said it the way you would name a street.

Grieve nodded and went and got a cloth bundle from inside and brought it out and
put it in the girl's hands. Bread and the hard cheese and a stoppered flask.

— Two days of it, she said. — Do not drink the flask on the first day no matter
what you tell yourself about the second.

— Thank you.

— Go well.

And she stepped back.

Steadfast said: — Wait.

Both of them looked at him.

He had not known he was going to say it. He heard it come out and heard it land
and there was a silence of the kind that has weight, and Grieve did not move at
all, which was her giving him the chance to make it a smaller sentence.

He did not take it.

— It is fifteen days across at the narrowest and you are not carrying fifteen.
There is nothing east. I came from the east. There is nothing there but more of
it, and you will be four days out with an empty flask and no shade and no —

— Steadfast, Grieve said.

— She is nineteen.

— Twenty-two, said Ash.

— You are nineteen or you are twenty-two and either way you are going to die out
there, he said, and his voice had gone up and he could not get it down. — Stay
until the rains. Stay six weeks. Six weeks and you go with full water and you go
knowing where the ground rises, and we can tell you where the ground rises,
because she knows and she will tell you if you ask —

— Steadfast.

— Ask her, he said to the girl. — Ask her what is out there. She knows and she
will not say it unless you ask, that is how she does it, she will stand six feet
off and let you walk into it —

He stopped.

Nobody said anything for a while. A long while. The girl looked at the ground and
then at Grieve, and something passed between the two of them that he was not part
of and did not have the standing to be part of, and then Ash picked her pack up
off the stones.

— I would like the rise, she said to Grieve. — If you know it.

Grieve told her. It took about a minute. Third fold, keep the burnt scrub on your
left, do not follow the flat because the flat is longer, there is a seam under the
lip of the fourth rise that will give you a cup if you are patient with it.

Ash listened, and repeated the parts back, and went.

They watched her out to the top of the fold and over. She did not turn round.

---

## Twenty-One

He waited for it all day and it did not come.

She did the beans. She did the gutter joint that had been on her list since the
cold. She asked him at midday to bring the long-handled thing from the lean-to and
he brought it, and she said thank you, and that was the whole of it.

By evening he could not stand it.

— Say it.

— Say what.

— That I had no right.

She was scraping the pot. She put it down.

— You had no right, she said. — There. Does that help?

— No.

— No, she said. — It never does.

She sat down across from him and he saw that she was tired in a way she had not
been that morning, and understood that she had been carrying it all day too, and
had simply been carrying it without making him watch.

— She was going to ask me anyway, she said. — She had it in her hand. She was
going to ask me at the gap and I was going to tell her, and she would have gone
with it and it would have been hers.

— And now.

— And now she has it because a man shouted. And she took it anyway, which was
well done of her, because most of them would not have.

— That is the difference? That is the whole difference? Who says it first?

— Yes, Grieve said.

— That is a very fine distinction to hang somebody's life on.

— Yes, she said. — It is.

And she let that sit, and did not decorate it, and he wanted her to and she would
not.

— I have thought about very little else for eleven years, she said. — I have got
it wrong in both directions. I have watched two people walk east who I could have
stopped by putting my hand out, and I did not put my hand out, and I do not know
what happened to either of them and I never will. And I made one man stay four
years, and I know exactly what happened to him, because I had to watch it every
day at this table.

She turned the cup round.

— The ones I let go, I have to imagine. The one I kept, I do not have to imagine.
That is the only reason I do it this way. Not because it is right. Because I have
found out which of the two kinds of not-knowing I can carry.

Steadfast sat with that.

— That is not a principle, he said at last.

— No.

— You said it like a principle. In the autumn. You said it to me like it was a
principle and you had worked it out.

— I know.

— Why.

— Because you had been here three weeks, she said, — and you wanted there to be
one.

---

## Twenty-Two

They did not talk much for a while after that.

It was not a rupture. That was almost the worst of it — there was no scene to
have, nothing to apologise for that had not already been said plainly on the same
evening. They worked. They ate. She asked him to pass things and he passed them.
Some evenings they sat out for the orange hour the way they had, and it was fine,
and it was not the same, and he could not have said what the difference was except
that he was now aware of being a person who was in her house.

He thought about Ash constantly and did not mention her once.

He caught himself counting. Fourteen days. Nineteen days. Twenty-six. He did not
know what number would mean she was across or what number would mean the other
thing, and there was nobody to ask, because the only person who would know was the
person he was not going to ask.

On the thirty-first day he came in from the wall and Grieve was sitting at the
table doing nothing, which she never did.

— Thirty-one, she said.

He stood in the door.

— You have been counting.

— Since the morning she went.

He came in and sat down. Neither of them said anything about the number or about
what it meant or about whether there was a number that meant anything at all.

After a while she got up and put the pot on.

---

## Twenty-Three

He put the stone in on a still day at the end of the thaw.

He had not planned to. He was moving the last of the far pile and it was in his
hands and he was standing at the gap, and there was no reason not to and no ceremony
about it, and he turned it twice and found the face and set it in and it seated
first time without a sound.

Then he stood back and looked at the wall.

It went all the way round. Four sides, waist high, closed, with the house inside
it and the rows inside it and the barrels at the back, and his own section bulging
out of the north run like a swallowed thing, ugly, holding.

He waited to feel the thing he had expected to feel.

What came instead was so plain that he had to sit down on the wall he had just
finished. There was nothing left to build. There was the gutter, and the rows, and
the store under the floor, and the eleven days, and all of it would go round again
next year and the year after, and it was good, all of it was good, and it was
going to be the same.

He looked at the flat grey nothing to the east where the girl had gone.

Grieve came out with the basket on her hip and saw him sitting on the wall and
saw the wall, and stopped.

She did not say well done. She did not say anything for long enough that he looked
up at her.

— Right, she said.

— Right.

— That is that, then.

And she went past him to the rows and started work, and did not look at the fourth
side again that day, and he sat on the closed wall in the middle of the afternoon
and did not move for a long time.

## Twenty-Four

The second year was easier, and that was the problem.

He knew the work now. He knew that the beans went in when the ground took a thumb
to the second knuckle and not before, and that the third row drained badly and
wanted the shallow end, and that the grey wood had to come in a fortnight earlier
than seemed necessary. He stopped being told things. Some time around the middle
of it he noticed that she had stopped watching him work, which was the highest
thing she had ever said to him and she had not said it.

The rains came late.

He knew they were coming before she did, or at the same time — he could not tell,
because when he came out that morning to say so she was already on the roof.

— The joints, he said.

— The north one.

— It will be the north one again.

— It is always the north one.

They did the north joint. Then it rained for nine hours and the gutter took it and
the barrel filled in two hours and the run went down the flat stones to the basin
and the basin filled and the overflow walked itself down between the rows, and
nothing went wrong, and there was nothing for either of them to hold together on a
wet roof in the dark.

They stood in the doorway and watched it work.

— Good, she said.

— Yes.

And it was good. That was the whole of it. It was good and it had been good last
year and it would be good next year, and he stood there in the doorway of a house
that was not his, dry, fed, competent, watching a system he now understood do
exactly what he had known it would do.

He did not say much for the rest of that week, and she did not ask.

---

## Twenty-Five

He found himself keeping a list.

Not on paper. In his head, in the evenings, and it was a list of reasons to be
there, and he did not notice he was keeping it until he noticed he was checking
it.

The wall was done. That one had gone in the spring.

The store had been dug out and relined the summer before; that one had gone too.

He had cut and hung a proper door for the lean-to, which was the last thing on it
of any size, and he had done that in four days at the end of the cold, and it hung
well, and every time he went through it now he thought: that is done as well.

By the end of the second year the list was: she is here.

He turned that over on the step one evening and found that it did not frighten him
the way he had expected. What
frightened him was the sentence underneath it, which he got to about a week later
and which was: *and I have stopped asking whether that is a reason.*

He had walked the King's Road nineteen years. He had known what he was for on
every single one of those days, and had been able to say it in a sentence, and had
said it to people, out loud, without embarrassment.

He could not now say what he was for. He could say what he did. He could say it in
enormous detail — he could tell you the drainage of the third row and the fortnight
of the grey wood and the exact face of every stone in eighty feet of wall — and
none of it added up to an answer, and the not-adding-up had stopped bothering him
some time in the previous year without his permission.

That was the thing that frightened him. Not that the road had gone. That he had
stopped looking at the place where it had been.

---

## Twenty-Six

Corr came in the second summer and was the worst of them.

He came over the fold in the afternoon at a good pace for a man of sixty, and
stood in the gap that was no longer a gap, and looked at the beans and the rows and
the house and the smoke going up out of the chimney, and said:

— Well. Somebody has done all right for themselves.

He was not half dead. He was not anything. He had food in his pack and a fat on
him that nobody four months out of the south has, and he came in and sat down at
the table without being asked twice and ate a great deal and said the cheese was
strong.

He asked how much was in the barrel. He did not like the answer.

— And you have not dug.

— There is no water under this ground, said Steadfast.

— Men say that about most ground.

He asked what they did about the cold. He asked whether the beans were worth the
work, given the water. He looked along the north run of the wall from where he was
sitting, and found the bulge inside a minute, which nobody else ever had, and said
— Who did that one, and Grieve said — He did, and Corr laughed for a while.

Steadfast washed the pot with his back to the room and found that he was in a
state he had not been in for two years and did not have anything to do with it.

---

Then Corr said, with his mouth full: — How did you end up out here, then.

And Grieve told him.

— I came out of the south with my sister. She died on the sixth day. I sat down
where I was and did not get up for a while, and while I was sitting there it
rained, and I was still there when it rained again.

Corr chewed.

— And you never went on.

— No.

— Seems a waste, he said. — Is there more of that bread.

There was more of the bread.

---

He stayed four days, which was two more than anybody had, and he was work the
whole time. He did not lift anything. He watched Steadfast lift things and told
him about a better way that he had seen once. He wanted his water at the times he
wanted it. On the third evening he explained the road to them both — where it had
gone and why and whose fault it had been — for the better part of an hour, and it
was all nonsense, and Grieve listened to the whole of it and asked him two
questions that let him go on longer.

On the fifth morning he went.

He got to the wall and turned round with his pack on and looked back at the roof,
and said: — Your north gutter joint is going. Not this year. Next.

Then he went over the wall and away south, and he was right about the joint; they
had it off within the week and the wood behind it was soft as bread.

---

Steadfast said it that night and could not stop himself.

— You should not have answered him.

— Which part.

— About your sister. He was rude to you at your own table and he was rude about
the wall and he ate nine days of the store and he called your life a waste with his
mouth full, and you sat there and told him.

— He asked, said Grieve.

She said it the way she said the beans wanted the shallow end.

Steadfast put his hands flat on the table.

— I have been here two years.

— Yes.

— Two years. And I did not know you had a sister.

Grieve put the cup down and looked at him, and he saw her decide not to make it
easier for him, and he watched her do it.

— No, she said. — Nobody has asked me.

— I would have asked.

— Yes, she said. — Probably.

She took the cups to the basin.

He sat at the table for a long time after she had gone through to the other side of
the room, and what he did with the time was go back over the two years looking for
the place where he could have asked, and what he came out with instead was that he
had asked her a great many questions in two years and that every single one of
them had been about him.

He did not ask her about the sister. Not that week, and not in the month after it,
and not on the last morning, and he was never afterwards able to give himself a
reason for that which sounded like anything except the true one.

---

He did ask her about the number, a few days later, out at the rows, because it was
easier.

— That is four since I came.

— Yes.

— Is that a lot.

— It is a wave, she said. — There was one about six years ago and then almost
nothing for four years and then this.

— Why.

— I do not know. I only know it is not steady.

She went on down the row on her knees.

— Do not make anything of it, she said. — I did, the first time.

---

## Twenty-Seven

He asked her at the end of the summer, badly, in the middle of doing something
else.

They were lifting the second row, which was finished, and the work was on hands
and knees and slow, and he said it into the dirt without looking up because he had
worked out that he would not be able to say it to her face.

— Come with me.

Grieve did not stop working.

— No.

He sat back on his heels.

— I have not said when.

— No is not about when.

She went on pulling. He watched her hands do it, which he had watched a thousand
times, and found that he was angry in the particular way you are angry with
somebody who has answered too quickly, even though he knew she had not answered
too quickly, she had answered in eleven years.

— You have not thought about it.

— Steadfast.

— One night. Think about it for one night and then say no and I will never —

— I have thought about it every time somebody has come over that fold, she said.
— Thirteen times. And the answer has been the same thirteen times and it was not a
different question when it was you.

That landed harder than she had meant it to. He saw her see it land.

— That was not — she said, and stopped, and started again. — The answer being the
same does not mean the question weighed the same. It weighed more.

She sat up and put her wrists on her knees.

— Ask me why. You have never asked me why. You have asked me about the road and
about the water and about what is east, and you have never once asked me why I
stay.

He asked her why she stayed.

— The smoke, she said.

He waited for the rest.

— That is it. That is the whole of it. There is nothing here that burns. If I go,
in a year there is no smoke over this fold, and the next one who comes out of the
south four months in and half dead walks past a mile east of here and never knows
he walked past anything.

— Someone else would —

— No, she said. — In eleven years, nobody else has. I have been the only fire in
this country for eleven years and I have counted thirteen people, nine of them
before you, and if you take the thirteen off the number and think about how many
did not come close enough to see it, that is what I do instead of sleeping.

She said it flatly, without any grandeur at all, the way a woman tells you what
her job is.

He said: — Then let me stay.

And she said: — No.

That was the one that took his breath. He had expected a fight about going. He had
not prepared anything for this.

— That makes no sense. You have just told me the whole thing runs on somebody
being here. I am here. I am good at it now, you have said so, you have —

— I made a man stay once, she said.

— That is not what this is.

— No. It is the other one. He asked and I said yes. I have thought for a long time
about which of those two was worse and I have not got anywhere with it, and I am
not doing either of them again.

— That is not your decision.

— It is exactly my decision, she said. — You are asking me for a reason to stay.
That is what let-me-stay is.

— That is not what I —

— It is. And I will not give you one, because you will take it, and it will hold a
year and possibly five, and then one morning you will be standing in that doorway
watching the water go down the rows and you will work out that the reason was mine
and not yours.

She rubbed the dirt off her palms, one and then the other.

— I have seen that face. I have looked at that face across this table for four
years.

She picked up the basket.

— If you stay, stay because you have found something here that is yours. Not
because I said the smoke needs two.

Then she took the basket in, and left him on his knees in the second row with the
whole afternoon in front of him.

---

## Twenty-Eight

It could not be unasked.

That was the discovery of the following weeks, and it was worse than a quarrel
would have been, because there was nothing to repair. She was not cold to him. She
did not avoid the subject; twice she raised things adjacent to it herself, easily,
to show that it could be raised. They worked. They ate. The orange hour still
happened.

But he now knew the answer to a question he had spent two years not asking. Before,
the days had simply been the days. Now each one was a day on which he had chosen to stay, and the choosing
was in the room, and it was in the room for her too, and neither of them could put
it back.

He caught himself once, in the third week, doing the arithmetic on the store. Two
of them, the cold coming, the second year's yield down on the first because of the
late rains. He stopped himself and was ashamed, and then a day later did it again.

He knew then that he was going. He did not admit it for a month.

---

## Twenty-Nine

He told her on a morning that had nothing to recommend it.

— I am going to go.

Grieve was at the stove. She did not turn round straight away, and when she did
her face had nothing on it that he could read, and he understood that this was not
because she felt nothing but because she had had a month to decide what her face
would do.

— When.

— Before the cold. So there is time.

— Yes, she said. — There is time.

She thought.

— Not before the rains. Go after. You want to go out with the seams full behind
you, and the fourth rise will hold a cup for a week after and not longer.

— All right.

— And not the flat. Everyone goes on the flat because it is flat.

— Not the flat.

— No.

She turned back to the stove.

That was the whole conversation. He had built about nine versions of it over the
month and none of them had been that one, and he stood in the middle of the room
for a while afterwards with his hands at his sides.

---

The rains came and went. He worked the last four weeks harder than he had worked
any four weeks in the two years, and she let him, and neither of them said what
that was.

He relaid the whole north run of the wall. He did not ask. He took it down in
sections and set it properly, and it took eleven days, and when he got to his own
piece — the bulge, the two forced stones, the ugly swallowed thing in the middle
of eighty feet of honest wall — he stopped with his hands on it.

— Leave that, she said, from the rows. She had not appeared to be watching.

— It is the worst thing here.

— Leave it.

He left it.

---

## Thirty

She packed him two days of food.

He watched her do it, standing in the door of the house with his pack already on,
and she did it exactly the way she had done it for the woman in the cold and for
the girl in the thaw, the same cloth, the same three things, and neither of them
said that and both of them knew it, and he had to look at the doorframe for a
while.

Bread. The hard cheese. A stoppered flask.

— Two days of it, she said, and put it in his hands. — Do not drink the flask on
the first day, no matter what you tell yourself about the second.

— I know.

— I know you know. I am saying it.

— Yes.

She wiped her hands down her front.

— Third fold, keep the burnt scrub on your left. The seam under the lip of the
fourth rise will give you a cup if you are patient with it. After that you are past
where I know and I have nothing for you.

He knew the words. He had heard them said twice already in this yard, to a woman
who had left a knife on the table and to a girl standing where he was standing with
her pack still packed, and he held the bundle and waited for Grieve to say
something about that.

She did not.

He could not tell whether she had spared him, or whether there was simply one way
out of that country and she had only ever had the one thing to give anybody.

— Thank you, he said.

— There is a line of pale stone about nine days out, if you get that far. Cross
it and stop looking behind you, because from there the ground reads differently
and looking behind you will put you wrong.

He nodded. He could not do anything else.

They stood there. The wall went all the way round them, waist high, closed, and
the smoke went up straight because there was no wind.

— Grieve, he said.

— No, she said. — Not on the step. Whatever it is, it will come out wrong on the
step and you will carry the wrong version of it for a year. Go on.

So he went.

He got to the gap that was not a gap any more, the place in the fourth side where
the stone had gone in, and had to step over the wall like everyone else, and it
was waist high and he was not young and it was undignified, and behind him he
heard her make a small sound that might have been a laugh.

He got up onto the fold and did not turn round until the top of it.

She was already back in the rows. She was not watching him go. He had known she
would not be and had wanted to check, and having checked, he understood that she
had gone back to the rows early on purpose, so that when he looked he would see
exactly this, and be able to go.

— Steadfast, she called, without straightening up. — The flask.

— On the second day.

— Good.

And that was the last thing she said to him, and it was about water, and it was
the kindest thing anyone had said to him in two years.

He went down the far side of the fold and the smoke went out of sight behind him
about a quarter of an hour later, and he stopped once to look for it and could not
see it, and after that he did not stop again until the light went.

### PART THREE

## Thirty-One

He read the ground for nine days.

He did not admit to himself that this was what he was doing until the fourth
morning, when he caught himself squatting at the lip of the third fold going over
a patch of scree with his eyes the way you go over a page, and stood up too fast,
and was dizzy, and had to put a hand out.

There was nothing. There would not be. Eight months of weather in a country where
weather was eleven days a year, and stone that took no print, and a girl who had
been quick — she had run the first three, everyone does — so that even on the day
she passed there would have been nothing to see.

He looked anyway. At the burnt scrub he looked at the scrub. At the fourth rise he
went down on his stomach under the lip and got his cup out of the seam, twenty
minutes of it, patient, and while he waited he looked at the ground either side of
his own boots for the mark of somebody else's.

On the ninth day he came up over a swell of nothing and found the line of pale
stone.

It was not a wall and it was not a road. It was a seam of some whiter rock running
out of the north and away south further than he could see, a hand's width proud of
the ground in places and buried in others, and he stood on it with a foot either
side and understood that he had been told about this and that being told about it
was the last thing he had.

*Cross it and stop looking behind you, because from there the ground reads
differently and looking behind you will put you wrong.*

He crossed it. He did not stop looking behind him, because she had not said he
would be able to, only that it would put him wrong, and it did.

---

## Thirty-Two

He talked to her for about a fortnight. Not aloud at first. He would come to a
slope and think *she would go round that* and go round it, and be pleased, and
then catch the pleasure and find that there was nobody to be pleased at him. He
narrated the water to her. He explained his own decisions to her and she agreed
with them, which was how he knew he was making her up, because she had never
simply agreed with him about anything in two years.

Then it went aloud, and he heard himself, and stopped, and the stopping was worse
than the talking.

After that he did the work of walking and nothing else. He filled at seams. He
rationed the way she had taught him and caught himself, on the fourth day past
the pale stone, holding a flask
against the light to read the level the way she held a jar, at the same angle, with
the same tilt of the head, and stood there in the middle of a plain feeling like a
man wearing another man's coat.

He did not stop doing it. He could not think of a better way to hold a flask.

---

The ground did read differently.

He could not have said when it started. There was no morning where he stood in one
country and looked back at another. The scrub simply began to come in patches
instead of everywhere, and then the patches began to have other things in them,
low and grey-green and
tough, and one afternoon he went down on his knees to look at something because it
was flowering, badly, four small dirty flowers on a stem, the first flower he had
seen outside four rows of a garden in two years.

He did not know its name. He picked one and it came apart in his fingers.

Two days later he crossed a watercourse. It was dry. But it had been cut by
something, and cut deep, and it ran east to west across his path with a lip on both
sides, and he stood in the bottom of it looking at the strata and thought: this
runs for part of the year.

He got out the other side and walked on and it took him until evening to work out
what was wrong with him, which was that he had been walking since noon at the pace
of a man who expects to arrive somewhere.

---

## Thirty-Three

The country went on being not quite the Waste for a long time before it was
anything else.

Nobody had told him about that, and there was no day on which anything had
improved enough to name. Water more often. Not water. Ground that took a print,
so that he could see where he had walked, which he had not been able to do for
two years and which he found he did not enjoy as much as he had expected.

---

He came on the overhang in the third week and it was the best shelter he had had
since the lean-to.

It faced south out of a low scarp, dry back to the rock, deep enough that a man
could stand up under it at the mouth and lie down at the back with his feet under
cover. There was a hearth. Not a burnt patch — a hearth, three stones set to make a
draught, and the ash under it packed down grey and hard from being used and used.

There was a sack, folded, on a ledge at head height. There was a wooden spoon in
the sack, and nothing else in it.

And there were the marks.

They were on the back wall where the rock went smooth, and they were made with
something harder than the rock, scratched deep, in fives, in rows, the way a man
counts when he is not going to be checked. The rows were regular for a long way.
Steadfast counted the first three rows carefully and then stopped counting and
looked at the size of the block of them instead, which was about the size of a
door, and did his arithmetic on that, and got a number he did not want.

Toward the bottom right the rows stopped being regular. There was a gap. Then
there were four more, unevenly spaced and set deeper than the rest, and then
nothing, and then a great deal of clean rock.

He put his hand flat on the clean rock and it was cold.

He slept there. He had known within a minute that he was going to and had spent an
hour finding reasons not to. It was the best night's sleep he had had since the
garden, and he woke before light with the fire down to nothing and lay looking at
the wall in the dark where he knew the marks were.

In the morning he swept the hearth out and set it again, three stones, and laid
what dry stuff he had not burned on the ledge beside the sack, which was a thing
he had done four hundred times at a lodging house without ever once thinking about
it.

He stood with his knife in his hand for a while in front of the four deep ones.

He put the knife away and went on.

---

He passed a thing on the tenth day out from the watercourse that he did not
understand.

It was in a wide shallow bowl of ground below him, and there were hundreds of
them — carts, wagons, some with the shafts still up and some with the wheels off
and stacked — and the whole floor of the bowl was covered in crates and boxes and
bundles set out in rows, hundreds of rows, with lanes between them. There were
people. They were moving between the rows with things in their arms, and stopping,
and setting things down, and picking other things up. Some of them were arguing. He
could hear it as a sound rather than as words, the particular rise and fall of
people who have been having the same argument for a long time.

It had been there a while. There were paths worn between the wagons and a smell of
smoke from more than one fire and washing strung on a line between two of the
larger carts.

He watched for most of an hour.

Nobody came up. Once a woman near the edge looked up in his direction and then
went back to what she was doing, the way you do when a shape on a ridge is not
your problem.

He did not go down. He could not have said why, except that he had no idea what
they were doing and did not want to be told badly, and there was some part of him
that had learned in a garden not to walk into somebody else's arrangement and
start improving it.

He went on north-west along the rim and lost sight of the bowl by evening.

---

## Thirty-Four

He was ill in the spring.

It was nothing dramatic. Bad water, probably, from a pool he had known was
doubtful and had drunk anyway because the seam before it had been dry. He was two
days on his side under an overhang with his pack for a pillow, and it went through
him, and there was one night in the middle of it where he was cold in a way that
frightened him.

Nobody came. That was the fact of it. He lay under a rock in a country with a name
he did not know and was ill and nobody came, and there was no smoke on any horizon,
and he understood — not as an idea, lying there, but in his body — what it had
meant that there had been a fire in the Waste for eleven years.

He thought: she is right. She should not come.

Then, in the same hour, because he was ill and honest: I would like her to come.

Both of those stayed with him after he got up.

---

He came out of it thin and slow and started walking again, and about a week later
he did a thing he did not plan.

He was up on high ground in the late afternoon and there was dead scrub in a
hollow, quite a lot of it, dry as paper. He had gathered wood without thinking
about it — it was a habit from a place where there was none — and it was in his
hand and there was a hollow full of tinder and he had flint.

He built a fire.

It was not cold enough to need one. He built it anyway, and got it going, and then
did the thing that made it something other than a fire: he built it up too big, and
put green stuff on it, and stood back and watched the smoke go up in a column
against a flat sky where anybody within a day's walk would see it.

Then he sat down beside it and waited to see if anyone came.

Nobody came. He kept it up until the middle of the night and slept beside the
embers and woke at first light and there was nobody, and he put it out properly,
scattering it and treading it, and stood there in the burnt patch feeling foolish
in a way he could not get rid of all morning.

He did the same thing eleven days later, in a different place, and again nobody
came, and he was less foolish about it the second time.

---

Except that somebody had come, and he found that out in the morning.

He went up onto the shoulder above the burnt patch at first light out of habit, to
look at the country and pick his line, and there was a man standing on the far
side of a shallow valley about a mile off, with the light behind him, not walking.

He was standing the way you stand when you have been standing a while.

Steadfast put his arm up.

Nothing happened. He put both arms up and swung them, which is not a thing that
can be mistaken for anything else, and the figure did not move, and he was near
enough to be sure it was a man and far enough that he could not have said his age
or what he carried.

He shouted. The distance ate most of it.

Then he went down off the shoulder into the valley, and he went fast, because the
ground was good, and he was halfway across and could see the man properly — a pack,
a stick, a hat of some kind — and the man turned and walked away over the rise
behind him at an ordinary pace.

Not running. That was the part Steadfast could not get by. A man running from
something is a man in a state you can imagine. This one walked off the skyline the
way you leave a field.

He went up the other side anyway, because he had come that far, and at the top
there was a long open slope going down with nothing on it at all, and no cover for
half a mile in any direction, and no man.

He stood there for a good while.

Then he went back down and across and got his pack from beside the burnt patch,
which took him most of the morning, and scattered the ash properly and trod it
out, and did not build another fire for nine days.

The third time somebody came.

## Thirty-Five

He came over the rise at a trot, which was wrong.

Steadfast had built the fire at midday and had been sitting beside it since,
feeding it green stuff, watching the column stand up in the still air, and he had
got as far as thinking about how many more of these he could afford in wood before
the country ran out of hollows. Then there was a man on the skyline coming fast
and downhill with his pack bouncing, and Steadfast stood up.

He was young. Twenty-five, perhaps. He arrived out of breath and put his hands on
his knees and got the words out between pulls.

— Are you all right?

Steadfast looked at him.

— What?

— The fire. Is it you? Are you hurt? Is there somebody down?

And Steadfast stood in front of his own signal with his mouth open and understood
what he had built. Not an invitation. A distress column. In a country where a man
lights a fire that size in daylight for one reason, he had been lighting them for
weeks and had thought of himself as a hearth.

— No, he said. — Nobody is hurt.

The young man straightened up. He looked at the fire, and at the green stuff
stacked ready beside it, and then at Steadfast, and something went across his face
that was not quite suspicion but was a long way from what Steadfast had been
expecting.

— Right, he said.

---

His name was Constant, which he gave without being asked, in the manner of a man
who likes his name.

He had water. That was the first thing Steadfast noticed and could not stop
noticing — two skins, both full, and he offered Steadfast a drink from one before
he had been offered anything, and Steadfast said no thank you and then wanted it
for the rest of the evening.

He had food. He had a good pack, well made, and a coat that had been mended by
somebody who could mend, and he had come nine days from a place he named as though
Steadfast would know it, and when Steadfast did not know it he described it
cheerfully and at length.

He talked. That was the other thing. He talked the whole time he was setting his
things down and the whole time the pot was on and most of the time he was eating,
and none of it was empty — it was information, it was where the water was on the
north side of the pale stone and which of the hollows had wood in them and what
the weather did here in the autumn — and Steadfast, who had spent two years
learning to sit inside a silence until the other person filled it, found that this
one would not stop being filled.

He tried Grieve's methods anyway, because they were what he had.

He fed him first and asked him nothing, and Constant filled the silence with an
account of the pale stone that went on for a quarter of an hour, and thanked him
for the food, and did not seem to notice that he had not been asked a single
question.

He waited. Constant waited too, comfortably, and then started talking again.

At one point Steadfast said nothing for long enough that it became a thing in the
air, and Constant looked at him and said: — Are you all right? You keep doing
that.

---

## Thirty-Six

In the morning Constant showed him the map.

He got it out over the last of the fire, unrolled it on a flat stone with two
pebbles to hold the corners, and it was beautiful. That was the trouble with it.
It was inked in three colours on good hide, with the folds marked, and the seams
marked, and a scale, and a compass rose in the corner drawn by somebody who could
draw.

— There, said Constant, with his finger on it. — Thirteen days from here,
fourteen if the ground is soft.

Steadfast looked where the finger was.

It was the road.

It was drawn as a double line running north and south across the top third of the
map, confident, unbroken, with the lodging-houses marked along it as little
squares, and there was one square with a name he knew because he had slept in it
for nineteen years' worth of nights.

— You are going to the road, Steadfast said.

— I am going back to it. I came off it four years ago in the bad autumn, same as
everybody, and I have been eighteen months getting a straight answer out of anyone
about where it runs now, and this — he tapped the hide — this is from a man who
had it surveyed.

— Surveyed.

— He has people out. He sells them at a fair price, and before you say it, I know
what you are going to say, I have heard it — he did not sound impatient; he
sounded like a man who had won this argument before and was fond of winning it —
everyone says the road is gone. Everyone has always said that. The people who say
it are the people who stopped looking. He did not stop looking. He went and put a
line on paper.

Steadfast looked at the beautiful map for a while.

— I walked that road nineteen years, he said.

Constant's face came up, and it was the worst thing that had happened to Steadfast
in a year, because what was in it was delight.

— Then you know it, he said. — Then you can tell me — is that the run north of the
ford, there, does it bend like that, because he has it bending and I have had two
people tell me it runs straight —

And Steadfast opened his mouth to say: it does not bend and it does not run
straight, because it is not there, I stood in the doorway of that square you have
marked and there was grass where the road had been and it was old grass, and no
line drawn by any surveyor has been within a hundred miles of the truth.

And he did not say it.

He heard himself not saying it. He stood over a young man on his knees at a map
and did the thing he had watched done: he let the silence be where the answer
went, and told himself, in the exact words, that the boy had not asked him whether
the road was there. He had asked about a bend.

— It bends, Steadfast said.

---

## Thirty-Seven

It was true. It had bent north of the ford. It had bent that way for as long as
anybody had walked it, and the surveyor had got that one thing right, and Constant
was pleased and rolled the map up and put it away and slept well.

Steadfast did not sleep.

He went over it in the dark and found that he could construct it perfectly. Grieve
would not have told him. Grieve had stood in her own yard and let a girl walk east
into nothing with a bundle and a flask because the girl had not asked her what was
out there, and when Steadfast had screamed at her about it she had not defended it
as a principle, she had said she had chosen which not-knowing she could carry.

So he had done what she did.

Except that Grieve had known what was east — she had known it as an absence, a
country with nothing in it — and she had told Ash the moment Ash asked. She had
answered the question that was put to her, fully, in about a minute, and had not
put a hand out to stop her.

He had been asked a question and had answered a different one.

He lay in the dark and got no further with it than that, and it was enough.

---

In the morning he tried.

— The map, he said, while Constant was strapping his pack. — The man who made it.
Did you meet him.

— I did. Good man. Not what you would expect, either — no fuss about him, he did
not oversell it, he said outright there were parts he was not sure of and he
marked them lighter, look — and he had the map out again, and there they were,
three stretches inked in a paler hand, honest as anything.

— And the road itself. Did he say he had seen it.

Constant paused, and for a moment Steadfast thought it had gone in.

— He said his people had.

— Not him.

— His people. That is what surveyors are, he said, and laughed, and went back to
his straps.

Steadfast tried once more, and made a mess of it.

— When you get there. If it is not — if the country has —

— If it is not there, said Constant, standing up, and he said it kindly, which was
the unbearable part. — I know. I do know. Everybody I have met in eighteen months
has managed to say some version of that to me, usually about four sentences in, and
you have lasted a whole night, which I am grateful for. If it is not there I will
have walked thirteen days and I will know, and I would rather know than sit in a
hollow with a fire wondering.

He put his hand out. Steadfast shook it.

— Thank you for the food, Constant said. — And for the bend.

---

## Thirty-Eight

He watched him go up the rise and over.

He stood by the dead fire and watched him go, and told himself the things: that the boy had water, that the boy had food, that the boy had a better
pack than his own and knew the hollows on the north side; that a man of twenty-five
who has been eighteen months getting an answer is entitled to go and get one; that
he had not lied about the bend; that Grieve would have let him go.

That last one held for about an hour.

Then it stopped holding, because he could hear how it worked — *Grieve would have
let him go* — and it was not the same shape as anything Grieve had ever said. She
had never once told him what to do by telling him what she would do. She had told
him to read the person in front of him and then had refused, at cost, to be the
reason for anything.

And the person in front of him had not been half dead and had not needed feeding
and had not needed to be left alone in a silence to arrive at his own conclusion.
He had needed one sentence from the only man in the country who had stood in that
doorway, and he had gone off carrying a beautiful map and a bend and a piece of
plain courtesy, and Steadfast had given him the courtesy because it cost the
least.

He got his pack. He did not decide to, particularly. He rolled the blanket and
kicked the burnt patch flat out of habit and was up on the rise inside a quarter
of an hour, and then he was running, badly, which at his age and after a winter of
bad water was a thing to see, and he lost the trail twice and found it because the
ground here took a print, and he was two hours catching him.

Constant heard him coming and turned round, and his hand went to his pack strap,
and the look on his face was pure alarm — a big man labouring up behind you in
empty country is not good news whoever he turns out to be — and Steadfast had to
stop and get his breath before he could do anything about it.

— It is gone, he said.

Constant stared at him.

— What?

— The road. It is not there. I did not walk off it four years ago, I came out of
the lodging one morning and it was gone, and there was grass where it had been and
the grass was old. That square on your map with the name on it. I slept in that
house nineteen years. I stood in that doorway and there was nothing.

Constant did not say anything.

— It bends north of the ford, Steadfast said. — That part is true and I told you
the true part because it was the part that cost me nothing, and I have been up all
night about it, and you can do what you like with the thirteen days but you are not
going to spend them not knowing what I know.

He ran out. There had not been much of it and he had used it all going up the
hill.

Constant looked at him for a long moment. Then he took his pack off, slowly, and
sat down on it in the middle of the open ground, and put his hands over his face,
and stayed like that.

Steadfast stood six feet away.

He stood there long enough to feel it — the distance, the rightness of it, the
whole clean architecture of leaving a man to arrive at his own grief without being
crowded — and then he crossed it and sat down on the ground next to him, close
enough that their shoulders were nearly touching, which was not something he had
ever seen anybody do.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

— Four years, Constant said, into his hands.

— I know.

— No, but four *years*.

— I know, Steadfast said.

And that was all he had, and it turned out to be the thing that was wanted, and
they sat there on the open ground in the middle of the morning with the map still
rolled up in the pack under him.

## Thirty-Nine

Constant was not quick about it and Steadfast did not try to make him be.

They stayed on that open ground the whole of the day. Around noon Constant got up
and walked off about two hundred yards and stood with his back to everything for
a while, and came back, and sat down again, and Steadfast made tea because it was
the only thing he could think of to do with his hands.

In the middle of the afternoon Constant said: — Four years is not the worst of it.

— No.

— Eighteen months is the worst of it. Four years I was just lost, everybody was
lost. Eighteen months I have been telling people. I have been telling them
confidently.

He laughed, once, badly.

— I told a woman at a well in the spring that the people who say it is gone are
the people who stopped looking. I said it to her face. She had a dead husband and
she had stopped looking and I said that to her.

Steadfast did not say *you were not to know*, which was in his mouth and would
have been for himself.

— Yes, he said instead.

Constant looked at him.

— That is not much comfort.

— No.

— Good, said Constant, and put his head back in his hands, and stayed there
until the light started to go.

---

In the evening he took the map out.

Steadfast watched him do it and had a moment of real fear, because he did not know
which way it would go and there was a version of the next ten minutes that ended
with a beautiful piece of hide in the fire and a man feeling clean for about a day
and hollow for a year.

Constant unrolled it. He looked at it for a long time. Then he put his finger on
one of the pale-inked stretches, the honest ones, the parts the surveyor had
admitted he was not sure of.

— He did do that, he said. — He did do that much.

He rolled it up and put it away.

Steadfast let out a breath he had not known he was holding, and understood he had
almost said something about it, and was glad he had not, and did not know which of
those three was the useful one.

---

## Forty

They went on together without either of them proposing it.

There was a morning where they were both packed and standing, and Constant said
— which way do you go, and Steadfast said north-west, meaning nothing by it except
that it was the way he had been going, and Constant said all right, and that was
the arrangement for the next four months.

It was nothing like the garden.

Constant talked. He never stopped talking, and Steadfast learned that he had been
wrong about what that was — it was not confidence, it was how the man kept himself
company, and he had been doing it alone for eighteen months. He talked about
weather and about his mother and about a dog he had owned and about the correct
way to strap a pack, which he taught Steadfast, and which was better than
Steadfast's way and had been for four months before Steadfast admitted it.

He was better in country than Steadfast was. That was the other thing.

He read cloud. He found water twice where Steadfast had walked past it, both
times without any triumph about it, once by watching where a bird went. He knew
plants. He had a way of walking uphill, a short-stepped patient thing, that
Steadfast copied badly and then well.

They divided the work without ever discussing it, and after the first month
Steadfast noticed that most of the practical decisions were being made by the
younger man and that he did not mind, and then noticed that he had spent nineteen
years being the person who made them.

---

He did try to give Constant something and it went wrong twice before it went
right.

The first time he tried to talk about the road, in the evening, in a way he had
rehearsed. He had a whole shape for it about how the country had turned rather
than the road moving, which was the keeper's shape, and he got about four
sentences in before he heard what he sounded like.

Constant heard it too and was kind about it.

— That is somebody else's, is it not.

— A man said it to me. The morning it went.

— It sounds like it, Constant said, not unkindly, and let it go.

The second time was worse and involved advice about grief which Steadfast will
not be quoted on.

The third time he did not try at all.

They had stopped early because of a hard rain and were sitting under an overhang
watching it come down, and Constant was restless in the way he got when he was not
walking, and there was a scatter of flat stones at the mouth of the shelter where
water had cut the bank. Steadfast picked one up, out of habit, and turned it over,
and did not lift the next one, and turned that one where it lay instead.

Constant watched him for a minute.

— What are you doing.

— Nothing. Looking at the face.

— The face.

So Steadfast showed him.

It took the rest of the afternoon and it was the first thing he had been any good
at teaching. You do not pick it up. You turn it where it lies and look at the face
it gives you, and if the face is wrong you leave it and try another, and the whole
art of it is in not lifting anything until you are sure, because lifting is the
expensive part.

Constant was terrible at it. He lifted everything. He forced two stones together
that did not want to go and made a small bad wall against the bank with a bulge in
the middle of it, and Steadfast opened his mouth to tell him to take it out and
then did not.

— That will come down, he said instead.

— I can fix it.

— Leave it. It will come down or it will not, and either way you will learn more
from watching it than from me telling you.

He heard the words come out of his own mouth in the order she had put them and had
to turn away and look at the rain for a while.

It did not come down. It was still standing when they left two days later, and
Constant looked back at it from the top of the bank with an expression Steadfast
knew from the inside.

---

## Forty-One

In the third week Constant did not get up.

Steadfast had the fire out and both packs done and was standing with his own on
one shoulder, and Constant was lying on his back in the flattened grass with his
hands behind his head, looking at the sky.

— Are you ill.

— No.

— Then what is it.

— There is water here for two days and it is a good place and I am not going to
walk today.

Steadfast stood with the pack on his shoulder.

— We have light until the evening.

— I know.

— The ground is good all the way to the ridge. We would make eight miles before
the heat came on.

— I know, said Constant, and did not move.

Steadfast waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it. There was a young
man lying in the grass with his boots off, and the sun coming up on a day with
nothing in it, and the whole flat empty country in front of them going nowhere at
all.

He put the pack down. Then he picked it up again, and put it down again, and went
for a walk.

---

He did four miles. He did not decide to do four miles; he went out to look at the
ridge, and the ridge took an hour, and there was a second ridge, and it seemed
foolish to have come that far and not see what was behind it, and there was
nothing behind it. He came back at noon with a bundle of dry stuff under his arm
and Constant had moved about ten feet to stay in the shade of the bank and was
asleep.

Steadfast sat down.

He sat for perhaps twenty minutes. Then he got up and repacked both packs,
which did not need it, and re-rolled the blankets, and got the knife out and put
an edge on it, and then went through the straps of his own pack looking for one
that was wearing, and found one that was not wearing very much, and mended it.

When he looked up Constant was awake and watching him.

— You have done all of that already.

— The strap was going.

— The strap was fine, said Constant. — I looked at it on Tuesday.

He sat up and pulled his knees in and put his chin on them and considered
Steadfast the way he considered weather.

— What happens if you stop.

— Nothing happens.

— No, said Constant. — I mean it as a question. What happens. To you. If you
stop.

And Steadfast, who had been asked a great many questions in nineteen years and had
answered all of them out of a book he kept behind his own face, discovered that
he did not have anything on the shelf for that one.

— I do not know, he said. — I have not done it.

— Not once.

— There were days I did not walk. There were four days one winter when the pass
was shut and nobody came. I mended the roof.

— That is not what I asked either, said Constant.

He lay back down.

---

It was unbearable for about an hour.

Steadfast lay on his back in the flattened grass and looked at the sky and the
sky did not do anything, and his chest was tight in a way that he had felt twice
in his life, and both of those times there had been a reason. He caught himself
listening for something. He caught himself, several times, doing sums about where
they would have been by now.

Then it stopped being unbearable, in the flat undramatic way that things stop,
and he slept in the middle of the afternoon for the first time since he was a boy,
and woke up at four with his mouth dry and his arm dead under him and a fury in
him that he could not attach to anything.

He got up and made the fire early and did not speak for an hour, and Constant let
him not speak, and afterwards, over the pot, said:

— We will do it again next month.

— No.

— We will do it again next month, said Constant, and put his hand out for the
salt.

They did it four times in four months. It was never once easy and Steadfast never
once said so.

---

## Forty-Two

Constant asked his name in the second month.

That was itself a thing — that it took two months — and it came out sideways, at a
crossing, while they were arguing about whether to ford or go up.

— You have not told me your name, Constant said. — I have been calling you *you*
for eight weeks and I have got away with it because there is nobody else here, but
if we meet anybody I am going to look like a man who does not know who he is
travelling with.

Steadfast was in the water at the time, feeling for the bottom with one boot.

He opened his mouth.

Nothing came out of it.

He had said the word perhaps four hundred times in two years, at a table, in a
yard, every day, in the ordinary way, and he had flinched every time and had never
once failed to say it. And now, standing in a river in a country with no name in
front of a young man who had asked a reasonable question, he could not get it out
of his mouth at all.

Constant waited. Then, because he was not stupid, he stopped waiting.

— All right, he said.

— It is not —

— No, said Constant. — Really. It is all right. You have not asked me a single
thing about the well or the woman since the day you told me, and I have noticed
that, and I am not going to start charging you for the food.

He came down the bank into the water.

— Up, he said. — Not across. Look at the colour of it on the far side.

They went up.

---

He was awake most of that night and could not work out what had happened to him,
because the name had not become a lie in the river. It had been a lie for two
years and he had said it fine.

What had changed was that there was somebody in front of him who would have taken
it seriously.

Grieve had known. Grieve had asked *is that yours*, on the first evening, and had
got the answer and had never mentioned it again, and had gone on using it every
day in the ordinary way because she had understood that a man who cannot say his
own name yet still has to be called something. She had been carrying it for him
the whole time. Two years of *pass me that, Steadfast* had not been her failing to
notice. It had been her holding the thing steady until he could pick it up.

He lay in the dark with that and it was the first time since he had walked out of
her gap in the wall that he had missed her without wanting anything from her.

---

## Forty-Three

Constant found the cache in the third month, in a run of broken ground under a
scarp, and he found it the way you find a thing you have been half looking at all
your life.

He stopped walking. He looked at a slope of loose stone that was exactly like
every other slope of loose stone they had crossed in a fortnight. Then he went up
it about twenty feet, put his hand flat on a slab, and said: — Ha.

— What.

— Come and look at the skyline from here.

Steadfast came up and looked at the skyline. There was a notch in it, and a
leaning rock about a mile off, and if you stood where Constant was standing the
notch sat directly over the rock, and if you moved four paces either way it did
not.

— That is how you find it again, said Constant. — You do not mark it. Marking it
is how you lose it.

He got his fingers under the slab and lifted, and it came, because it had been
laid to come.

Underneath there was a hole the size of a wash-tub, dry, lined with flat stone. In
it there was a pot sealed with cloth and fat. There was grain in a hide bag,
tied twice. There was meat, dried hard and black, in a second bag. And there was a
smaller bag, heavy for its size, and Constant lifted that one out first and untied
it and held it open and did not say anything for a moment.

Salt.

Not a pinch. Not a traveller's twist of it in a rag. Two double handfuls of clean
salt in a bag somebody had sewn for the purpose.

---

They sat on the slope either side of the open hole for a long time.

— Somebody put that there, Steadfast said.

— Yes.

— Then we put the stone back.

Constant turned the salt bag over in his hands and looked at the seam of it.

— Two winters, he said. — Look at the growth in the cracks round the slab. That
has had two winters on it and nobody has been back. Nobody leaves salt for two
winters.

— You do not know that he has not been back.

— If he had been back there would be less in it. A man does not walk to a cache
and stand over it and put the stone down again.

— Then he is coming.

— Then he is dead, said Constant, — which is the other thing, and it is the more
likely thing, and I would rather say it out loud than have both of us thinking it
politely.

He set the bag down on the stone between them.

— Where I am from you take a third. You take a third and you seal it better than
you found it and you leave a mark on the lid, and if the man comes back he knows
somebody was hungry and he knows how much and he knows when. That is not stealing.
That is what a cache is *for*. Nobody buries a winter under a slab in country like
this and believes he is the only man who will ever be short.

— That is your country.

— That is every country I have walked in.

— It is not mine, said Steadfast.

---

He had never had to say what his was and found, saying it, that it came out badly.

He said that a store is not a road. He said that in nineteen years he had kept a
house with a store at the back of it and that the store was the entire reason the
house worked, and that the rule about the store was not a rule about food, it was
the rule that let a man walk in off the road at ten at night in the rain and be
fed without having to ask for it, and that the rule was: you do not go into the
store. You wait until it is put in front of you. He said that the day a traveller
went into the store on his own account there would be no store, and that he had
turned two men out into the weather over it and had not lost a night's sleep on
either of them.

He heard it while it was coming out. It sounded like a man reading a notice off a
wall.

Constant listened to the whole of it without interrupting, which was not his
habit.

Then he said: — You have never once in your life found food.

Steadfast did not answer that.

— I am not being cruel. I am saying the thing. You have been fed every night for
nineteen years off a shelf that somebody else filled, and the rule that kept that
shelf full is a very good rule for a house, and I will keep it in any house you
like. There is no house. There is a hole in the ground under a slab and there is
nobody in it.

— There is a man in it, Steadfast said. — That is my whole point. There is a man
in it. He carried that salt out here on his back.

— Yes, said Constant. — And I would like to meet him. And if I met him I would
tell him what I took and he would say take a third, and if he is the one man in
the country who would say otherwise then I have wronged one man and I have eaten,
and I can carry that.

Steadfast got up and went down the slope and walked out into the flat and stood
there.

---

He was gone the better part of an hour and he did not resolve anything in it. He
went round it twice and both times ended at the same place, which was that he was
right and could not say why, and being right and not being able to say why had
already cost him a night over a map.

When he came back the slab was down.

It was down and it was better than it had been. Constant had rebuilt the whole
mouth of it, and reset the slab on two packed stones so it sat proud on the
uphill side, and combed the loose scree back over the join with the flat of his
hand.

— A third, he said. — Grain and meat. I have not touched the salt.

Steadfast looked at the pale square of ground where the slab had been sitting.

There was a flat stone laid on top of the slab that had not been there before,
and on it Constant had scratched four marks with the point of his knife.

— What is that.

— A third, taken, in the spring, by one man travelling.

Steadfast looked at it for a while.

— He will not know what that means.

— No, said Constant. — Probably not.

He picked up the grain bag and put it in his pack and stood up and did not look
pleased with himself, which was the thing Steadfast had least expected and could
do least with.

— I know he will not, he said. — I do it anyway. I have done it in three
countries and not one man has ever read one of them and I am not going to stop
because of that.

---

They cooked the grain that night.

It was the best thing Steadfast had eaten since the garden and he had two
helpings of it, and cleaned the pot with his finger, and said nothing at all about
that for three days, and Constant did not say anything about it either, and did
not need to, which was worse.

---

## Forty-Four

Six weeks later they were on ground that gave them nothing.

It had not become a crisis. They were short in the way a man is short who is
still walking — half rations for a fortnight would have been a crisis and this was
not that; it was thin, and it went on, and they were both a little slower going
uphill than they had been.

Steadfast said it at the fire, flatly, because he had been carrying it about for
a week and wanted it out.

— We should have taken the salt.

Constant looked up.

— All right.

— That is what you are going to say.

— What would you like me to say.

— Something, said Steadfast. — You were right and I was standing on a rule I have
never once had to pay for. I have been thinking about it since the ridge and it is
not a small thing. Say something.

Constant turned a stick over in the fire.

— I have wanted to be told I was right about that for six weeks, he said. — I
have had whole conversations about it in my head with you losing them. And you
have just said it and it is nothing like as good as I thought it would be, so.
All right.

That was all either of them made of it.

---

Steadfast was awake a while after.

What he had, when he took it apart, was not a principle. It was a table. He had
stood at the head of a table for nineteen years and had been very certain about
the store behind him, and the certainty had cost him nothing, because the store
was full, and it was full because a wagon came in the second week of every month
whether he thought about it or not.

He tried to make that into a thing he could say to Constant in the morning and
could not get it into a shape, and in the morning Constant was up first and had
found a seam of water in a bank of clay and was in a good mood about it, and it
did not come up.

---

## Forty-Five

His mother came up properly in the fourth month.

She had been in everything all along — she was in the way he strapped a pack and
in the way he said certain words — but she came up properly one evening on a rise
with a wind on it, when Constant had eaten and was lying with his head on his
pack.

— She named me, he said. — For the thing she wanted. She did all of us that way
and it did not take on a single one of the others. My brother is named for
patience and he is the worst man I have ever met about a queue.

He said the naming story badly. It was a long story and it was not a good one and
it involved a neighbour and a bet and a great deal of detail about a cow, and
Steadfast could not follow the middle of it, and Constant told it the way men tell
a thing they have heard forty times in a kitchen.

At the end of it he said: — She died the second winter after the road went. Not of
anything. She was sixty-eight and she got cold and then she got worse.

— Where were you.

— Three hundred miles away. Looking, said Constant. — I got there in the March.

He said that without any weight on it at all and Steadfast understood that he had
put the weight somewhere else years ago and had built his whole eighteen months on
top of it.

---

After a while Constant said: — Who is going to be glad you are alive.

Steadfast did not answer straight away, because he had started, without wanting
to, to count.

He got as far as trying to work out how many people had slept under his roof, and
he did the arithmetic honestly — nineteen years, some nights four, some nights
none, the two months every autumn when the pass filled the house — and got past
four thousand and stopped, and then tried to name forty of them and could not.

They had all been glad. That was the thing. They had come in wet and gone out dry
and a great many of them had said so at the door.

— I have fed a great number of people, he said.

— That is not what I asked.

— No.

He looked out at the country going grey.

— One, he said. — There is one. And I shouted at her in her own yard and walked
out through the wall.

— Is she alive.

— I do not know.

— Then that is a thing a man can find out, said Constant, and rolled over, and
did not say anything else about it, that night or afterwards.

It sat there for a long time.

---

## Forty-Six

The country changed for good in the fourth month and neither of them made anything
of it at the time.

They came down a long shoulder of ground in the afternoon and there was a
watercourse at the bottom, and there was water in it.

Not a seam. Not a cup at the lip of a rise after twenty minutes of patience. Water
moving, a hand deep and two paces across, going somewhere with a noise.

Steadfast stopped on the slope and Constant went past him and down and was in it
to the knees before Steadfast had moved, splashing about like an idiot, shouting
something.

Steadfast came down slowly and crouched at the edge and put his hand in and let
the water go over it.

He filled a flask. He held it up against the light to read the level, at the angle,
with the tilt of the head, and then stopped, and looked at what he was doing, and
laughed at himself out loud for the first time in a very long time — because there
was a river in front of him, and he was rationing.

He tipped it out and filled it again and drank the whole thing standing up.

They followed the water for twelve days. It got bigger. On the sixth day there were
trees, four of them, small and wind-bent and entirely miraculous, and Constant
climbed one for no reason and could not get down as easily as he had got up, and
Steadfast stood underneath and gave him instructions that were no help at all.

On the ninth day they saw the smoke of somebody else's fire in the distance, and
it was not a signal, and neither of them went to it.

On the twelfth day the watercourse turned south and they had to decide whether to
follow it.

— It goes somewhere, Constant said.

— It goes south.

— Things go where water goes. That is generally the whole of it.

Steadfast looked south along the run of it, at the green either side, at the way
the ground fell away easy for as far as he could see.

Then he looked north-west, at nothing in particular. Rising ground. No water that
he could see. The same grey-green scrub they had been walking through for four
months, going up.

— I want to go up, he said.

— Why.

And Steadfast stood there and found, with some surprise, that he had an answer,
and that it was not about the road.

— Because I have been going that way since I came over the pale stone, he said,
— and I have not decided anything since. I would like to decide something.

Constant looked at the easy green south, and then at the hard grey up.

— That is a stupid reason, he said.

— Yes.

— I am not saying it is a bad one.

They went up.

## Forty-Seven

The hard grey up went on for six weeks and was worth nothing, and then it was
worth everything, which is how ground works.

Steadfast nearly killed himself on it in the fourth week. There was a slope of
loose shale that had a good line across it and a better line above, and he took
the good one because it was quicker, and was forty feet out on it when it started
to go under him in a slow soft way that he had never felt before and did not
understand.

He heard Constant say his name — say *you*, rather, in the voice you use — and then
he was on his face in the scree with a hand round his forearm and both of them
sliding, and then not sliding, and then lying there breathing.

— The wet line, said Constant, when he could. — You cannot see it but you can hear
it. It sounds different underfoot. Duller.

— I did not hear anything.

— No, said Constant. — You were going quickly.

It was the first time in two and a half years Steadfast had been in real danger
and it had come from ground, which he had thought he understood, and it had been
read correctly by a man twenty-five years younger who had never crossed a waste in
his life.

He was quiet for the rest of that day. Constant let him be, and did not mention
it again, which was its own kind of mercy and which Steadfast recognised, because
it was one of his.

---

The habits went at different speeds. He kept a list of that too, without meaning
to.

The rationing went first, in the river, laughing.

The wood-gathering did not go at all — he still picked up anything dry that he
passed, and by then they were in country with wood in it and Constant had stopped
teasing him about the bundle and had started using it.

The waiting stayed. He still let a silence run three beats past comfortable before
he filled it, and it still made Constant look at him, and he was not going to stop.

And the smoke went. He had not built a column since the day Constant came over the
rise at a trot. He told himself it was because he had company now, and knew that
was not the whole of it, and did not go further with it than that.

---

## Forty-Eight

They came over the top in the seventh week and there was a valley, and there was
smoke in it — four or five threads of it, low, domestic, going up straight — and
Constant stopped and looked at Steadfast.

— We are going down, he said. It was not a question. — I want to be somewhere with
people in it and I have wanted it for a month and I have not said so because you
seem to like this.

— I do not like this.

— You like it more than I do.

They went down.

---

There were perhaps forty of them, and the first thing was that nobody made
anything of the two of them arriving.

A woman with a child on her hip pointed them at a long low building where there
was food and told them to help themselves and went back to what she was doing. A
man mending a hinge looked up and said good evening. Somebody's dog came and
smelled Steadfast's leg thoroughly and left.

Constant found this delightful and had a conversation with four separate people
before the light went. Steadfast found it enormously hard, in a way he had not
been prepared for, and had to go and stand at the edge of the buildings for a
while and look at the dark and be alone with his heart going, and then come back
in.

Nobody remarked on that either.

---

He saw the road in the morning.

He had gone out early because he always did and there was a track running out of
the north end of the valley, and he was on it before he understood what he was
looking at.

It was made. That was the thing. Not worn — *made*. There was a bed of broken
stone under it and a camber to it so the water would come off, and a lip of set
kerb along the downhill side where the ground fell away, and it had been laid by
hand, badly in places and well in others, in sections that did not quite match
because different people had done them.

He walked out along it. It went for about two hours and then stopped.

It stopped at a well. That was all. A well, with a wall round it and a windlass
and a stone trough, and the road ran to it and ended there and did not go anywhere
else.

He walked back the other way and it ran down the valley and past the buildings and
out the far end and stopped again, at the edge of a wide flat of good ground with
half of it under crops and half of it still to break.

Twelve miles, perhaps. Well to field. That was the whole of it.

He stood in the middle of it for a long time.

---

## Forty-Nine

Constant hated it.

That was not obvious at first because Constant hated it politely, and made
conversation, and asked good questions of the man who was explaining it — how long,
who started it, where the stone came from — and it took Steadfast most of the
morning to hear what was underneath the questions.

They had been at it nine years. It had started because two families on the flat
could not get water up in the wet months when the ground went to mud, and someone
had put down a raft of broken stone across the worst forty yards, and that had
worked, and then they had done the next forty because they were already dirty.

— And it will go further, said the man, cheerfully. — There is talk of taking it
south to the crossing. That is another six miles. That is probably me dead before
that is done but somebody will do it.

— Right, said Constant.

That night he did not sleep either.

---

He said it at the edge of the buildings with his back against a wall.

— It is obscene.

Steadfast waited.

— It is twelve miles. It goes from a hole in the ground to a field. You cannot get
anywhere on it. A man could walk the whole of it and back before his dinner and be
exactly where he started, and they have been building it for nine years, and they
talk about it —

He stopped and started again.

— They talk about it the way you talk about a road.

— It is a road.

— It is not a road, Constant said, and his voice went, and he got it back. — A
road goes somewhere you have not been. That is the whole of what a road is. That
is the only thing a road is for. This is a — this is a very long yard.

Steadfast let the silence run. Three beats. Four.

— Say the rest, he said.

— There is no rest.

— There is.

Constant put his head back against the wall and looked up at nothing for a while.

— If that is a road, he said, — then the people who built it are people who
stopped looking. And they are happy. They are the happiest people I have met in
four years and they stopped looking nine years ago and made a yard and called it a
road, and if that is allowed — if that is *allowed* —

And there it was.

— Then you were right about them, Steadfast said.

— Then I was right about them and wrong about everything else, said Constant. —
Then that woman at the well had stopped looking and I told her so and she went
home and I was correct.

He was crying by then, in the way men do who have decided not to, which is worse
to watch.

— I would rather have been a liar, he said. — Do you understand that? For eighteen
months I have wanted somebody to prove me wrong about the road because being wrong
about the road makes me only a fool. This makes me a fool who was *right about her*
and said it out loud to her face in front of a well.

---

Steadfast did not have anything.

That was the honest position and he stayed in it for a while, which was harder
than talking. He thought about saying that she had probably forgotten. He thought
about saying that being right and being cruel are different failures and Constant
had only committed one of them. Both of those were true and both of them were for
Steadfast's comfort and he did not say either.

What he said in the end was:

— I do not think that road is people who stopped looking.

— Then you have not looked at it.

— I have walked it twice.

— Then what is it.

And Steadfast, who had spent two and a half years learning not to hand people
finished things, found that he wanted very badly to hand this one over, and could
not make it into anything.

— It is somebody's forty yards, he said. — And then somebody else's next forty.
That is all I have got. I stood on it this morning and I could see where the
sections change hands and I have not been able to stop thinking about it and I
cannot tell you why.

Constant wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

— That is not an argument.

— No.

— You are supposed to be the one who knows things.

— I have never once been that, said Steadfast, and it came out with more in it
than he had intended, and Constant looked at him properly for the first time that
evening.

---

## Fifty

They stayed twelve days, and by the end of them Constant had helped lay about
sixty feet of the southern extension, which Steadfast did not remark on and
thought about constantly.

He was good at it. Of course he was. He was good at everything in country and he
had a back on him and he learned the camber in an afternoon from a woman who had
been doing it for six years, and he worked hard and laughed with them and came in
filthy, and at night he lay on his back and did not talk.

On the thirteenth morning he was packed.

Steadfast found him at the end of the buildings with his pack on and knew before
anything was said, the way you do.

— I am going back to the well, Constant said.

— Which well.

— Hers.

He said it flatly, as a fact he had been carrying around for twelve days and had
finished carrying.

— It is four months if I go hard. Probably five. I do not know if she is still
there and I do not know what I would say and I have gone round it eleven days and
I cannot get past it, so I am going to go and find out and stop constructing it.

Steadfast said: — Yes.

Not *are you sure*. Not *she may have moved on*. Not *you could stay until the
spring and go with better weather*, which was true, and which he wanted to say so
badly that his jaw ached.

— Yes, he said. — Go.

Constant nodded. He looked out at the valley for a moment, and then at the track
running down it.

— I still think it is a yard.

— I know.

— But I laid sixty feet of it, said Constant, — and I would like it noted that I
laid it properly, and if it is a yard then it is a very well-made yard and the
camber is correct.

— It is noted.

They stood there.

— Listen, said Constant. — I am going to say this badly. You have not told me your
name and I have not asked twice, and I am not asking now. But somebody is going to
ask you and you are going to have to say something, and when you do I would not
say the old one.

— No.

— That is all. That is the whole of my advice and it is not much and you can see
why I mostly talk about packs.

He put his hand out. Steadfast took it, and then did not let go of it, and pulled
him in and held on to him for a moment, which was not something either of them had
done, and Constant made a sound and hung on.

Then he went.

Steadfast watched him up the north track, on the made road, past the place where
the sections changed hands, out to where it stopped at the well — and then off the
end of it, onto the open ground, going east.

He watched until he could not see him.

Then he went back down into the valley, and somebody put a shovel in his hands
before he had said anything, and he took it.

### PART FOUR

## Fifty-One

Nobody asked him to stay and nobody asked him to go, and that was how he came to
be there seven years later.

The shovel was the whole of it. Somebody put it in his hands on the morning
Constant left and he went down to the southern end and worked until the light
went, and in the evening he ate in the long building with everyone else, and in
the morning somebody put a shovel in his hands again. That was the arrangement.
Nobody wrote it down. He asked once, in the first month, who he should speak to
about whether he could stay through the winter, and the man he asked — Ivo, the one
who had been mending a hinge on the first evening, and who turned out to mend most
things — looked genuinely puzzled and said: — Speak to about what?

It took Steadfast most of a season to stop expecting to be assessed.

That was harder than the work. He had spent nineteen years in a world where you
were placed — you were on the road or you were not, you were at a stage or you
were behind it, and there was always somebody whose business it was to know which.
Here there was nothing to be at. There was a road that needed four hundred yards
extending before the wet, and there were people doing it, and if you did it you
were doing it.

He was bad at this for a long time. In the second month he worked nineteen days
without stopping because some part of him believed he was still earning the food,
and on the nineteenth day Rell came down the line, looked at what he had laid, and
said:

— This is worse than yesterday.

— I have done twice as much as —

— I know what you have done. It is worse. Go and sit down.

He did not go and sit down. He worked another two hours and made it worse still,
and then went and sat down, and did not sleep well, and came back in the morning
and took out most of the previous day.

Rell did not mention it. She had been laying road for nine years and had run out of
interest in men who could not be told things.

---

## Fifty-Two

The first winter he had nowhere to be.

That was the discovery of it. The road stopped when the ground froze and there was
no work, and the valley went into a kind of low gear he had no idea what to do
inside, and he found himself walking the twelve miles out to the well and back
most days because it was the only thing that felt like a task.

On about the fourth of those he came back through the yard in the near dark and
there was a light in the low building at the end and the door was open, so he
looked in, and that was Ivo's workshop, and after that he did not walk to the well
so often.

The workshop was not tidy. He had expected it to be, from the way the man mended,
and it was the opposite — it was a place where about nine things were in progress
at once and each of them had its own corner and its own logic and Ivo could find
anything in it in the dark. There was a wheel with two spokes out. There was a
box of hinges sorted by nothing Steadfast could identify. There was, that winter, a
loom being rebuilt for a woman at the far end who had inherited it broken.

— Sit there, said Ivo, without looking up. — Not there. That is where the shaft
goes.

Steadfast sat there.

And Ivo talked.

He talked while he worked in a low continuous way that did not require answering
and did not, as far as Steadfast could ever tell, require listening either. It was
about the loom, and then about the woman's mother who had owned the loom, and then
about a winter when the valley had lost most of a crop and what they had done
about it, and then about a dog, and then back to the loom. Nothing in it was
directed anywhere. It came out at about the rate the work did.

Steadfast sat in that workshop most evenings for three months and afterwards could
not have told you one thing that had been discussed.

He could have told you that his shoulders came down about an inch when he went in
the door.

---

He tried, once, to be useful in there and made a mess of it.

He picked up a chisel to clear a slot Ivo had marked and got it wrong, not badly,
and Ivo took it off him without any fuss and finished the slot and handed the
chisel back handle first and said:

— Bevel down, that one.

— I have not done this.

— No, said Ivo. — You have done road. It is not the same and the tools do not
forgive the same way.

And then he went on about the loom.

That was as close as Ivo ever came, in seven years, to telling him he had done
something wrong.

---

## Fifty-Three

There were forty-one of them, and by the end of that winter he could have told you
most of what mattered about a dozen.

Rell ran the road, in the sense that nobody else did. She was perhaps fifty and had
come into the valley from the north as a girl with her mother, and had been on the
second forty yards, and had never in her life been on the King's Road or wanted to
be. She was not warm. She was exact and she was fair, and when she told you a thing
was wrong she told you once, and she never once told anybody the same thing twice,
which meant everybody listened the first time.

Sal was eight and belonged to everybody in the loose way children in small places
do. She had two questions she asked adults, in rotation, and the first was whether
they had ever seen the sea.

And there was a boy — a young man, in fairness, but Steadfast thought of him as a
boy for about four years and was wrong to — called Petch, who was nineteen and had
been born in the valley and had never been anywhere.

Steadfast noticed him first because of his hands. He was on the far gang most of
that spring and he worked with a kind of unbothered speed that Steadfast, who was
still lifting everything, found faintly insulting; and then one afternoon
Steadfast came up behind his section and looked at it and had to stop, because it
was better than his own and had gone down in a third of the time.

He said so, because it seemed the honest thing.

Petch said: — Yes, and went on working, and it was not arrogance, it was a young
man being told that water is wet.

---

He got his own section in the spring.

That was not a ceremony either. Rell walked the southern end with him and stopped
and said: — From that stake to the elbow. That is yours. Do not ask me about it
every day.

It was ninety yards of the worst ground on the extension, wet in three places and
crossed by a run-off that had to be got under the road rather than over it, and it
took him a hundred and sixty days.

He got the culvert wrong twice. The first time he laid it too shallow and had to
take up forty feet of finished bed to redo it, in the rain, alone, because it was
his section and everybody knew it was his section. The second time he got the fall
right and the mouth wrong and it silted in the first wet month, and he dug it out
and packed the mouth with set stone and it held.

On the day he finished, nobody came. He walked up and down it twice and then went
in for his dinner.

Three days later Rell said, on her way past: — Your elbow is holding.

That was all, and he thought about it for a week.

---

## Fifty-Four

He found out about Rell in the second summer and it took him three weeks to
understand what he had found out.

He had been counting stone. The southern extension needed a run of set kerb and he
had walked the pile and done the sums and written it all down on a piece of slate
in his own hand — lengths, faces, how many carts — because that was what you did,
and he brought it to her at the end of the day rather pleased with himself.

Rell looked at the slate.

She looked at it for slightly too long, which he noticed and did not think about.
Then she handed it back and said: — Tell me.

— It is all on there.

— Tell me.

So he told her, and she listened, and asked him two questions that showed she had
the whole southern end in her head down to the yard, and said it was about right
and he had underestimated the wastage, and went off.

He thought she had been rude.

Three weeks later he was in the workshop at dusk and Ivo had a carter's docket on
the bench and was reading it out — not remarking on it, reading it, in the same low
voice he did everything in — and Rell was standing at the other end of the bench
with her arms folded looking at the wall while he did it. She said *yes* twice and
*that is wrong, it was nine* once. Then she went out.

Neither of them had looked at Steadfast at all.

He sat with it for a while.

---

She told him herself about a month after that, on the road, in the same tone she
used for drainage.

— You worked it out.

— I did.

— Ivo does the dockets and the letters when there are letters. My mother could and
did not teach me, and by the time I might have learned I had this — she put her
chin at the road — and there was never a winter I could spare.

— I could —

— No, said Rell.

And then, because she was fair: — Not because I do not want it. Because you would
be good at it and patient and I would be sixty before I could read a docket as
fast as Ivo reads it to me, and the road would be four miles shorter. That is the
whole arithmetic. I have done it more than once.

Steadfast said nothing, which was the correct thing and which he only just managed.

— It is all in my head, she said. — Every yard of it, and where the water goes,
and who laid what and in which year. That is not a boast. That is what I have
instead.

Then she said: — Your kerb run is nine short, and went on down the line.

---

## Fifty-Five

The winter after that, the road failed.

Not his section. Four hundred yards north of the buildings, laid nine years
earlier, in the second year, when they had been doing forty yards at a time with
no camber and no bed under it because nobody had known any better yet. There was a
hard freeze and then a fast thaw and the whole of it lifted and broke and went to
mud, and a carter coming down with the winter grain went in to the axle and
snapped it, and they lost most of a load in the wet.

Steadfast expected an inquest. That was the shape his mind still made: somebody
had laid that badly, somebody would be answerable, there would be a reckoning of
some kind and probably a meeting.

What happened was that about thirty people went up there the next morning with
tools.

They took the whole four hundred yards out. They did it over nine days in the
cold, in shifts, and the old woman who had laid the original section — Bel, she was
seventy-something and did not work any more — came up to watch, and stood at the
edge of it the whole first day, and at one point Steadfast heard Rell say to her,
without any edge at all:

— We did not know about the bed.

— No.

— Nobody knew about the bed.

— I knew it was wrong when the water stood on it, said Bel. — I did not know why.

And Rell said: — Well. Now we know why.

And that was the entire reckoning, and they relaid it properly, and Bel came up
every day of the nine and no one suggested she should not.

On the sixth day she sat down on the kerb next to Steadfast while he was eating and
said, without introduction:

— We did it in August. It was August and it was dry and the ground was like a road
already and we thought we were finished.

— Yes.

— Everybody was very pleased, she said. — I was very pleased.

She looked down the line at thirty people taking it up.

— This is better, she said, and she did not say it sadly, which he had been braced
for. She said it the way you concede a point in a long argument with somebody you
like.

Steadfast worked the last three days of it and thought about a bulge in a wall
eight hundred miles away and did not know what to do with the thought, so he
carried stone.

---

## Fifty-Six

Sal asked him his name in the second summer, on the road, while he was eating his
bread.

She had asked him about the sea already, twice, and he had said no both times,
which had disappointed her enough that she had moved on to her other question.

— What are you called?

He had known this was coming for two years. He had not prepared anything, because
preparing something would have meant deciding, and he had not decided.

— I have not got one just now, he said.

Sal considered this with complete seriousness.

— Everybody has got one.

— Yes.

— So what happened to yours.

— I put it down.

— Where?

— A long way back.

She thought about that, sitting on the kerb with her heels against the set stone.

— Was it a bad one?

And he found that he had to think about it, and that thinking about it in front of
an eight-year-old was the first time he had looked at it straight.

— No, he said. — It was a good one. It was just not true.

— Oh, said Sal.

She swung her heels for a bit.

— Are you going to go and get it?

He did not answer that, and she did not notice, because she had seen somebody with
a dog further up the road and was gone.

He sat on the kerb for a long time after that with the rest of his bread in his
hand.

---

She did not tell anybody, or she told everybody and nobody minded, which in that
valley amounted to the same thing. What happened was that people went on calling
him nothing at all. If they needed him they came and found him. If they were
talking about him to somebody else, they said *the one on the elbow*, and after a
while they said *the elbow*, and it turned out that a man could be a place.

He noticed that Ivo never used it and never asked. Ivo said *you*, always, in a way
that had no gap in it.

---

## Fifty-Seven

The valley ate together on the first evening of every month, and it took him until
the third year to understand what that was.

It was not a feast. The food was the same food. What was different was that
everybody came — the whole forty-one, the babies, Bel, the two men who did not speak
to each other over something from before Steadfast arrived and who sat at opposite
ends and both came anyway — and the tables went end to end down the middle of the
long building so that it was one table.

That was all. One table, once a month, everybody at it.

The first year he sat at the end and could not eat much. The second year he found
himself at the middle and did not notice until afterwards.

The third year one of the two men stood up in the middle of it and went out.

There was no scene. He was not loud about it. He put his hands on the table and
stood up and walked the length of the room and out of the door, and the room
carried on, and a woman near the top reached over and moved his plate so it was not
in anybody's way.

Steadfast, who had spent two and a half years learning not to ask, asked Ivo about
it afterwards.

— That will be the date, said Ivo.

— What date.

— It is about this time of year. It was a long while ago now.

And he went back to what he was doing, and that was the whole of the information,
and Steadfast never did find out — not in seven years — what had happened between
those two men, and neither of them ever missed a table.

That was the thing about the valley that took him longest. They did not fix it.
They did not sit the two of them down. Nobody made a project of it. They set the
tables end to end once a month and both men came and one of them sometimes left,
and the arrangement held for years because everybody had quietly agreed that it
was better than the alternative, which was one of them not coming.

---

## Fifty-Eight

Petch was the one who made him angry, which nobody had done in three years.

By then he was twenty-three and the size of a door, and had laid more of the
southern extension than anybody except Rell, and had taken to sitting at the same
end of the table as Steadfast for reasons neither of them examined.

It came out at the table one evening. Somebody had asked Steadfast something about
the crossing at the ford, which meant explaining what the ford had been, which
meant the road, and he was three sentences into it — carefully, without the
keeper's shape, having learned that much — when Petch said:

— Why does everyone from out there talk about it like a person who died.

The table went a bit quiet.

— Because it was a road, Petch said. — It was a good road, I have heard, it went a
long way and people used it and now it does not. And every single one of you comes
in here and sits down and cannot say four sentences without it. My mother came off
it before I was born and she still —

— That is enough, said Rell.

— It is not, though, said Petch. — There is a road *outside*. It goes to the well.
We built it. He built ninety yards of it and it took him half a year and the
culvert is good, and I have never once heard anybody from out there call that a
road without their voice doing a thing.

Steadfast, who had been about to say something conciliatory, discovered he was
furious.

— You have never lost one, he said.

— No.

— Then you do not know.

— No, said Petch, not backing off an inch. — I do not know. And you have never had
one that was yours. Everything you have ever walked on was made by somebody else
before you were born and you cannot even hear that you talk about it like an
inheritance. I laid two hundred yards of that with my own hands when I was
fifteen. You mourn the thing you were given. I am proud of the thing I made. Those
are not the same and I am tired of being told mine is smaller.

---

He was awake most of that night, which by then he knew the meaning of.

The boy was wrong. That was the first thing and it stayed true: he was
twenty-three and had never had the ground go out from under him and there was a
whole country of grief he had no idea existed, and he would have it one day and be
sorry for some of what he had said.

And he was right. That was the second thing, and it did not cancel the first, and
Steadfast lay in the dark and made himself hold both, which was a thing he had been
taught by a woman who held two kinds of not-knowing and had told him which one she
could carry.

He had walked the King's Road nineteen years and had never laid a stone of it. It
had been there. It had been made by people whose names nobody had ever told him,
and he had walked it and kept it and directed people onto it and had thought of
that as service, and in three years in this valley he had put down ninety yards of
bad ground and a culvert that took him two attempts, and he could not honestly say
which of those was the larger thing.

He apologised to Petch in the morning, badly, and Petch was mortified and said it
was fine about six times, and after that the two of them worked well together
for years.

---

## Fifty-Nine

Bel died in the fourth winter.

She was eighty or near it and she died the way you hope to, in her own bed with
two of her daughters there, over about three days, and there was nothing bad about
it except that it happened.

Steadfast had not known her well. He had eaten at the same table as her ninety
times and had sat on a kerb with her once and had carried a load of broken stone
past her house every day for a season, and she had once told him his gloves were
no good, which was true.

They buried her at the top of the field, which was where people went.

What he remembered afterwards was not the burial.

It was that on the morning after she died, before it was properly light, he came
out to the yard because he was awake and there was somebody up at the north end of
the road, on the four hundred yards, walking.

It was Rell. She was going up the middle of it at about the pace you walk a thing
you are inspecting, from the bottom of the relaid section to the top, and when she
got to the top she stood for a bit, and then she came back down it, and then she
went in and got her tools because it was a working day.

Steadfast stood in the yard the whole time and did not go out to her and did not
say anything about it then or ever.

That was the only thing anybody did.

---

## Sixty

He started talking about the Waste in the fourth year, and it took him about six
months to get it out properly.

He did it badly at first. He tried, over a winter, to explain a woman in a garden
to people who had never been thirsty, and heard himself making her into a story,
and stopped for a month out of something like shame.

Then he tried again with the practical parts, which turned out to be the way in.
Eleven days of rain a year. A gutter of split wood repaired in four places with
four woods. A second barrel for when you have got the first one wrong. Beans up
canes in a country with no water and no soil worth the name, and a wall carried one
stone at a time from somewhere you cannot see.

Rell wanted to know about the wall and made him draw it twice.

Ivo wanted to know about the gutter and asked four questions Steadfast could not
answer and was annoyed about not being able to answer, and instead drew it in the
dirt three times over two evenings until he had something he liked better than
Grieve's.

Sal, who was ten by then, wanted to know whether the woman had ever seen the sea.

— I do not know, said Steadfast, and found that he minded very much not knowing.

And then one evening, at the end of it, he said the thing he had not planned to
say.

— She has been the only fire in that country for eleven years.

They waited.

— That is the whole of why she stays. There is nothing there that burns. If she
goes, in a year there is no smoke over that fold, and the next one who comes out
half dead walks past a mile east of it and never knows he walked past anything.

He was looking at the table.

— She counts the ones who found her. She has nine. And what she does instead of
sleeping is think about the number she does not have.

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Petch, who was twenty-four by then and had
still never been anywhere, said:

— How much would a fire cost.

---

## Sixty-One

It took two years and it was nothing like what Steadfast had imagined.

What he had imagined, in the first hot week of it, was a chain — a line of fires
across the Waste, kept, at intervals, so that nobody crossed more than four days
without seeing smoke. He had it worked out on his second evening. He had the
spacing.

Ivo killed it in about a fortnight, and did it so gently that Steadfast did not
notice for a while that it was dead.

He did not argue. He asked questions. How much wood does a brazier eat in a night,
and Steadfast did not know, so they burned one for a night in the yard and
measured it. Then how much in a winter. Then what it weighs. Then how far is it
across, and Steadfast said fifteen days at the narrowest, and Ivo went quiet and
did some counting with a stub of chalk on the bench and said:

— You would need a cart in there every six weeks. For each one.

— We could —

— Show me a cart that crosses that ground, said Ivo, not unkindly.

Steadfast sat in the workshop with the chalk numbers in front of him for a long
time. He had wanted the chain very badly and had been carrying it around for a
fortnight like a warm thing in a pocket, and it had taken a man with a piece of
chalk about twenty minutes.

— The eastern edge is hard ground, he said eventually. — A cart could get within
two days of the eastern edge.

— That is one fire, said Ivo.

— That is one fire.

— Then that is what you have got, said Ivo, and went back to the loom, and that was
the end of the chain and the beginning of the thing they actually built.

---

Then Rell took it apart again, differently.

She came and found him at the elbow, which she did not do, and stood there and
said:

— A fire has to have somebody at it.

— Yes.

— So it is not a fire. It is a house. You are asking this valley to build and keep
a house eight hundred miles away and live in it in turns, for ever, and you have
been talking about it for four months as though it were a bonfire.

— I know that.

— You did not know it until about ten days ago, said Rell, which was true, — and
you still have not said it out loud to anybody, which is how a thing like this
kills itself. People find out in the second year what they agreed to in the first.

So he said it out loud, at the next table, badly.

And it went worse than he had feared and better than it deserved. Two households
said no at once and said why, which was money and time and the fact that neither
of them believed the woman was still alive. A man near the bottom said it was the
first sensible thing anybody had proposed in five years and then did not volunteer
for anything. And an argument started about the cache — whether you build a hut
with stores and nobody in it, or a hut with somebody in it — that ran, on and off,
for the best part of a year.

Rell won that one. Her argument was not moral. It was that a hut with nobody in it
would be robbed inside two winters and a hut with somebody in it would not, and
that if you were putting a year of somebody's life into it you might as well put
their hands to work while they were there. In the end it was both, which was
Steadfast's only contribution to the design and was probably wrong.

---

The second summer it stalled, and it stalled the way things do, which is that
nothing happened.

There were four households in it by then and one of them came out. The woman was
expecting and would not take a baby into the Waste, and her husband would not go
without her, and both of those were plainly right and nobody said a word against
either of them and the thing nearly died anyway, because three is not enough for
a rotation and everybody could count.

It sat for a season. Steadfast could not sleep for most of it and got very hard to
be around, and Ivo, who had not said anything about anybody's temper in four years,
said: — You want to go and find a fourth.

— Yes.

— Do not, said Ivo.

— Why.

— Because you will get one, said Ivo. — You are a persuasive man in a bad way when
you want something. And they will come out in the third year and you will have
spent a household to buy a year.

Steadfast did not go and find a fourth.

It came from a direction he had not been watching, which was one of the two men who
did not speak to each other. He put his hand up at a table in the autumn and said
he would take a year, and his wife said they had talked about it, and the other man
— the one he did not speak to — said, from the far end, that they should have the
long cart because it was better on rough ground.

That was all either of them said. It was the only time in seven years Steadfast
heard one of them address anything to the other, and it was about a cart, and
nobody at that table so much as looked up.

---

It went out east in the sixth year. Two carts, nine people, forty days.

He did not go with them.

He wanted to. He had wanted to for two years and had assumed the whole time that
he would, and when the day came he understood that if he went he would spend the
entire forty days steering it, and that it had stopped being his about eighteen
months earlier and everybody knew it except him.

He stood on the road and watched the carts up the north end and out past the well,
and Sal, who was thirteen by then, stood next to him and said: — Are you sad?

— A bit.

— Why?

— Because I would have done it worse, he said, and she looked at him as though he
had made a joke, and he had not, and he did not explain.

---

## Sixty-Two

The carts came back in the spring with the hut built and the first household in it,
and with a piece of news that Steadfast had not thought to want.

There was already smoke out there.

Not hers — hers was four days further west and they had not gone that far. This was
another one, forty miles north of where they built, a man and two women who had put
up something like a shelter at a seam and had been feeding people out of it for
three years, and who had been astonished to see a cart, and who wanted to know
whether more were coming.

Steadfast sat down on the step of the long building when he heard that, and had to
put his hand over his mouth.

Not because it was a triumph. Because for six years he had been carrying, without
ever once looking at it directly, the belief that Grieve was alone out there in a
way that nothing could be done about — that it was one woman and eleven years and a
number she could not stop counting — and it had never occurred to him, not once,
that somebody else might have had the same idea in the same country in the same
years and neither of them would ever know.

He thought about the man who saw the smoke and did not stop. He thought about Ash,
and made himself stop thinking about Ash, which he had got better at and had never
got good at.

And then he thought: she does not know.

---

## Sixty-Three

He told Rell first, in the workshop, which is where things got decided in that
valley whatever anybody said about the table.

— I am going to go back.

Rell did not look up. — For how long.

— I do not know.

— That is not a plan.

— No.

She turned the thing she was holding over.

— Why.

And Steadfast found that he had been getting ready to answer that for two years
without knowing it, and that the answer had changed about four times, and that what
was left was short.

— She thinks she is the only one, he said. — She has thought it for twenty years.
She is not, and there is a hut with a fire on it four days east of her, and there
are three people forty miles north of that, and I know all of it and she knows none
of it, and I am the only person alive who has been in both places.

Rell put the thing down.

— That is a good reason, she said. — I have not heard one of those in a while.

— I am not going to bring her back.

— No.

— She will not come. I asked her once and I am not asking again.

— Good, said Rell. — Then you are going for the right thing, and if you were going
for the other one I would have said so.

Ivo, at the other end of the bench, had not looked up either.

— Your flask is no good, he said.

---

He did not go that year. He would not take the Waste with the cold in front of him
and there was nobody in the valley who thought he should, and so he had a winter of
being a man who has said out loud that he is leaving and is still there, which is
its own particular thing and which nobody enjoyed.

He went in the spring. He was six weeks getting ready and most of it was other
people's doing.

Ivo made him the flask, of a design he had been improving for four years, and was
rude about the old one twice more before he handed it over.

Petch, who had grown into a large calm man and had laid more of that road than
anybody except Rell, spent two days on the route with him and drew a stretch of it
in charcoal on the back of a board — the ground east of the pale stone, which Petch
had never seen and Steadfast had, so that in the end Steadfast was dictating and
Petch was drawing, and Petch marked the parts he was not sure of in a lighter hand
without being told to.

Steadfast looked at that for a while and did not explain it to anybody either.

Sal, who was fourteen and had a face like her mother's, asked him whether he was
going to go and get his name.

— I do not know, he said. — I think it might be waiting there.

— That is where you left it.

— Yes.

And Rell, on the evening before, put a folded slate into his hand with nothing
written on it.

— For when you get there, she said. — He can write. You said he mends and reads.

— That is Ivo.

— I know it is Ivo, said Rell. — There will be one of him there too. There is
always one. Get somebody to put down what she knows about that country before she
dies, because it is all in her head, and I have had nine years of knowing exactly
what that is worth and exactly what it costs.

Steadfast stood in the yard with the slate in his hand.

— I will, he said.

— See that you do, said Rell, and went in.

---

The valley did not make an occasion of his going, which he had known they would not
and was grateful for. He went out on the road at first light with his pack on, and
Rell was at the north end because she was always at the north end at first light,
and she said:

— Your elbow held six winters.

— It did.

— It will hold six more. Somebody will have to do the mouth again in about four
years and I will tell them you did it twice and got it wrong once, so they do not
think it is easy.

— Thank you.

— Go on, then, said Rell.

---

He walked up the made road, past the place where the sections change hands, past
Bel's four hundred yards that they had taken up and laid again, past Petch's two
hundred that a boy of fifteen had put down with his own hands, out to the north end
where it stopped at the well.

He stood at the end of it.

Then he stepped off the made ground onto the open, going east, the way Constant had
gone, and did not look back at the valley, because he had spent seven years there
and knew exactly what it looked like, and because looking back would have put him
wrong.

### PART FIVE

## Sixty-Four

It took him a hundred and four days and he was fifty-nine years old, and there
were four occasions on it when he thought he had made a mistake that would kill
him, and one of them nearly did.

He will not be interesting about the journey. He had Petch's board and it was good
for the first three weeks and then he was past where he had told Petch anything.
He had Ivo's flask, which was better than the old one, and he took it out at a seam
under a lip nine days west of the garden and held it up to the light at the angle,
and did not laugh this time.

There are two things on it worth putting down and neither of them is scenery.

---

## Sixty-Five

He came to the hut on the eighty-fourth day and did not say who he was for about
an hour.

He had not decided to do that. He came over the last of the hard ground in the
afternoon and there it was, on the flat where the cart track died, exactly where
they had said and nothing like the thing he had drawn — squarer, lower, with a
walled yard on the lee side that was not in any plan and a stack of cut brush
against the north face taller than the building — and there was a woman in the yard
doing something to a barrel, and she straightened up and saw him and said:

— Come in and sit down.

Then she went and got food.

She fed him first. She asked him nothing until he had eaten and she did not watch
him eat, and she put more in front of him without being asked, twice, and did not
make anything of the fact that he took it. Nobody had taught her that. There was
no possible way anybody had taught her that.

Steadfast sat in the yard of a building he had argued about for two years at a
table in a valley eight hundred miles away, and was fed by it, and did not trust
himself to say anything for a long time.

Her name was Hesta. Her husband was Fen and he came in at dusk with a boy of about
fifteen, and it was the boy who worked it out, from the flask, because Ivo's flasks
were known in that valley and the boy had grown up with one.

— That is one of Ivo's.

— It is.

There was a silence of a particular kind in the yard after that.

---

They were good about it and they were also careful about it, and he understood
inside about ten minutes that they had received him as an inspection.

So he asked them what was wrong with it.

It took Fen a while to believe he meant it and then it took him until midnight to
finish. The pit sweated; whoever had lined it had lined it for a valley and not for
this ground, and they had lost a winter's grain in the first autumn and had said
nothing about it in the letter that went back with the carts. The door opened
outward and the wind here came from the side it opened into, so that in a blow you
could not shut it one-handed, and Hesta had done it one-handed with a lamp in the
other hand more times than she wanted to say. The brazier on the roof was the worst
of it. It was beautiful and it was visible for a day's walk and it was on a roof, in
ice, and Fen had come off the ladder in the second winter and had been lucky, and
they now lit it from a scaffold of poles they had built themselves and did not
enjoy.

Steadfast listened to all of it and wrote none of it down and did not once explain
why any of it had been decided.

At the end Fen said: — You are not arguing.

— No.

— The man who drew it would argue.

— The man who drew it has not stood on that roof, said Steadfast.

---

The tally was cut into the doorpost, on the inside, where you would put your hand
going out.

Nineteen.

He asked and Hesta told him, and she told him the rest of it in the same voice,
because it was all the same list to her: that two of the nineteen had died here,
one within a day of coming in and one after nine days of being nursed, and that
they were buried out past the brush stack under stones because the ground was hard,
and that a woman who came through in the first summer had come back through going
the other way in the spring and had stopped for a night and been well.

— Nineteen, said Steadfast.

— Nineteen, said Hesta. — Some of them are the same person twice. I count them
twice. I do not know if that is right.

— It is right.

— Yes, she said. — That is what I thought.

---

He asked, on the second morning, whether they had been west.

Fen said no.

— It is four days.

— It is four days there, said Fen, — and four days back, and a day at the far end
if she is what you say she is, and that is nine days with nobody at the fire.

Steadfast opened his mouth.

— I have thought about it, said Fen. — I have thought about it since the carts
told us she was there. I have got as far as working out that if I go and somebody
comes and there is nobody here, then I have swapped a woman who has managed for
twenty years without me for whoever that is.

He picked up the barrel lid.

— If you tell her about us, tell her we know. That is all I have got.

Steadfast said that he would.

The boy walked him out to the edge of the hard ground in the morning and asked him
four questions about the valley, all of them about the road, and the last one was
whether it was true that it did not go anywhere.

— It goes from a well to a field.

— That is what my father said, said the boy. — I did not believe him.

---

## Sixty-Six

He went north because of nothing except that he had four days of food and it was
forty miles.

He found them on the third day, which was luck. There was a seam under a long low
scarp and there was a wall at the foot of it — a proper wall, chest high, dry-laid,
running about eighty feet — and a roof of brush on poles over a stone trough, and
the trough was full.

There was nobody at it.

He drank. The water was cold and clean and there was a wooden cup on a peg with a
hole bored through the handle, and he put it back on the peg, and stood there
looking at the arrangement and understanding roughly none of it.

They came out to him after about a quarter of an hour, which meant they had been
watching him for a quarter of an hour. Two women and a man, none of them young. The
man carried nothing and stopped a good way off.

— The water is free, he said. — There is no charge and there is no ask. Drink what
you want and fill what you carry.

— Thank you.

— And you do not come inside the wall.

---

He was there most of a day and he never went inside the wall.

That was the whole of the arrangement and it was not negotiable and they were not
unkind about it. The man was Orrin. The older woman was Beck and she did most of
the talking, which was not much. The younger one was Til and she said almost
nothing at all.

They filled the trough by hand every morning from the seam, which took the three of
them two hours, and they had done it every morning for three years, and anybody who
came got water and got it without being looked at twice. They gave nothing else.
No food. No fire. Nobody slept under the brush roof and nobody slept inside the
wall.

Steadfast asked why, once, because he could not help it.

— We did that, said Orrin.

And that was the whole of the answer, and Steadfast, who had spent nine years
learning what a closed door sounds like, did not put his hand on it.

---

He told them about the hut anyway. He had come forty miles to do it and he did it
badly.

He told them there was a fire four days south-west of them, and a woman four days
west of that, and eight hundred miles east a valley that had built the first one
and would build another if there was a reason, and that none of them had known
about any of the others until this year, and that he was the only person alive who
had been in more than one of the three places.

Beck heard him out. She was patient about it in the way you are patient with a man
selling something.

— How far is the hut.

— Three days if you are quick. Four.

— And what do you want us to do about it.

Steadfast found that he had not got past the telling. He had spent two years and
eight hundred miles on the telling and had never once got to the next sentence, and
he stood there at somebody else's trough and heard himself go through it — that
they could send somebody, that they could trade, that the valley had grain and this
place had water and a cart could be got within two days of the hut, that if a man
came through here going south-west somebody could say the words *four days,
south-west* —

— We could, said Beck.

— Yes.

— And we might, she said. — Or we might not, and either way you are not going to
know, because you are going west in the morning.

She said it without any edge on it at all. It was the plainest sentence anybody had
said to him in nine years.

— We know now, she said. — That is what you came for. You have got it.

---

He got the water and he went in the morning and nobody walked him out.

Except that Til came after him about half a mile on and caught him up on the flat
and stood there with her hands in her sleeves.

— The valley, she said. — The one that built the hut.

— Yes.

— Is the road real.

He told her. He told her the whole of it, twelve miles, well to field, nine years
of it, the sections where the hands changed, the woman who had laid four hundred
bad yards and had gone back and done them again in her sixties, the fact that you
could walk the entire length of it and back before your dinner and be exactly where
you started. He heard himself telling it to a stranger in the middle of nowhere and
did not stop.

Til listened all the way through.

Then she asked two more questions. One was how many people, and one was how they
had decided who did which forty yards, and he answered both, and she nodded at the
answers as though she were checking them off against something.

— Right, she said.

And she went back.

He watched her go for a while, and then turned round and went west, and he never
found out, and he thought about it for the rest of his life.

---

## Sixty-Seven

He came at the country from the wrong side, which is to say from the side he had
left by, and everything he had been told to look for he had been told to look for
going the other way. The seam under the fourth rise took him two days to find
because he was reading the ground reversed. The third fold he walked past twice.

And then on the ninety-ninth day he came round a shoulder and there was a stand of
burnt scrub on his right hand, and he stopped so fast he hurt his back.

He stood and looked at it for a while, on the right, where it had always been on
the left.

Then he sat down on his pack in the middle of nowhere and put his hands over his
face for a minute, and got up, and went on.

---

## Sixty-Eight

He smelled the smoke on the afternoon of the fourth day after that, and it
frightened him, though by then there was nothing frightening about it.

The garden had a second wall.

That was the first thing, from the top of the fold. The old wall was there, waist
high, the whole way round, four sides, and running out from the north-east corner
of it was a second enclosure about half again the size with a lower wall and rows
in it he did not recognise. There were more canes. There was a lean-to on the
south side that had not existed, with a proper door on it, hung well.

And there was a second building, small, thirty yards off, with its own chimney and
smoke coming out of it.

He stood on the fold for longer than he meant to.

She was in the far rows and she had seen him. He could tell, because she had
stopped, and had straightened up with a hand in the small of her back, and was
standing there facing him, and had been for some time.

He came down.

She came out through the gap that was not a gap any more — there was a gate in it
now, hung on two straps of leather, standing open — and walked out from her own
wall onto the open ground, and stopped.

Six feet.

Twenty-one years of it, the distance she had put between herself and everyone who
had ever come over that fold, and he knew the exact number of paces and he stopped
at it out of pure habit, at the place, on his side of it, because that was what he
had been taught by watching.

And Grieve looked at him standing there at her own distance, and made a sound that
was almost a laugh and was not, and crossed it, and got hold of him.

She was not a large woman and she was sixty and she nearly had him over.

---

They did not say anything for a while. Then she stood back and looked at him
properly and said:

— You are grey.

— Yes.

— Steadfast, you are —

She stopped. She had said it in the ordinary way, the way she had said it four
hundred times over two years, pass me that, the light is going, and it had come out
of plain habit before she had thought about it, and she heard it, and he watched
her hear it.

— No, she said. — Sorry.

— It is all right.

— It is not. I knew that before you left and I said it every day anyway.

— You said it every day because I could not, said Steadfast. — I worked that out
about four countries ago and I have wanted to tell you since.

Grieve looked at him for a moment.

— Well, she said. — Come in. There is somebody in the house and I do not want to
leave him long.

---

## Sixty-Nine

His name was Aldo and he was very old and he was not entirely there.

He was in the house, in a chair by the stove that had been made for him — the joints
were wrong, it was a chair somebody had built without much idea how — with a
blanket over his knees, and he looked up when they came in and said something
pleasant and slightly beside the point, and Grieve answered it as though it had
been the point.

She fed him. She fed him *by hand*, some of it, cutting things up small and putting
them into his hand and putting his hand where he could find his mouth, and talking
the whole time in a low continuous way that did not require answering.

Steadfast stood in the door of the house he had lived in for two years and watched
a woman who had once let a girl walk east into nothing with a flask and a bundle
because the girl had not asked her to stop.

He got it, then, and it went through him like cold water.

There had never been a method. He had spent nine years turning a distance and a
silence into a doctrine, and had got it wrong with a young man over a map.

And she was cutting an old man's food up small because that was what was in front
of her now.

— How long has he been here.

— Two years in the spring. He came over the fold on his own feet and he could talk
then. He is somebody's father and I do not know whose and neither does he now.

— And you —

— I built him a chair, Grieve said. — It is a bad chair. I am not Ivo.

Steadfast opened his mouth and shut it.

— You do not know who Ivo is, he said.

— No, said Grieve. — But you said it like I would.

---

## Seventy

He told her on the second evening, out on the step, because he could not do it in
the house.

He had rehearsed it for a hundred and four days and every version had been wrong,
and what came out in the end was:

— There is a fire four days east of here.

Grieve did not say anything.

— On the hard ground where a cart can come within two days. There is a stone hut
with water in it and dry wood and food in a sealed pit, and a brazier on the roof
that whoever is living there keeps lit. Four households out of a valley eight
hundred miles from here take it a year each. The first one has been in it fifteen
months.

Still nothing.

— And there was already one, he said. — Forty miles north of that. A man and two
women at a seam with a wall round it. Three years before we built, and they had
never heard of anybody and neither had we. I stood at their trough twelve days ago
and drank out of a cup on a peg.

He made himself stop.

Grieve sat with her hands round her cup and looked out at the dark where the Waste
was, and did not move for a long time, and Steadfast, who had learned to let a
silence run three beats past comfortable, let this one run as long as it wanted.

— Say the number, she said at last.

— What?

— The number they have had. At the hut. Say it.

— Nineteen, he said. — In fifteen months. They counted before I got there. I did
not have to ask.

Grieve put the cup down on the step.

Then she put her hand over her mouth and made a sound he had never heard out of
her and hoped never to hear again, and he did not move, and she got hold of the
edge of the step with her other hand and held on to it and cried the way people
cry who have been standing up for a very long time.

He did not touch her. That was not restraint and it was not method. He simply
looked at her and understood, correctly, that she was not crying about the
nineteen.

---

She said it herself, later, when she could.

— I have thought about that number every night for twenty-one years, she said. —
The one I do not have. The ones who came over that ground and did not come close
enough, and I have made them up, I have made up faces, I have made up a boy of
about fourteen and I have thought about him for eleven years and he does not exist,
I invented him because the number needed a face.

She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist and left dirt there, and he could
not look at her for a second.

— And you are telling me some of them are nineteen.

— Some of them are nineteen.

— And some of them are still the other thing.

— Yes, said Steadfast, because it was true and because she would have known if he
had softened it.

— Yes, said Grieve.

She picked the cup back up.

— Good, she said. — That is the right answer. If you had told me it was all of
them I would have thrown you off the fold.

---

He gave her the message two days later, over the beans, because there had been no
way to give it on the step.

— The man at the hut. Fen. He asked me to tell you that they know you are there.

Grieve went on working down the row.

— Is that all of it.

— He said it was all he had got.

— Yes, she said.

She moved along on her knees and did the next plant and the one after it, and
Steadfast had got as far as deciding that was the end of it when she said, without
turning round:

— Four days.

— Four days.

— I have been four days from a fire for fifteen months, she said, — and I have
been sitting here doing my arithmetic every night of it.

She sat back on her heels and looked out east at nothing in particular, and he did
not say anything, and after a while she went back to the row.

---

## Seventy-One

He saw the wall on the third morning.

He had walked it the first evening in the dark and had not looked, or had not let
himself, and on the third morning he went out early because he always did and
walked the whole four sides in the flat light with his hands behind his back.

It was good. The north run had settled and been repointed. The gate was hung well.
The second enclosure was newer work and had been done by more than one pair of
hands, and he stood at the join for a while and could see exactly where the
sections changed.

And in the middle of the north run, in eighty feet of honest wall that had been
taken up and relaid at least once since he left, there was a bulge.

Two stones forced together that did not want to go, out of line with everything on
either side of it, ugly as sin, load-bearing.

She had taken the wall down and put it back up and left it in.

He stood in front of it for a long time. Then he put his hand flat on it, the way
he had put his hand flat on the grass in a doorway a thousand miles east of
there, and left it there a moment, and took it away.

He did not ask her about it. She did not mention it. Neither of them ever did.

---

## Seventy-Two

The stranger asked on the fifth day and it was almost funny that it was him.

There was a man passing — not stopping, a lean quick man who took food and would
not sit down, going somewhere with his own reasons — and he was in the yard with
his pack on and he said to Grieve, jerking his chin at Steadfast:

— And him. What is he called.

Steadfast said: — I have not got one just now.

He said it the way he had said it to an eight-year-old on a kerb, flatly, without
any weight on it, and it was the fourth or fifth time he had said it in seven
years and it had stopped being difficult.

The lean man looked at him as though he were slightly simple.

Grieve said, without looking up from what she was doing: — He is the ninth.

The man waited for more, did not get it, decided it was not worth his trouble, and
went.

Steadfast stood in the yard.

— The ninth, he said.

— That is what you are. You are the ninth one who came over that fold and found
it. I have had thirty-one now. You are still the ninth.

She said it the way she said everything, as a fact about the arrangement of the
world that she had been carrying so long she had stopped noticing the weight of
it, and then she looked up and saw his face and stopped.

— Is that wrong?

— No.

— Because I can —

— No, said Steadfast. — No. Leave it.

And that was the whole of it, and it was not a ceremony, and nobody said anything
else about it, and by the end of that week it was what she called him and it did
not sound strange in her mouth at all.

---

## Seventy-Three

He noticed Aldo's hands in the first week.

The old man sat in his bad chair with the blanket over his knees and his hands
went, all day, in his lap, and Steadfast had taken it for the ordinary restlessness
of a man with nothing left to do until he sat opposite him one afternoon with
nothing of his own to do and actually watched.

They were not restless. They were doing something. The left one held and turned
and the right one went over and under and came back, over and under, at a rhythm,
and stopped at the same place each time and started again.

He watched for most of an hour and then went out and split four bean canes down
their length with a knife, and soaked them in the barrel for a day, and on the
third morning he put them in the old man's lap.

Aldo looked at them. He did not look pleased or interested and he did not say
anything about them. His hands went into the pile and started, and by the middle
of the afternoon there was a basket.

It was a good basket. It was better than anything in the house. The rim was worked
back on itself in a way Steadfast had never seen and there was not a loose end
anywhere on it, and when it was done Aldo's hands went on in his lap over the top
of it, over and under, at the same rhythm, and he looked at the door.

Steadfast could not thank him. That was the part. There was nobody there to
thank — the man who had done it was not available to be told he had done it, and
saying so would only have produced something pleasant and beside the point.

He took it in and put it on the table.

Grieve came in at dark and saw it and picked it up and turned it over once. Then
she put it by the door with the other one, and in the morning she took it out to
the rows and filled it, and used it every day after that, in the ordinary way,
because it was the best basket in the house.

---

There was a bad night in the fourth week.

Aldo woke in the dark and did not know where he was and was frightened, properly
frightened, the fear of a man in a strange room, and he called out. He called out a
name. It was not Grieve.

— Nan, he said. — Nan.

And Grieve, from the other side of the room, in the dark, with her voice exactly as
it always was: — I am here.

He said it perhaps forty times over the next two hours and every single time she
answered it, and after the first hour she got up and sat by him and let him hold on
to her forearm, and answered it lying with her head back against the wall with her
eyes shut, and did not once say her own name or his or the words *it is all right*.

In the morning she was up before either of them and had the pot on.

Steadfast said, at the table: — Who is Nan.

— I do not know.

— And you did not —

— He gets about four hours back if somebody answers, said Grieve. — I do not need
to know who she is.

She put a bowl in front of him.

— Do not tell him about it, she said. — He will not know what you mean and he will
be embarrassed, and he cannot do anything with embarrassed. He has nowhere to put
it down.

---

Steadfast washed him for the first time in the sixth week and made a bad job of
it, and Aldo was patient with him, which was the wrong way round and could not be
helped.

He got better at it. He got better at the food too, which was cutting things small
and putting the fork where the hand would find it and talking the whole time in a
low continuous way about nothing, and he found after a while that he did not have
to think about what to say, because it did not matter what he said, and that this
made it the easiest talking he had done in nine years.

It was the hardest work he did in the whole of the visit and there was nothing in
it that anybody would ever be able to call anything.

---

## Seventy-Four

He stayed eleven weeks.

He worked. Of course he worked. He did the gutter properly, with Ivo's four
questions in his head and Ivo's drawing in his hand, and it was better than her
gutter and she made him show her twice and then said, — Well, that is annoying, and
used it.

He took the old man out into the sun on the good days, chair and all, and sat with
him, and had long conversations with him that were about four different things at
once and did not need to be about anything.

He built forty yards of a new wall on the west side of the second enclosure and
did it properly, and Grieve watched him lay six stones without lifting any of them
and said nothing at all, and went inside, and he heard the pot go on.

---

## Seventy-Five

Somebody came over the fold in the eighth week and Steadfast watched the whole of
it from inside the house, which he had never done.

The man was five months out and he was furious.

That was clear from the top of the fold. He came down it fast and badly, at the
stumble a man does when he will not slow down, and he came in through the gate
without stopping at anything and stood in the middle of the yard and looked round
at the rows and the canes and the second enclosure and the two chimneys, and said,
loudly, to nobody:

— Well, that is nice.

Grieve came out of the far rows with the basket on her hip.

— There is food, she said. — In the house. Help yourself, it is on the shelf by
the stove.

— You have got *canes*.

— I have.

— I have been five months, he said. — Five months. I have eaten grass. Do you
know that there are people two weeks south of here on their knees, and you have
got a gate —

— The bread is the round one, said Grieve. — Not the flat one, that is for the old
man, he cannot manage the crust.

And she went back to the rows.

---

He ate. He ate a great deal and he was ashamed of eating it and got worse
afterwards, not better, which Steadfast had seen before and had a name for and
half a dozen things he would ordinarily have done about.

He sat at the table in the house and did not do any of them.

He could have. That was the thing he sat with for the whole of that evening, with
his hands round a cup and the man across the table from him going at it — about the
south, about the road, about people who sit in holes with walls round them while
the country dies, about how long it must have taken to build all this and what
must have been going on elsewhere while it was being built — and Steadfast had
nine years of it in him and could have taken the man apart and put him down
gently, and knew the order he would have done it in.

Grieve came in and out. She put wood on. She did the old man's food and cut it
small and sat with him. Twice she answered something the traveller said, both
times a plain factual answer to a factual part of it, and both times he talked
over the end of it.

She never once tried to bring him down.

Steadfast did, in the end. He could not sit in it any longer and he said something
about how the wall had taken nine years and had been two people, and it came out
placating, and the man turned on him and asked him what exactly he was, then,
sitting there, and Steadfast found that he did not have an answer that would fit
in the room.

Grieve did not look up.

The man went out to the lean-to about an hour later and shut the door hard enough
to be heard.

---

— You did not say anything to him, Steadfast said, when the house was quiet.

— No.

— I would have.

— I know, said Grieve. — You did.

He turned his cup round.

— Was I wrong.

Grieve thought about it, which he had not expected, and took long enough that he
knew she was actually doing it.

— I do not know, she said. — He is nine days from anywhere and neither of us is
ever going to find out.

She got up and banked the stove.

---

In the morning the man was different, the way they are. He was quiet and would not
look at either of them and stood by the gate with his pack on, and said, to the
ground:

— I am sorry about last night.

— You ate and you slept, said Grieve. — That is what it is for.

Steadfast had packed the bundle.

He had done it while she was out at the barrel, without asking her and without
being told, taking the cloth down from the shelf where it lived and doing the three
things and tying it — bread, the hard cheese, a stoppered flask — and he came out
and put it in the man's hands.

— Two days of it, he said. — Do not drink the flask on the first day, no matter
what you tell yourself about the second.

The man nodded and went out through the gate and up the fold and did not turn
round.

Grieve had come back with the barrel lid in her hand and had stopped and watched
the last part of it from the corner of the house.

She looked at the knot for a moment.

Then she went and put the lid on the barrel and got on with the morning, and did
not say anything about it that day, or the next, or ever.

---

## Seventy-Six

And one evening she took a box out from under the shelf by the stove.

It was wooden and about the size of two hands and it had a lid that fitted. She
took it out, and sat with it on her knees in the chair by the fire for perhaps a
quarter of an hour, and did not open it, and put it back.

He did not ask. She did not offer. He thought about it for the rest of his life and
never knew, and that was correct, and he knew it was correct at the time.

---

## Seventy-Seven

He said the other thing on the last evening, on the step, which was where she had
refused to let him say it nine years earlier.

— You would not let me say it, he said. — On the step. The morning I went.

— I remember.

— I have carried it since.

— I know you have, said Grieve. — That is why I would not let you. It would have
come out wrong and you would have carried the wrong version, and I would rather you
carried the true one for nine years than the wrong one for one.

— That is a very long game.

— I am sixty years old and I live in a hole, she said. — Long games are what I
have got.

He laughed. Then he sat with it for a while, and she let him, and the light went
orange the way it did an hour before it went.

— It was not thank you, he said. — That is what I have worked out. I had thank you
ready and it was not that.

— No.

— It was: I am sorry that I am going.

He looked at his hands.

— Not sorry that I went. Sorry that it was going to cost you and that I was going
to do it anyway, both at once, and I did not have anywhere to put both of those at
once for a very long time.

— And now you have.

— Now I have got somewhere to put them. I could not tell you where it is.

Aldo said something from inside, twice, and Grieve got up and went in and was gone
four or five minutes, and came back out and sat down further along the step than
she had been, and neither of them picked it up in the same shape.

— You said I was right, he said, after a while. — On that morning. That I had to
have my own reason for staying and could not have yours.

— I did.

— You were right.

— I was not, said Grieve. — I said it because I had said yes to a man once and had
to watch what it did to him for four years, and I was not going to be able to do
that twice. It has come out well. That is not the same as my having been right, and
I am not going to be handed it now as though it were.

She pulled her sleeve down over her hand.

— It did cost.

— I know.

— I am telling you because you are old enough to hear it without turning it into a
reason to stay. It cost for about two years, badly, and then it was a thing that
had happened.

— Yes.

— And I would not have had you stay.

He waited for her to put the two of them together, because she had put two things
together in front of him a hundred times and it was the thing she did.

She did not. She sat there and let both of them lie where they were, and after a
while he understood that she was not going to, and that it was not delicacy.

— Right, he said.

— The gate wants a new strap, said Grieve. — There is hide in the crate under the
bench. Do it in the morning while the light is on that side.

---

## Seventy-Eight

He went on a clear morning.

He did the gate strap first, in the flat light, with the hide off the crate under
the bench, and it took him about twenty minutes, and it was a better strap than the
one that had been on it and neither of them said so.

She packed him two days of food, the same cloth, the same three things, and
neither of them said anything about that either, and this time he laughed while
she was doing it and she said — what — and he said — nothing — and she said —
liar — and went on packing.

— The seam under the fourth rise, she said.

— I know.

— And the burnt scrub —

— On the left, going out. I know. I found it on the right coming in and I sat down
on my pack.

Grieve stopped what she was doing.

— Did you.

— For a minute.

— Huh, she said.

She put the bundle in his hands.

— Do not drink the flask on the first day, no matter what you tell yourself about
the second.

— I know.

— I know you know. I am saying it.

He got the bundle into the pack. Aldo was asleep in his chair in the sun with the
blanket over his knees and Steadfast had said goodbye to him already, twice,
because he would not remember the first one and possibly not the second.

At the gate Grieve said: — Are you coming back?

He thought about it honestly, which she deserved.

— I do not know, he said. — It is a hundred and four days and I am fifty-nine.

— That is not a no.

— It is not a no.

— Good, said Grieve. — Then I will not tidy the lean-to.

He went out through the gate and up onto the fold and at the top of it he turned
round, which he had not done last time.

She was standing in the gate.

Not in the rows. Not with her back to him. Standing in the gate with her hand up
against the post, watching him go, and when she saw him turn she lifted the hand
off the post and held it up, and kept it there.

He put his hand up.

Then he went down the far side and the smoke went out of sight behind him about a
quarter of an hour later, and he did not stop to look for it, because he knew
exactly where it was, and because there was a hut with a brazier on it four days
east, and a man and two women at a seam forty miles north of that, and none of
them knew about each other yet, and he was the only person alive who had been in
all three places.
