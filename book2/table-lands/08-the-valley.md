# Draft — the Table Lands (expanded)

---

## Fifty-One

Nobody asked him to stay and nobody asked him to go, and that was how he came to
be there seven years later.

The shovel was the whole of it. Somebody put it in his hands on the morning
Constant left and he went down to the southern end and worked until the light
went, and in the evening he ate in the long building with everyone else, and in
the morning somebody put a shovel in his hands again. That was the arrangement.
Nobody wrote it down. He asked once, in the first month, who he should speak to
about whether he could stay through the winter, and the man he asked — Ivo, the one
who had been mending a hinge on the first evening, and who turned out to mend most
things — looked genuinely puzzled and said: — Speak to about what?

It took Steadfast most of a season to stop expecting to be assessed.

That was harder than the work. He had spent nineteen years in a world where you
were placed — you were on the road or you were not, you were at a stage or you
were behind it, and there was always somebody whose business it was to know which.
Here there was nothing to be at. There was a road that needed four hundred yards
extending before the wet, and there were people doing it, and if you did it you
were doing it.

He was bad at this for a long time. In the second month he worked nineteen days
without stopping because some part of him believed he was still earning the food,
and on the nineteenth day Rell came down the line, looked at what he had laid, and
said:

— This is worse than yesterday.

— I have done twice as much as —

— I know what you have done. It is worse. Go and sit down.

He did not go and sit down. He worked another two hours and made it worse still,
and then went and sat down, and did not sleep well, and came back in the morning
and took out most of the previous day.

Rell did not mention it. She had been laying road for nine years and had run out of
interest in men who could not be told things.

---

## Fifty-Two

The first winter he had nowhere to be.

That was the discovery of it. The road stopped when the ground froze and there was
no work, and the valley went into a kind of low gear he had no idea what to do
inside, and he found himself walking the twelve miles out to the well and back
most days because it was the only thing that felt like a task.

On about the fourth of those he came back through the yard in the near dark and
there was a light in the low building at the end and the door was open, so he
looked in, and that was Ivo's workshop, and after that he did not walk to the well
so often.

The workshop was not tidy. He had expected it to be, from the way the man mended,
and it was the opposite — it was a place where about a dozen things were in progress
at once and each of them had its own corner and its own logic and Ivo could find
anything in it in the dark. There was a wheel with two spokes out. There was a
box of hinges sorted by nothing Steadfast could identify. There was, that winter, a
loom being rebuilt for a woman at the far end who had inherited it broken.

— Sit there, said Ivo, without looking up. — Not there. That is where the shaft
goes.

Steadfast sat there.

And Ivo talked.

He talked while he worked in a low continuous way that did not require answering
and did not, as far as Steadfast could ever tell, require listening either. It was
about the loom, and then about the woman's mother who had owned the loom, and then
about a winter when the valley had lost most of a crop and what they had done
about it, and then about a dog, and then back to the loom. Nothing in it was
directed anywhere. It came out at about the rate the work did.

Steadfast sat in that workshop most evenings for three months and afterwards could
not have told you one thing that had been discussed.

He could have told you that his shoulders came down about an inch when he went in
the door.

---

He tried, once, to be useful in there and made a mess of it.

He picked up a chisel to clear a slot Ivo had marked and got it wrong, not badly,
and Ivo took it off him without any fuss and finished the slot and handed the
chisel back handle first and said:

— Bevel down, that one.

— I have not done this.

— No, said Ivo. — You have done road. It is not the same and the tools do not
forgive the same way.

And then he went on about the loom.

That was as close as Ivo ever came, in seven years, to telling him he had done
something wrong.

---

## Fifty-Three

There were forty-one of them, and by the end of that winter he could have told you
most of what mattered about a dozen.

Rell ran the road, in the sense that nobody else did. She was perhaps fifty and had
come into the valley from the north as a girl with her mother, and had been on the
second forty yards, and had never in her life been on the King's Road or wanted to
be. She was not warm. She was exact and she was fair, and when she told you a thing
was wrong she told you once, and she never once told anybody the same thing twice,
which meant everybody listened the first time.

Sal was seven and belonged to everybody in the loose way children in small places
do. She had two questions she asked adults, in rotation, and the first was whether
they had ever seen the sea.

And there was a boy — a young man, in fairness, but Steadfast thought of him as a
boy for about four years and was wrong to — called Petch, who was nineteen and had
been born in the valley and had never been anywhere.

Steadfast noticed him first because of his hands. He was on the far gang most of
that spring and he worked with a kind of unbothered speed that Steadfast, who was
still lifting everything, found faintly insulting; and then one afternoon
Steadfast came up behind his section and looked at it and had to stop, because it
was better than his own and had gone down in a third of the time.

He said so, because it seemed the honest thing.

Petch said: — Yes, and went on working, and it was not arrogance, it was a young
man being told that water is wet.

---

He got his own section in the spring.

That was not a ceremony either. Rell walked the southern end with him and stopped
and said: — From that stake to the elbow. That is yours. Do not ask me about it
every day.

It was ninety yards of the worst ground on the extension, wet in three places and
crossed by a run-off that had to be got under the road rather than over it, and it
took him a hundred and sixty days.

He got the culvert wrong twice. The first time he laid it too shallow and had to
take up forty feet of finished bed to redo it, in the rain, alone, because it was
his section and everybody knew it was his section. The second time he got the fall
right and the mouth wrong and it silted in the first wet month, and he dug it out
and packed the mouth with set stone and it held.

On the day he finished, nobody came. He walked up and down it twice and then went
in for his dinner.

Three days later Rell said, on her way past: — Your elbow is holding.

That was all, and he thought about it for a week.

---

## Fifty-Four

He found out about Rell in the second summer and it took him three weeks to
understand what he had found out.

He had been counting stone. The southern extension needed a run of set kerb and he
had walked the pile and done the sums and written it all down on a piece of slate
in his own hand — lengths, faces, how many carts — because that was what you did,
and he brought it to her at the end of the day rather pleased with himself.

Rell looked at the slate.

She looked at it for slightly too long, which he noticed and did not think about.
Then she handed it back and said: — Tell me.

— It is all on there.

— Tell me.

So he told her, and she listened, and asked him two questions that showed she had
the whole southern end in her head down to the yard, and said it was about right
and he had underestimated the wastage, and went off.

He thought she had been rude.

Three weeks later he was in the workshop at dusk and Ivo had a carter's docket on
the bench and was reading it out — not remarking on it, reading it, in the same low
voice he did everything in — and Rell was standing at the other end of the bench
with her arms folded looking at the wall while he did it. She said *yes* twice and
*that is wrong, it was seven* once. Then she went out.

Neither of them had looked at Steadfast at all.

He sat with it for a while.

---

She told him herself about a month after that, on the road, in the same tone she
used for drainage.

— You worked it out.

— I did.

— Ivo does the dockets and the letters when there are letters. My mother could and
did not teach me, and by the time I might have learned I had this — she put her
chin at the road — and there was never a winter I could spare.

— I could —

— No, said Rell.

And then, because she was fair: — Not because I do not want it. Because you would
be good at it and patient and I would be sixty before I could read a docket as
fast as Ivo reads it to me, and the road would be four miles shorter. That is the
whole arithmetic. I have done it more than once.

Steadfast said nothing, which was the correct thing and which he only just managed.

— It is all in my head, she said. — Every yard of it, and where the water goes,
and who laid what and in which year. That is not a boast. That is what I have
instead.

Then she said: — Your kerb run is six short, and went on down the line.

---

## Fifty-Five

The winter after that, the road failed.

Not his section. Four hundred yards north of the buildings, laid nine years
earlier, in the second year, when they had been doing forty yards at a time with
no camber and no bed under it because nobody had known any better yet. There was a
hard freeze and then a fast thaw and the whole of it lifted and broke and went to
mud, and a carter coming down with the winter grain went in to the axle and
snapped it, and they lost most of a load in the wet.

Steadfast expected an inquest. That was the shape his mind still made: somebody
had laid that badly, somebody would be answerable, there would be a reckoning of
some kind and probably a meeting.

What happened was that about thirty people went up there the next morning with
tools.

They took the whole four hundred yards out. They did it over eight days in the
cold, in shifts, and the old woman who had laid the original section — Bel, she was
seventy-something and did not work any more — came up to watch, and stood at the
edge of it the whole first day, and at one point Steadfast heard Rell say to her,
without any edge at all:

— We did not know about the bed.

— No.

— Nobody knew about the bed.

— I knew it was wrong when the water stood on it, said Bel. — I did not know why.

And Rell said: — Well. Now we know why.

And that was the entire reckoning, and they relaid it properly, and Bel came up
every day of the eight and no one suggested she should not.

On the sixth day she sat down on the kerb next to Steadfast while he was eating and
said, without introduction:

— We did it in August. It was August and it was dry and the ground was like a road
already and we thought we were finished.

— Yes.

— Everybody was very pleased, she said. — I was very pleased.

She looked down the line at thirty people taking it up.

— This is better, she said, and she did not say it sadly, which he had been braced
for. She said it the way you concede a point in a long argument with somebody you
like.

Steadfast worked the last three days of it and thought about a bulge in a wall
eight hundred miles away and did not know what to do with the thought, so he
carried stone.

---

## Fifty-Six

Sal asked him his name in the second summer, on the road, while he was eating his
bread.

She had asked him about the sea already, twice, and he had said no both times,
which had disappointed her enough that she had moved on to her other question.

— What are you called?

He had known this was coming for two years. He had not prepared anything, because
preparing something would have meant deciding, and he had not decided.

— I have not got one just now, he said.

Sal considered this with complete seriousness.

— Everybody has got one.

— Yes.

— So what happened to yours.

— I put it down.

— Where?

— A long way back.

She thought about that, sitting on the kerb with her heels against the set stone.

— Was it a bad one?

And he found that he had to think about it, and that thinking about it in front of
an eight-year-old was the first time he had looked at it straight.

— No, he said. — It was a good one. It was just not true.

— Oh, said Sal.

She swung her heels for a bit.

— Are you going to go and get it?

He did not answer that, and she did not notice, because she had seen somebody with
a dog further up the road and was gone.

He sat on the kerb for a long time after that with the rest of his bread in his
hand.

---

She did not tell anybody, or she told everybody and nobody minded, which in that
valley amounted to the same thing. What happened was that people went on calling
him nothing at all. If they needed him they came and found him. If they were
talking about him to somebody else, they said *the one on the elbow*, and after a
while they said *the elbow*, and it turned out that a man could be a place.

He noticed that Ivo never used it and never asked. Ivo said *you*, always, in a way
that had no gap in it.

---

## Fifty-Seven

The valley ate together on the first evening of every month, and it took him until
the third year to understand what that was.

It was not a feast. The food was the same food. What was different was that
everybody came — the whole forty-one, the babies, Bel, the two men who did not speak
to each other over something from before Steadfast arrived and who sat at opposite
ends and both came anyway — and the tables went end to end down the middle of the
long building so that it was one table.

That was all. One table, once a month, everybody at it.

The first year he sat at the end and could not eat much. The second year he found
himself at the middle and did not notice until afterwards.

The third year one of the two men stood up in the middle of it and went out.

There was no scene. He was not loud about it. He put his hands on the table and
stood up and walked the length of the room and out of the door, and the room
carried on, and a woman near the top reached over and moved his plate so it was not
in anybody's way.

Steadfast, who had by then spent six years learning not to ask, asked Ivo about it
afterwards.

— That will be the date, said Ivo.

— What date.

— It is about this time of year. It was a long while ago now.

And he went back to what he was doing, and that was the whole of the information,
and Steadfast never did find out — not in seven years — what had happened between
those two men, and neither of them ever missed a table.

That was the thing about the valley that took him longest. They did not fix it.
They did not sit the two of them down. Nobody made a project of it. They set the
tables end to end once a month and both men came and one of them sometimes left,
and the arrangement held for years because everybody had quietly agreed that it
was better than the alternative, which was one of them not coming.

---

## Fifty-Eight

Petch was the one who made him angry, which nobody had done in three years.

By then he was twenty-three and the size of a door, and had laid more of the
southern extension than anybody except Rell, and had taken to sitting at the same
end of the table as Steadfast for reasons neither of them examined.

It came out at the table one evening. Somebody had asked Steadfast something about
the crossing at the ford, which meant explaining what the ford had been, which
meant the road, and he was three sentences into it — carefully, without the
keeper's shape, having learned that much — when Petch said:

— Why does everyone from out there talk about it like a person who died.

The table went a bit quiet.

— Because it was a road, Petch said. — It was a good road, I have heard, it went a
long way and people used it and now it does not. And every single one of you comes
in here and sits down and cannot say four sentences without it. My mother came off
it before I was born and she still —

— That is enough, said Rell.

— It is not, though, said Petch. — There is a road *outside*. It goes to the well.
We built it. He built ninety yards of it and it took him half a year and the
culvert is good, and I have never once heard anybody from out there call that a
road without their voice doing a thing.

Steadfast, who had been about to say something conciliatory, discovered he was
furious.

— You have never lost one, he said.

— No.

— Then you do not know.

— No, said Petch, not backing off an inch. — I do not know. And you have never had
one that was yours. Everything you have ever walked on was made by somebody else
before you were born and you cannot even hear that you talk about it like an
inheritance. I laid two hundred yards of that with my own hands when I was
fifteen. You mourn the thing you were given. I am proud of the thing I made. Those
are not the same and I am tired of being told mine is smaller.

---

He was awake most of that night, which by then he knew the meaning of.

The boy was wrong. That was the first thing and it stayed true: he was
twenty-three and had never had the ground go out from under him and there was a
whole country of grief he had no idea existed, and he would have it one day and be
sorry for some of what he had said.

And he was right. That was the second thing, and it did not cancel the first, and
Steadfast lay in the dark and made himself hold both, which was a thing he had been
taught by a woman who held two kinds of not-knowing and had told him which one she
could carry.

He had walked the King's Road nineteen years and had never laid a stone of it. It
had been there. It had been made by people whose names nobody had ever told him,
and he had walked it and kept it and directed people onto it and had thought of
that as service, and in three years in this valley he had put down ninety yards of
bad ground and a culvert that took him two attempts, and he could not honestly say
which of those was the larger thing.

He apologised to Petch in the morning, badly, and Petch was mortified and said it
was fine about six times, and after that the two of them worked well together
for years.

---

## Fifty-Nine

Bel died in the fourth winter.

She was eighty or near it and she died the way you hope to, in her own bed with
two of her daughters there, over about three days, and there was nothing bad about
it except that it happened.

Steadfast had not known her well. He had eaten in the same room as her most
evenings for four years and had sat on a kerb with her once and had carried a load of broken stone
past her house every day for a season, and she had once told him his gloves were
no good, which was true.

They buried her at the top of the field, which was where people went.

What he remembered afterwards was not the burial.

It was that on the morning after she died, before it was properly light, he came
out to the yard because he was awake and there was somebody up at the north end of
the road, on the four hundred yards, walking.

It was Rell. She was going up the middle of it at about the pace you walk a thing
you are inspecting, from the bottom of the relaid section to the top, and when she
got to the top she stood for a bit, and then she came back down it, and then she
went in and got her tools because it was a working day.

Steadfast stood in the yard the whole time and did not go out to her and did not
say anything about it then or ever.

That was the only thing anybody did.

---

## Sixty

He started talking about the Waste in the fourth year, and it took him about six
months to get it out properly.

He did it badly at first. He tried, over a winter, to explain a woman in a garden
to people who had never been thirsty, and heard himself making her into a story,
and stopped for a month out of something like shame.

Then he tried again with the practical parts, which turned out to be the way in.
Eleven days of rain a year. A gutter of split wood repaired in four places with
four woods. A second barrel for when you have got the first one wrong. Beans up
canes in a country with no water and no soil worth the name, and a wall carried one
stone at a time from somewhere you cannot see.

Rell wanted to know about the wall and made him draw it twice.

Ivo wanted to know about the gutter and asked four questions Steadfast could not
answer and was annoyed about not being able to answer, and instead drew it in the
dirt three times over two evenings until he had something he liked better than
Grieve's.

Sal, who was ten by then, wanted to know whether the woman had ever seen the sea.

— I do not know, said Steadfast, and found that he minded very much not knowing.

And then one evening, at the end of it, he said the thing he had not planned to
say.

— She has been the only fire in that country for eleven years.

They waited.

— That is the whole of why she stays. There is nothing there that burns. If she
goes, in a year there is no smoke over that fold, and the next one who comes out
half dead walks past a mile east of it and never knows he walked past anything.

He was looking at the table.

— She counts the ones who found her. She had thirteen when I left. And what she
does instead of sleeping is think about the number she does not have.

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Petch, who was twenty-four by then and had
still never been anywhere, said:

— How much would a fire cost.

---

## Sixty-One

It took two years and it was nothing like what Steadfast had imagined.

What he had imagined, in the first hot week of it, was a chain — a line of fires
across the Waste, kept, at intervals, so that nobody crossed more than four days
without seeing smoke. He had it worked out on his second evening. He had the
spacing.

Ivo killed it in about a fortnight, and did it so gently that Steadfast did not
notice for a while that it was dead.

He did not argue. He asked questions. How much wood does a brazier eat in a night,
and Steadfast did not know, so they burned one for a night in the yard and
measured it. Then how much in a winter. Then what it weighs. Then how far is it
across, and Steadfast said fifteen days at the narrowest, and Ivo went quiet and
did some counting with a stub of chalk on the bench and said:

— You would need a cart in there every six weeks. For each one.

— We could —

— Show me a cart that crosses that ground, said Ivo, not unkindly.

Steadfast sat in the workshop with the chalk numbers in front of him for a long
time. He had wanted the chain very badly and had been carrying it around for a
fortnight like a warm thing in a pocket, and it had taken a man with a piece of
chalk about twenty minutes.

— The eastern edge is hard ground, he said eventually. — A cart could get within
two days of the eastern edge.

— That is one fire, said Ivo.

— That is one fire.

— Then that is what you have got, said Ivo, and went back to the loom, and that was
the end of the chain and the beginning of the thing they actually built.

---

Then Rell took it apart again, differently.

She came and found him at the elbow, which she did not do, and stood there and
said:

— A fire has to have somebody at it.

— Yes.

— So it is not a fire. It is a house. You are asking this valley to build and keep
a house eight hundred miles away and live in it in turns, for ever, and you have
been talking about it for four months as though it were a bonfire.

— I know that.

— You did not know it until about ten days ago, said Rell, which was true, — and
you still have not said it out loud to anybody, which is how a thing like this
kills itself. People find out in the second year what they agreed to in the first.

So he said it out loud, at the next table, badly.

And it went worse than he had feared and better than it deserved. Two households
said no at once and said why, which was money and time and the fact that neither
of them believed the woman was still alive. A man near the bottom said it was the
first sensible thing anybody had proposed in five years and then did not volunteer
for anything. And an argument started about the cache — whether you build a hut
with stores and nobody in it, or a hut with somebody in it — that ran, on and off,
for the best part of a year.

Rell won that one. Her argument was not moral. It was that a hut with nobody in it
would be robbed inside two winters and a hut with somebody in it would not, and
that if you were putting a year of somebody's life into it you might as well put
their hands to work while they were there. In the end it was both, which was
Steadfast's only contribution to the design and was probably wrong.

---

The second summer it stalled, and it stalled the way things do, which is that
nothing happened.

There were four households in it by then and one of them came out. The woman was
expecting and would not take a baby into the Waste, and her husband would not go
without her, and both of those were plainly right and nobody said a word against
either of them and the thing nearly died anyway, because three is not enough for
a rotation and everybody could count.

It sat for a season. Steadfast could not sleep for most of it and got very hard to
be around, and Ivo, who had not said anything about anybody's temper in four years,
said: — You want to go and find a fourth.

— Yes.

— Do not, said Ivo.

— Why.

— Because you will get one, said Ivo. — You are a persuasive man in a bad way when
you want something. And they will come out in the third year and you will have
spent a household to buy a year.

Steadfast did not go and find a fourth.

It came from a direction he had not been watching, which was one of the two men who
did not speak to each other. He put his hand up at a table in the autumn and said
he would take a year, and his wife said they had talked about it, and the other man
— the one he did not speak to — said, from the far end, that they should have the
long cart because it was better on rough ground.

That was all either of them said. It was the only time in seven years Steadfast
heard one of them address anything to the other, and it was about a cart, and
nobody at that table so much as looked up.

---

It went out east in the sixth year. Two carts, ten people, forty days.

He did not go with them.

He wanted to. He had wanted to for two years and had assumed the whole time that
he would, and when the day came he understood that if he went he would spend the
entire forty days steering it, and that it had stopped being his about eighteen
months earlier and everybody knew it except him.

He stood on the road and watched the carts up the north end and out past the well,
and Sal, who was twelve by then, stood next to him and said: — Are you sad?

— A bit.

— Why?

— Because I would have done it worse, he said, and she looked at him as though he
had made a joke, and he had not, and he did not explain.

---

## Sixty-Two

The carts came back in the spring with the hut built and the first household in it,
and with a piece of news that Steadfast had not thought to want.

There was already smoke out there.

Not hers — hers was four days further west and they had not gone that far. This was
another one, forty miles north of where they built, a man and two women who had put
up something like a shelter at a seam and had been feeding people out of it for
three years, and who had been astonished to see a cart, and who wanted to know
whether more were coming.

Steadfast sat down on the step of the long building when he heard that, and had to
put his hand over his mouth.

Not because it was a triumph. Because for six years he had been carrying, without
ever once looking at it directly, the belief that Grieve was alone out there in a
way that nothing could be done about — that it was one woman and eleven years and a
number she could not stop counting — and it had never occurred to him, not once,
that somebody else might have had the same idea in the same country in the same
years and neither of them would ever know.

He thought about the man who saw the smoke and did not stop. He thought about Ash,
and made himself stop thinking about Ash, which he had got better at and had never
got good at.

And then he thought: she does not know.

---

## Sixty-Three

He told Rell first, in the workshop, which is where things got decided in that
valley whatever anybody said about the table.

— I am going to go back.

Rell did not look up. — For how long.

— I do not know.

— That is not a plan.

— No.

She turned the thing she was holding over.

— Why.

And Steadfast found that he had been getting ready to answer that for two years
without knowing it, and that the answer had changed about four times, and that what
was left was short.

— She thinks she is the only one, he said. — She has thought it for twenty years.
She is not, and there is a hut with a fire on it four days east of her, and there
are three people forty miles north of that, and I know all of it and she knows none
of it, and I am the only person alive who has been in both places.

Rell put the thing down.

— That is a good reason, she said. — I have not heard one of those in a while.

— I am not going to bring her back.

— No.

— She will not come. I asked her once and I am not asking again.

— Good, said Rell. — Then you are going for the right thing, and if you were going
for the other one I would have said so.

Ivo, at the other end of the bench, had not looked up either.

— Your flask is no good, he said.

---

He did not go that year. He would not take the Waste with the cold in front of him
and there was nobody in the valley who thought he should, and so he had a winter of
being a man who has said out loud that he is leaving and is still there, which is
its own particular thing and which nobody enjoyed.

He went in the spring. He was six weeks getting ready and most of it was other
people's doing.

Ivo made him the flask, of a design he had been improving for four years, and was
rude about the old one twice more before he handed it over.

Petch, who had grown into a large calm man and had laid more of that road than
anybody except Rell, spent two days on the route with him and drew a stretch of it
in charcoal on the back of a board — the ground east of the pale stone, which Petch
had never seen and Steadfast had, so that in the end Steadfast was dictating and
Petch was drawing, and Petch marked the parts he was not sure of in a lighter hand
without being told to.

Steadfast looked at that for a while and did not explain it to anybody either.

Sal, who was thirteen and had a face like her mother's, asked him whether he was
going to go and get his name.

— I do not know, he said. — I think it might be waiting there.

— That is where you left it.

— Yes.

And Rell, on the evening before, put a folded slate into his hand with nothing
written on it.

— For when you get there, she said. — He can write. You said he mends and reads.

— That is Ivo.

— I know it is Ivo, said Rell. — There will be one of him there too. There is
always one. Get somebody to put down what she knows about that country before she
dies, because it is all in her head, and I have had a lifetime of knowing exactly what
that is worth and exactly what it costs.

Steadfast stood in the yard with the slate in his hand.

— I will, he said.

— See that you do, said Rell, and went in.

---

The valley did not make an occasion of his going, which he had known they would not
and was grateful for. He went out on the road at first light with his pack on, and
Rell was at the north end because she was always at the north end at first light,
and she said:

— Your elbow held six winters.

— It did.

— It will hold six more. Somebody will have to do the mouth again in about four
years and I will tell them you did it twice and got it wrong once, so they do not
think it is easy.

— Thank you.

— Go on, then, said Rell.

---

He walked up the made road, past the place where the sections change hands, past
Bel's four hundred yards that they had taken up and laid again, past Petch's two
hundred that a boy of fifteen had put down with his own hands, out to the north end
where it stopped at the well.

He stood at the end of it.

Then he stepped off the made ground onto the open, going east, the way Constant had
gone, and did not look back at the valley, because he had spent seven years there
and knew exactly what it looked like, and because looking back would have put him
wrong.
