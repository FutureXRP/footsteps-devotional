# Draft — eighth installment

---

## Forty-Five

Nobody asked him to stay and nobody asked him to go, and that was how he came to
be there seven years later.

The shovel was the whole of it. Somebody put it in his hands on the morning
Constant left and he went down to the southern end and worked until the light
went, and in the evening he ate in the long building with everyone else, and in
the morning somebody put a shovel in his hands again. That was the arrangement.
Nobody wrote it down. He asked once, in the first month, who he should speak to
about whether he could stay through the winter, and the man he asked — Ivo, the one
who had been mending a hinge on the first evening, and who turned out to mend most
things — looked genuinely puzzled and said: — Speak to about what?

It took Steadfast most of a season to stop expecting to be assessed.

That was harder than the work. He had spent nineteen years in a world where you
were placed — you were on the road or you were not, you were at a stage or you
were behind it, and there was always somebody whose business it was to know which.
Here there was nothing to be at. There was a road that needed four hundred yards
extending before the wet, and there were people doing it, and if you did it you
were doing it.

He was bad at this for a long time. In the second month he worked nineteen days
without stopping because some part of him believed he was still earning the food,
and on the nineteenth day Rell came down the line, looked at what he had laid, and
said:

— This is worse than yesterday.

— I have done twice as much as —

— I know what you have done. It is worse. Go and sit down.

He did not go and sit down. He worked another two hours and made it worse still,
and then went and sat down, and did not sleep well, and came back in the morning
and took out most of the previous day.

Rell did not mention it. She had been laying road for nine years and had run out of
interest in men who could not be told things.

---

## Forty-Six

There were forty-one of them, and by the end of the first winter he could have
told you most of what mattered about a dozen.

**Rell** ran the road, in the sense that nobody else did. She was perhaps fifty
and had come into the valley from the north as a girl with her mother, and had
been on the second forty yards, and had never in her life been on the King's Road
or wanted to be. She was not warm. She was exact and she was fair, and when she
told you a thing was wrong she told you once, and she never once told anybody the
same thing twice, which meant everybody listened the first time.

**Ivo** was older and mended. Hinges, wheels, the windlass at the well, a boy's
arm once when it was set badly. He talked while he worked in a low continuous way
that did not require answering, and Steadfast, who had spent two years with a
woman who did not talk and a year with a man who did not stop, found that he could
sit in Ivo's workshop for a whole afternoon and be perfectly comfortable and
afterwards not be able to say what had been discussed.

**Sal** was eight and belonged to everybody in the loose way children in small
places do. She had two questions she asked adults, in rotation, and the first was
whether they had ever seen the sea.

And there was **Petch**, who was nineteen and had been born in the valley and had
never been anywhere, and who was going to matter later.

---

He got his own section in the spring.

That was not a ceremony either. Rell walked the southern end with him and stopped
and said: — From that stake to the elbow. That is yours. Do not ask me about it
every day.

It was ninety yards of the worst ground on the extension, wet in three places and
crossed by a run-off that had to be got under the road rather than over it, and it
took him a hundred and sixty days.

He got the culvert wrong twice. The first time he laid it too shallow and had to
take up forty feet of finished bed to redo it, in the rain, alone, because it was
his section and everybody knew it was his section. The second time he got the fall
right and the mouth wrong and it silted in the first wet month, and he dug it out
and packed the mouth with set stone and it held.

On the day he finished, nobody came. He walked up and down it twice and then went
in for his dinner.

Three days later Rell said, on her way past: — Your elbow is holding.

That was all, and he thought about it for a week.

---

## Forty-Seven

The winter after that, the road failed.

Not his section. Four hundred yards north of the buildings, laid nine years
earlier, in the second year, when they had been doing forty yards at a time with
no camber and no bed under it because nobody had known any better yet. There was a
hard freeze and then a fast thaw and the whole of it lifted and broke and went to
mud, and a carter coming down with the winter grain went in to the axle and
snapped it, and they lost most of a load in the wet.

Steadfast expected an inquest. That was the shape his mind still made: somebody
had laid that badly, somebody would be answerable, there would be a reckoning of
some kind and probably a meeting.

What happened was that about thirty people went up there the next morning with
tools.

They took the whole four hundred yards out. They did it over nine days in the
cold, in shifts, and the old woman who had laid the original section — she was
seventy-something and did not work any more and came up to watch — stood at the
edge of it the whole first day, and at one point Steadfast heard Rell say to her,
without any edge at all:

— We did not know about the bed.

— No.

— Nobody knew about the bed.

— I knew it was wrong when the water stood on it, said the old woman. — I did not
know why.

And Rell said: — Well. Now we know why.

And that was the entire reckoning, and they relaid it properly, and the old
woman came up every day of the nine and no one suggested she should not.

Steadfast worked the last three days of it and thought about a bulge in a wall
eight hundred miles away and did not know what to do with the thought, so he
carried stone.

---

## Forty-Eight

Sal asked him his name in the second summer, on the road, while he was eating his
bread.

She had asked him about the sea already, twice, and he had said no both times,
which had disappointed her enough that she had moved on to her other question.

— What are you called?

He had known this was coming for two years. He had not prepared anything, because
preparing something would have meant deciding, and he had not decided.

— I have not got one just now, he said.

Sal considered this with complete seriousness.

— Everybody has got one.

— Yes.

— So what happened to yours.

— I put it down.

— Where?

— A long way back.

She thought about that, sitting on the kerb with her heels against the set stone.

— Was it a bad one?

And he found that he had to think about it, and that thinking about it in front of
an eight-year-old was the first time he had looked at it straight.

— No, he said. — It was a good one. It was just not true.

— Oh, said Sal.

She swung her heels for a bit.

— Are you going to go and get it?

He did not answer that, and she did not notice, because she had seen somebody with
a dog further up the road and was gone.

He sat on the kerb for a long time after that with the rest of his bread in his
hand.

---

She did not tell anybody, or she told everybody and nobody minded, which in that
valley amounted to the same thing. What happened was that people went on calling
him nothing at all. If they needed him they came and found him. If they were
talking about him to somebody else, they said *the one on the elbow*, and after a
while they said *the elbow*, and it turned out that a man could be a place.

He noticed that Ivo never used it and never asked. Ivo said *you*, always, in a
way that had no gap in it.

---

## Forty-Nine

Petch was the one who made him angry, which nobody had done in three years.

He was nineteen, born in the valley, and had never walked more than two days from
it, and he had a way of talking about the road — the King's Road, the real one, the
one nobody in the valley had ever used — that was not hostile exactly but was
utterly without patience.

It came out at the table one evening. Somebody had asked Steadfast something about
the crossing at the ford, which meant explaining what the ford had been, which
meant the road, and he was three sentences into it — carefully, without the
keeper's shape, having learned that much — when Petch said:

— Why does everyone from out there talk about it like a person who died.

The table went a bit quiet.

— Because it was a road, Petch said. — It was a good road, I have heard, it went a
long way and people used it and now it does not. And every single one of you comes
in here and sits down and cannot say four sentences without it. My mother came off
it before I was born and she still —

— That is enough, said Rell.

— It is not, though, said Petch. — There is a road *outside*. It goes to the
well. We built it. He built ninety yards of it and it took him half a year and the
culvert is good, and I have never once heard anybody from out there call that a
road without their voice doing a thing.

Steadfast, who had been about to say something conciliatory, discovered he was
furious.

— You have never lost one, he said.

— No.

— Then you do not know.

— No, said Petch, not backing off an inch. — I do not know. And you have never had
one that was yours. Everything you have ever walked on was made by somebody else
before you were born and you cannot even hear that you talk about it like an
inheritance. I laid two hundred yards of that with my own hands when I was
fifteen. You mourn the thing you were given. I am proud of the thing I made. Those
are not the same and I am tired of being told mine is smaller.

---

He was awake most of that night, which by then he knew the meaning of.

The boy was wrong. That was the first thing and it stayed true: he was nineteen
and had never had the ground go out from under him and there was a whole country
of grief he had no idea existed, and he would have it one day and be sorry for
some of what he had said.

And he was right. That was the second thing, and it did not cancel the first, and
Steadfast lay in the dark and made himself hold both, which was a thing he had
been taught by a woman who held two kinds of not-knowing and had told him which
one she could carry.

He had walked the King's Road nineteen years and had never laid a stone of it. It
had been there. It had been made by people whose names nobody had ever told him,
and he had walked it and kept it and directed people onto it and had thought of
that as service, and in three years in this valley he had put down ninety yards of
bad ground and a culvert that took him two attempts, and he could not honestly say
which of those was the larger thing.

He apologised to Petch in the morning, badly, and Petch was mortified and said it
was fine about eleven times, and after that the two of them worked well together
for years.

---

## Fifty

The valley ate together on the first evening of every month, and it took him
until the third year to understand what that was.

It was not a feast. The food was the same food. What was different was that
everybody came — the whole forty-one, the babies, the old woman who had laid the
bad section, the two men who did not speak to each other over something from
before Steadfast arrived and who sat at opposite ends and both came anyway — and
the tables went end to end down the middle of the long building so that it was one
table.

That was all. One table, once a month, everybody at it.

The first year he sat at the end and could not eat much. The second year he found
himself at the middle and did not notice until afterwards. The third year Rell
put him on the bread, which meant walking the length of it with the boards and
putting it down within reach of everybody, twice, and by the time he sat down his
own food was cold and he did not care at all.

He thought, that night, that he had spent two years in a garden with a woman who
fed every stranger who came over the fold and had never once been to a table with
more than three people at it.

---

## Fifty-One

He started talking about the Waste in the fourth year, and it took him about six
months to get it out properly.

He did it badly at first. He tried, over a winter, to explain a woman in a garden
to people who had never been thirsty, and heard himself making her into a story,
and stopped for a month out of something like shame.

Then he tried again with the practical parts, which turned out to be the way in.
Eleven days of rain a year. A gutter of split wood repaired in four places with
four woods. A second barrel for when you have got the first one wrong. Beans up
canes in a country with no water and no soil worth the name, and a wall carried
one stone at a time from somewhere you cannot see.

Rell wanted to know about the wall. Ivo wanted to know about the gutter and asked
four questions that Steadfast could not answer and was annoyed about not being
able to answer, and wrote to nobody about it because there was nobody to write to,
and instead drew it in the dirt three times over two evenings until he had
something he liked better than Grieve's.

And then one evening, at the end of it, he said the thing he had not planned to
say.

— She has been the only fire in that country for eleven years.

They waited.

— That is the whole of why she stays. There is nothing there that burns. If she
goes, in a year there is no smoke over that fold, and the next one who comes out
half dead walks past a mile east of it and never knows he walked past anything.

He was looking at the table.

— She counts the ones who found her. She has nine. And what she does instead of
sleeping is think about the number she does not have.

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Petch, who was twenty-three by then and had
still never been anywhere, said:

— How much would a fire cost.

---

## Fifty-Two

It took two years and it was nothing like what Steadfast had imagined.

What he had imagined, in the first hot week of it, was a chain — a line of fires
across the Waste, kept, at intervals, so that nobody crossed more than four days
without seeing smoke. He had it worked out on his second evening. He had the
spacing.

What actually happened was this.

Ivo said the wood was the whole problem and spent a season on it and concluded
that you could not carry enough and could not grow it there, and that anything
burning in that country would have to burn something brought in, and that the
carrying was the entire project and the fire was the easy part. That killed the
chain. Four fires, at Steadfast's spacing, would need a wagon every six weeks
across ground no wagon could cross.

So it became one fire, at the eastern edge, where the ground was hard enough to
bring a cart within two days of.

Then it became one fire and a cache, because Rell said a fire with nobody at it
was a fire for one night and a cache was a fire for a year, and they argued about
that for a month, and in the end it was both — a stone hut with water and dry wood
and food in a sealed pit, and a brazier on the roof of it that anybody staying
there kept lit.

Then it became the question of who stays, which nearly ended the whole thing,
because the answer was that somebody had to live there and nobody in the valley
wanted to live in the Waste, and Steadfast heard himself starting to say that he
would go, and heard Grieve saying *if you stay, stay because you have found
something here that is yours*, and stopped, and did not say it.

In the end four households said they would take it in turn, a year each, and one
of the two men who did not speak to the other put his hand up first, and that was
the closest thing to a miracle Steadfast saw in the valley.

It went out east in the sixth year. Two carts, nine people, forty days.

He did not go with them.

He wanted to. He had wanted to for two years and had assumed the whole time that
he would, and when the day came he understood that if he went he would spend the
entire forty days steering it, and that it had stopped being his about eighteen
months earlier and everybody knew it except him.

He stood on the road and watched the carts up the north end and out past the well,
and Sal, who was thirteen by then, stood next to him and said: — Are you sad?

— A bit.

— Why?

— Because I would have done it worse, he said, and she looked at him as though he
had made a joke, and he had not, and he did not explain.

---

## Fifty-Three

The carts came back in the spring with the hut built and the first household in
it, and with a piece of news that Steadfast had not thought to want.

There was already smoke out there.

Not hers — hers was four days further west and they had not gone that far. This
was another one, forty miles north of where they built, a man and two women who had
put up something like a shelter at a seam and had been feeding people out of it
for three years, and who had been astonished to see a cart, and who wanted to know
whether more were coming.

Steadfast sat down on the step of the long building when he heard that, and had
to put his hand over his mouth.

Not because it was a triumph. Because for six years he had been carrying, without
ever once looking at it directly, the belief that Grieve was alone out there in a
way that nothing could be done about — that it was one woman and eleven years and
a number she could not stop counting — and it had never occurred to him, not once,
that somebody else might have had the same idea in the same country in the same
years and neither of them would ever know.

He thought about the man who saw the smoke and did not stop. He thought about
Ash, and made himself stop thinking about Ash, which he had got better at and had
never got good at.

And then he thought: she does not know.

---

## Fifty-Four

He told Rell first, in the workshop, which is where things got decided in that
valley whatever anybody said about the table.

— I am going to go back.

Rell did not look up. — For how long.

— I do not know.

— That is not a plan.

— No.

She turned the thing she was holding over.

— Why.

And Steadfast found that he had been getting ready to answer that for two years
without knowing it, and that the answer had changed about four times, and that
what was left was short.

— She thinks she is the only one, he said. — She has thought it for twenty
years. She is not, and there is a hut with a fire on it four days east of her, and
there are three people forty miles north of that, and I know all of it and she
knows none of it, and I am the only person alive who has been in both places.

Rell put the thing down.

— That is a good reason, she said. — I have not heard one of those in a while.

— I am not going to bring her back.

— No.

— She will not come. I asked her once and I am not asking again.

— Good, said Rell. — Then you are going for the right thing, and if you were going
for the other one I would have said so.

---

He was six weeks getting ready and most of it was other people's doing.

Ivo made him a flask, unasked, of a design he had been improving for four years,
and was rude about the old one. Petch, who had grown into a large calm man and had
laid more of that road than anybody except Rell, spent two days on the route with
him and drew a stretch of it in charcoal on the back of a board — the ground east
of the pale stone, which Petch had never seen and Steadfast had, so that in the
end Steadfast was dictating and Petch was drawing, and Petch marked the parts he
was not sure of in a lighter hand without being told to.

Steadfast looked at that for a while and did not explain it to anybody either.

Sal, who was fourteen and had a face like her mother's, asked him whether he was
going to go and get his name.

— I do not know, he said. — I think it might be waiting there.

— That is where you left it.

— Yes.

The valley did not make an occasion of his going, which he had known they would
not and was grateful for. He went out on the road at first light with his pack on,
and Rell was at the north end because she was always at the north end at first
light, and she said:

— Your elbow held six winters.

— It did.

— It will hold six more. Somebody will have to do the mouth again in about four
years and I will tell them you did it twice and got it wrong once, so they do not
think it is easy.

— Thank you.

— Go on, then, said Rell.

---

He walked up the made road, past the place where the sections change hands, past
the old woman's four hundred yards that they had taken up and laid again, past
Petch's two hundred that a boy of fifteen had put down with his own hands, out to
the north end where it stopped at the well.

He stood at the end of it.

Then he stepped off the made ground onto the open, going east, the way Constant
had gone, and did not look back at the valley, because he had spent six years there and knew exactly what it
looked like, and because looking back would have put him wrong.
