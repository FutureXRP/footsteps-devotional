# Draft — fourth installment

## Nineteen

He read the ground for nine days.

He did not admit to himself that this was what he was doing until the fourth
morning, when he caught himself squatting at the lip of the third fold going over
a patch of scree with his eyes the way you go over a page, and stood up too fast,
and was dizzy, and had to put a hand out.

There was nothing. There would not be. Eight months of weather in a country where
weather was eleven days a year, and stone that took no print, and a girl who had
been quick — she had run the first three, everyone does — so that even on the day
she passed there would have been nothing to see.

He looked anyway. At the burnt scrub he looked at the scrub. At the fourth rise he
went down on his stomach under the lip and got his cup out of the seam, twenty
minutes of it, patient, and while he waited he looked at the ground either side of
his own boots for the mark of somebody else's.

On the ninth day he came up over a swell of nothing and found the line of pale
stone.

It was not a wall and it was not a road. It was a seam of some whiter rock running
out of the north and away south further than he could see, a hand's width proud of
the ground in places and buried in others, and he stood on it with a foot either
side and understood that he had been told about this and that being told about it
was the last thing he had.

*Cross it and stop looking behind you, because from there the ground reads
differently and looking behind you will put you wrong.*

He crossed it. He did not stop looking behind him, because she had not said he
would be able to, only that it would put him wrong, and it did.

---

## Twenty

He talked to her for about a fortnight. Not aloud at first. He would come to a
slope and think *she would go round that* and go round it, and be pleased, and
then catch the pleasure and find that there was nobody to be pleased at him. He
narrated the water to her. He explained his own decisions to her and she agreed
with them, which was how he knew he was making her up, because she had never
simply agreed with him about anything in two years.

Then it went aloud, and he heard himself, and stopped, and the stopping was worse
than the talking.

After that he did the work of walking and nothing else. He filled at seams. He
rationed the way she had taught him and caught himself, on the fourth day past
the pale stone, holding a flask
against the light to read the level the way she held a jar, at the same angle, with
the same tilt of the head, and stood there in the middle of a plain feeling like a
man wearing another man's coat.

He did not stop doing it. He could not think of a better way to hold a flask.

---

The ground did read differently.

He could not have said when it started. There was no morning where he stood in one
country and looked back at another. The scrub simply began to come in patches
instead of everywhere, and then the patches began to have other things in them,
low and grey-green and
tough, and one afternoon he went down on his knees to look at something because it
was flowering, badly, four small dirty flowers on a stem, the first flower he had
seen outside four rows of a garden in two years.

He did not know its name. He picked one and it came apart in his fingers.

Two days later he crossed a watercourse. It was dry. But it had been cut by
something, and cut deep, and it ran east to west across his path with a lip on both
sides, and he stood in the bottom of it looking at the strata and thought: this
runs for part of the year.

He got out the other side and walked on and it took him until evening to work out
what was wrong with him, which was that he had been walking since noon at the pace
of a man who expects to arrive somewhere.

---

## Twenty-One

The country went on being not quite the Waste for a long time before it was
anything else.

Nobody had told him about that, and there was no day on which anything had
improved enough to name. Water more often. Not water. Ground that took a print,
so that he could see where he had walked, which he had not been able to do for
two years and which he found he did not enjoy as much as he had expected.

He passed a thing on the tenth day out from the watercourse that he did not
understand.

It was in a wide shallow bowl of ground below him, and there were hundreds of
them — carts, wagons, some with the shafts still up and some with the wheels off
and stacked — and the whole floor of the bowl was covered in crates and boxes and
bundles set out in rows, hundreds of rows, with lanes between them. There were
people. They were moving between the rows with things in their arms, and stopping,
and setting things down, and picking other things up. Some of them were arguing. He
could hear it as a sound rather than as words, the particular rise and fall of
people who have been having the same argument for a long time.

It had been there a while. There were paths worn between the wagons and a smell of
smoke from more than one fire and washing strung on a line between two of the
larger carts.

He watched for most of an hour.

Nobody came up. Once a woman near the edge looked up in his direction and then
went back to what she was doing, the way you do when a shape on a ridge is not
your problem.

He did not go down. He could not have said why, except that he had no idea what
they were doing and did not want to be told badly, and there was some part of him
that had learned in a garden not to walk into somebody else's arrangement and
start improving it.

He went on north-west along the rim and lost sight of the bowl by evening.

---

## Twenty-Two

He was ill in the spring.

It was nothing dramatic. Bad water, probably, from a pool he had known was
doubtful and had drunk anyway because the seam before it had been dry. He was two
days on his side under an overhang with his pack for a pillow, and it went through
him, and there was one night in the middle of it where he was cold in a way that
frightened him.

Nobody came. That was the fact of it. He lay under a rock in a country with a name
he did not know and was ill and nobody came, and there was no smoke on any horizon,
and he understood — not as an idea, lying there, but in his body — what it had
meant that there had been a fire in the Waste for eleven years.

He thought: she is right. She should not come.

Then, in the same hour, because he was ill and honest: I would like her to come.

Both of those stayed with him after he got up.

---

He came out of it thin and slow and started walking again, and about a week later
he did a thing he did not plan.

He was up on high ground in the late afternoon and there was dead scrub in a
hollow, quite a lot of it, dry as paper. He had gathered wood without thinking
about it — it was a habit from a place where there was none — and it was in his
hand and there was a hollow full of tinder and he had flint.

He built a fire.

It was not cold enough to need one. He built it anyway, and got it going, and then
did the thing that made it something other than a fire: he built it up too big, and
put green stuff on it, and stood back and watched the smoke go up in a column
against a flat sky where anybody within a day's walk would see it.

Then he sat down beside it and waited to see if anyone came.

Nobody came. He kept it up until the middle of the night and slept beside the
embers and woke at first light and there was nobody, and he put it out properly,
scattering it and treading it, and stood there in the burnt patch feeling foolish
in a way he could not get rid of all morning.

He did the same thing eleven days later, in a different place, and again nobody
came, and he was less foolish about it the second time.

The third time somebody came.
