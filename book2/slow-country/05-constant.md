# Draft — fifth installment

---

## Thirty-Five

He came over the rise at a trot, which was wrong.

Steadfast had built the fire at midday and had been sitting beside it since,
feeding it green stuff, watching the column stand up in the still air, and he had
got as far as thinking about how many more of these he could afford in wood before
the country ran out of hollows. Then there was a man on the skyline coming fast
and downhill with his pack bouncing, and Steadfast stood up.

He was young. Twenty-five, perhaps. He arrived out of breath and put his hands on
his knees and got the words out between pulls.

— Are you all right?

Steadfast looked at him.

— What?

— The fire. Is it you? Are you hurt? Is there somebody down?

And Steadfast stood in front of his own signal with his mouth open and understood
what he had built. Not an invitation. A distress column. In a country where a man
lights a fire that size in daylight for one reason, he had been lighting them for
weeks and had thought of himself as a hearth.

— No, he said. — Nobody is hurt.

The young man straightened up. He looked at the fire, and at the green stuff
stacked ready beside it, and then at Steadfast, and something went across his face
that was not quite suspicion but was a long way from what Steadfast had been
expecting.

— Right, he said.

---

His name was Constant, which he gave without being asked, in the manner of a man
who likes his name.

He had water. That was the first thing Steadfast noticed and could not stop
noticing — two skins, both full, and he offered Steadfast a drink from one before
he had been offered anything, and Steadfast said no thank you and then wanted it
for the rest of the evening.

He had food. He had a good pack, well made, and a coat that had been mended by
somebody who could mend, and he had come twelve days from a place he named as though
Steadfast would know it, and when Steadfast did not know it he described it
cheerfully and at length.

He talked. That was the other thing. He talked the whole time he was setting his
things down and the whole time the pot was on and most of the time he was eating,
and none of it was empty — it was information, it was where the water was on the
north side of the pale stone and which of the hollows had wood in them and what
the weather did here in the autumn — and Steadfast, who had spent two years
learning to sit inside a silence until the other person filled it, found that this
one would not stop being filled.

He tried Grieve's methods anyway, because they were what he had.

He fed him first and asked him nothing, and Constant filled the silence with an
account of the pale stone that went on for a quarter of an hour, and thanked him
for the food, and did not seem to notice that he had not been asked a single
question.

He waited. Constant waited too, comfortably, and then started talking again.

At one point Steadfast said nothing for long enough that it became a thing in the
air, and Constant looked at him and said: — Are you all right? You keep doing
that.

---

## Thirty-Six

In the morning Constant showed him the map.

He got it out over the last of the fire, unrolled it on a flat stone with two
pebbles to hold the corners, and it was beautiful. That was the trouble with it.
It was inked in three colours on good hide, with the folds marked, and the seams
marked, and a scale, and a compass rose in the corner drawn by somebody who could
draw.

— There, said Constant, with his finger on it. — Thirteen days from here,
fourteen if the ground is soft.

Steadfast looked where the finger was.

It was the road.

It was drawn as a double line running north and south across the top third of the
map, confident, unbroken, with the lodging-houses marked along it as little
squares, and there was one square with a name he knew because he had slept in it
for nineteen years' worth of nights.

— You are going to the road, Steadfast said.

— I am going back to it. I came off it four years ago in the bad autumn, same as
everybody, and I have been eighteen months getting a straight answer out of anyone
about where it runs now, and this — he tapped the hide — this is from a man who
had it surveyed.

— Surveyed.

— He has people out. He sells them at a fair price, and before you say it, I know
what you are going to say, I have heard it — he did not sound impatient; he
sounded like a man who had won this argument before and was fond of winning it —
everyone says the road is gone. Everyone has always said that. The people who say
it are the people who stopped looking. He did not stop looking. He went and put a
line on paper.

Steadfast looked at the beautiful map for a while.

— I walked that road nineteen years, he said.

Constant's face came up, and it was the worst thing that had happened to Steadfast
in a year, because what was in it was delight.

— Then you know it, he said. — Then you can tell me — is that the run north of the
ford, there, does it bend like that, because he has it bending and I have had two
people tell me it runs straight —

And Steadfast opened his mouth to say: it does not bend and it does not run
straight, because it is not there, I stood in the doorway of that square you have
marked and there was grass where the road had been and it was old grass, and no
line drawn by any surveyor has been within a hundred miles of the truth.

And he did not say it.

He heard himself not saying it. He stood over a young man on his knees at a map
and did the thing he had watched done: he let the silence be where the answer
went, and told himself, in the exact words, that the boy had not asked him whether
the road was there. He had asked about a bend.

— It bends, Steadfast said.

---

## Thirty-Seven

It was true. It had bent north of the ford. It had bent that way for as long as
anybody had walked it, and the surveyor had got that one thing right, and Constant
was pleased and rolled the map up and put it away and slept well.

Steadfast did not sleep.

He went over it in the dark and found that he could construct it perfectly. Grieve
would not have told him. Grieve had stood in her own yard and let a girl walk east
into nothing with a bundle and a flask because the girl had not asked her what was
out there, and when Steadfast had screamed at her about it she had not defended it
as a principle, she had said she had chosen which not-knowing she could carry.

So he had done what she did.

Except that Grieve had known what was east — she had known it as an absence, a
country with nothing in it — and she had told Ash the moment Ash asked. She had
answered the question that was put to her, fully, in about a minute, and had not
put a hand out to stop her.

He had been asked a question and had answered a different one.

He lay in the dark and got no further with it than that, and it was enough.

---

In the morning he tried.

— The map, he said, while Constant was strapping his pack. — The man who made it.
Did you meet him.

— I did. Good man. Not what you would expect, either — no fuss about him, he did
not oversell it, he said outright there were parts he was not sure of and he
marked them lighter, look — and he had the map out again, and there they were,
three stretches inked in a paler hand, honest as anything.

— And the road itself. Did he say he had seen it.

Constant paused, and for a moment Steadfast thought it had gone in.

— He said his people had.

— Not him.

— His people. That is what surveyors are, he said, and laughed, and went back to
his straps.

Steadfast tried once more, and made a mess of it.

— When you get there. If it is not — if the country has —

— If it is not there, said Constant, standing up, and he said it kindly, which was
the unbearable part. — I know. I do know. Everybody I have met in eighteen months
has managed to say some version of that to me, usually about four sentences in, and
you have lasted a whole night, which I am grateful for. If it is not there I will
have walked thirteen days and I will know, and I would rather know than sit in a
hollow with a fire wondering.

He put his hand out. Steadfast shook it.

— Thank you for the food, Constant said. — And for the bend.

---

## Thirty-Eight

He watched him go up the rise and over.

He stood by the dead fire and watched him go, and told himself the things: that the boy had water, that the boy had food, that the boy had a better
pack than his own and knew the hollows on the north side; that a man of twenty-five
who has been eighteen months getting an answer is entitled to go and get one; that
he had not lied about the bend; that Grieve would have let him go.

That last one held for about an hour.

Then it stopped holding, because he could hear how it worked — *Grieve would have
let him go* — and it was not the same shape as anything Grieve had ever said. She
had never once told him what to do by telling him what she would do. She had told
him to read the person in front of him and then had refused, at cost, to be the
reason for anything.

And the person in front of him had not been half dead and had not needed feeding
and had not needed to be left alone in a silence to arrive at his own conclusion.
He had needed one sentence from the only man in the country who had stood in that
doorway, and he had gone off carrying a beautiful map and a bend and a piece of
plain courtesy, and Steadfast had given him the courtesy because it cost the
least.

He got his pack. He did not decide to, particularly. He rolled the blanket and
kicked the burnt patch flat out of habit and was up on the rise inside a quarter
of an hour, and then he was running, badly, which at his age and after a winter of
bad water was a thing to see, and he lost the trail twice and found it because the
ground here took a print, and he was two hours catching him.

Constant heard him coming and turned round, and his hand went to his pack strap,
and the look on his face was pure alarm — a big man labouring up behind you in
empty country is not good news whoever he turns out to be — and Steadfast had to
stop and get his breath before he could do anything about it.

— It is gone, he said.

Constant stared at him.

— What?

— The road. It is not there. I did not walk off it four years ago, I came out of
the lodging one morning and it was gone, and there was grass where it had been and
the grass was old. That square on your map with the name on it. I slept in that
house nineteen years. I stood in that doorway and there was nothing.

Constant did not say anything.

— It bends north of the ford, Steadfast said. — That part is true and I told you
the true part because it was the part that cost me nothing, and I have been up all
night about it, and you can do what you like with the thirteen days but you are not
going to spend them not knowing what I know.

He ran out. There had not been much of it and he had used it all going up the
hill.

Constant looked at him for a long moment. Then he took his pack off, slowly, and
sat down on it in the middle of the open ground, and put his hands over his face,
and stayed like that.

Steadfast stood six feet away.

He stood there long enough to feel it — the distance, the rightness of it, the
whole clean architecture of leaving a man to arrive at his own grief without being
crowded — and then he crossed it and sat down on the ground next to him, close
enough that their shoulders were nearly touching, which was not something he had
ever seen anybody do.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

— Four years, Constant said, into his hands.

— I know.

— No, but four *years*.

— I know, Steadfast said.

And that was all he had, and it turned out to be the thing that was wanted, and
they sat there on the open ground in the middle of the morning with the map still
rolled up in the pack under him.
