# Chapter Twenty-Five

## Planted to Flourish

Two trees, the same drought year, and only one of them seems to know it.

The first browns and thins, dropping leaves early, fighting to hold what it has. It
may survive. It will not do anything more than survive. The second stands heavy and
green with fruit on the branches as though the dry season were happening somewhere
else entirely, and the reason has nothing to do with the tree. Its roots have found
water the drought cannot reach.

The psalmist sings about trees like the second one and is explicit about the cause:
they are planted in the house of the LORD; they will flourish in the courts of our
God. And then the detail that turns it from a nice image into a promise — they will
still produce fruit in old age. They will be full of sap and green.

He does not say they flourish because they are hardy. He says they flourish because
of where they are standing.

Jeremiah has the same picture and adds the mechanism. Blessed is the man who trusts
in the LORD: he will be as a tree planted by the waters, who spreads out its roots
by the river, and will not fear when heat comes, but its leaf will be green. In the
year of drought it will not be anxious, and will not cease from yielding fruit.

He will not fear *when* heat comes. Not if. The drought is assumed. What is promised
is not exemption from the dry year but a root system that reaches past it.

---

This is what Reconnect actually settles into, and it is worth distinguishing from
what people expect.

The expectation, after a long upheaval, is restoration to a previous condition — the
return of what was lost, life resumed at the point it was interrupted. That is not
what is on offer and it is not what happens.

What happens is that a person ends up rooted somewhere different. Not stronger,
exactly; the language of strength suggests something achieved. Rooted. Drawing from
a source that was not being drawn from before, mostly because it was not needed
before, because there was surface water and the surface water was enough.

The drought did not create the stream. It made you go down to it.

---

Paul's version of this is the one to keep, because he prays it for people rather
than describing it.

He asks that Christ may dwell in their hearts through faith, so that they, being
rooted and grounded in love, may be strengthened to comprehend with all the saints
what is the width and length and height and depth, and to know the love of Christ
which surpasses knowledge.

Two metaphors jammed together — rooted, which is agricultural, and grounded, which
is architectural, a foundation. He wants both: the living thing that draws, and the
built thing that bears weight. And what they are rooted and grounded in is not
doctrine, or practice, or a community, but love.

Then the deliberate absurdity: that you may know the love of Christ which surpasses
knowledge. He is asking for people to know something he has just said cannot be
known. It is not sloppy writing. It is the distinction between knowing about and
knowing, and the whole of this phase lives in the gap between them. You knew about
the love of God before your upheaval. What this phase offers is the other kind of
knowing, and it is generally only available to people who have run out of everything
else.

---

And then there is joy, which has to be handled carefully in a book like this.

David sings: you have turned my mourning into dancing for me. You have removed my
sackcloth and clothed me with gladness.

Hold the verb. He does not say God erased the mourning, or that it turned out not to
have been worth mourning, or that he now understands why it happened. He says it was
*turned* — the same material, reoriented. And the image is a change of clothes: the
rough mourning garment worn next to the skin through a long grief is taken off, and
something festival is put on.

The clothes come off. The body that wore them is the same body, and it remembers.

Which is why the resurrection appearances matter so much here. He came back with the
wounds. He showed them to Thomas and invited him to put his hand into the side. The
risen body is not a body from which the crucifixion has been edited; it is the same
body, glorified, still marked. If that is true of Him, then the expectation that
your restoration will involve the erasure of what you went through is not just
unrealistic — it is a lower thing than what is actually promised.

Joy after grief is not the same as joy before it. It is deeper and it is quieter and
it has room in it for other people's sorrow, which the earlier kind never did.

## In You

Two questions, and the first is about the root system.

Where are your roots actually going? Not what you believe — what you draw on. When
the pressure comes at four in the afternoon on a hard day, what do you reach for?
Most people can answer this instantly if they are honest, and for many the answer is
distraction, or work, or the approval of a particular person, or the numbing thing.

The psalm's claim is that flourishing is a matter of location rather than effort.
You cannot try harder to be green. You can only be planted somewhere the water is,
and then let time do what time does. Which in practice is unglamorous: the same
practices, kept for years, in the same direction. Roots are not a project. They are
the accumulated result of staying somewhere.

The second question is about the drought, and it is more hopeful than it sounds.

The dry years will come again. That is in both texts, stated plainly, and any
account of the far side of upheaval that promises otherwise is lying to you. You are
not being handed a life without further shaking; you have been handed a root system
that reaches past the surface, and the next drought will test whether the roots
actually went where you think they went.

And be patient about fruit. The psalm's promise is about old age — still bearing
fruit when they are old, full of sap and green. That is a long-horizon statement,
and it should relieve some pressure. You do not have to be visibly fruitful in the
first year after your wilderness. Trees that have been through drought put their
energy into roots before they put it into fruit, and that is not a failure of the
tree; it is good management.

Finally: expect joy to be different. If you are waiting to feel the way you felt at
twenty-two, you will wait forever and miss what is actually being given, which is
better and does not announce itself as loudly. Gladness put on over a body that
remembers the sackcloth is a more durable garment than gladness that has never
needed to be given.

## In the Body

The Body's version of this is the difference between churches that survive their
shaking and churches that flourish through it, and the distinguishing factor is
almost always depth of root rather than strength of structure.

The evidence is unusually clear here. The Christian communities that have come
through the twentieth and twenty-first centuries' worst pressure — the Chinese
church through the Cultural Revolution, the Russian church through seventy years of
official atheism, the Ethiopian church, the churches of the Middle East through
wars and emptying — were not structurally strong. Most of them lost their buildings,
their institutions, their clergy and their legal existence. What they had was
Scripture memorised, prayer that did not require premises, and households that kept
the faith without professional support.

That is a root system. And it is precisely the thing the Western Church allowed to
atrophy during the decades of surface water, because when the buildings were full
and the culture was friendly, depth was optional and programme was measurable.

So the question for the Body now is not whether the institutions will survive. Some
will and many will not, and the historical record suggests that is not the decisive
variable. The question is whether ordinary Christians in the West have anything to
draw on when the surface water goes — whether they can pray without a service, know
Scripture without a screen, hold a household in faith without institutional support,
and endure the loss of social respectability without losing the thing itself.

Where that is being built, the Church will be green in the drought. Where it is not,
no amount of structural reinforcement will help, because the problem was never the
structure.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

The stream.

Not the tree, which is mortal and can be felled. Not the season, which turns. What
holds is the source the roots found — the house of the LORD, the river the psalmist
names, a supply that does not fail when every surface water in the country has dried
to cracked mud.

That is the specific unshakable thing in this chapter, and it is worth naming
precisely because it is so easily confused with the tree's own resilience. You are
not being promised that you will become strong enough for the next drought. You are
being told where the water is.

A tree planted there does not merely survive the heat. It flourishes through it,
green and fruitful, because what feeds it lies deeper than anything the dry year can
reach.

## For Reflection

1. When the pressure comes at four in the afternoon, what do you actually reach for?
2. What practice have you kept long enough for it to have become a root rather than
   an effort? What would it take to have three of those?
3. Where are you still waiting to feel the way you felt before, instead of receiving
   the joy that is actually on offer?
