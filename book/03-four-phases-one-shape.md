# Chapter Three

## Four Phases, One Shape

Forty years later, standing on a ridge on the wrong side of the Jordan, an old man
tries to explain to a generation that was not born yet what the last four decades
were about.

Moses is at the end. The people in front of him are the children of the people who
came out of Egypt; almost none of the adults who crossed the sea are still alive.
And the thing he most wants them to understand is not the law, which he will get
to, but the *route*. Remember all the way which the LORD your God has led you these
forty years in the wilderness, he says. It was to humble you, to test you, to know
what was in your heart.

He is doing something the whole generation could not do while it was happening. He
is reading the map backwards. From inside, those forty years were a mess — a series
of unrelated crises, thirst and hunger and snakes and rebellion and the endless
grinding sameness of a place that goes nowhere. From the ridge, it has a shape. It
went from somewhere, through somewhere, to somewhere.

That is what a map is for. It cannot shorten the journey. It can tell you that the
featureless middle is a place on the route and not the end of the road.

---

Here is the map this book uses. Four phases. They are not original to me; they are
what almost everyone who has looked closely at upheaval keeps finding.

The Old Testament scholar Walter Brueggemann, reading the Psalms, found that they
sort into three broad postures — settled orientation, the collapse into
disorientation, and the surprising arrival of a new orientation on the far side.
The management writer William Bridges, studying organisational change, described
every real transition as an ending, then a disorienting neutral zone, then a new
beginning — and argued that the ending comes first, always, which is why change
programmes that start with the new thing fail. Both are describing, in their own
vocabularies, the pattern Scripture narrates constantly.

I have split it into four, because the break and the wilderness are different
enough to need separate names, and because the return of communion at the end is
too important to fold into the arrival of new bearings.

### Disconnect — the break

The old is left behind. Something snaps or is cut. Abram walks out of Ur at
seventy-five. Elisha slaughters his oxen and burns the plough — no way back to
farming now. Fishermen leave nets in the boat. Luther nails a list to a door.

The break is loud, fast, and often looks like the whole story. It is not. It is the
first quarter.

### Disorientation — the wilderness in between

The old map is gone and no new one has arrived. This is the longest phase and the
one nobody warns you about. It is Israel in the trackless waste. It is Babylon,
where a people who had a temple, a king and a land now had none of the three and
had to work out whether their God had come with them. It is Job cycling through
every argument he has. It is the dark night the mystics wrote about, where prayer
goes dry and the presence you had built a life on simply is not there.

Here the question arrives: *where now is the authority?* Here is where most people
quit, in both directions — some by going back, some by burning it all down.

### Reorientation — new bearings

Slowly, something truer takes shape. Not the old thing restored; a new thing
formed. Israel at Sinai receives an identity, not just a rescue. The exiles come
home and rebuild with a different understanding of what a temple was ever for. The
disciples, after the resurrection, have to learn to read every text they ever knew
in a new light.

Reorientation is not relief. It is reconstruction, and it is slow, and it usually
begins so quietly you miss it — a voice behind you saying *this is the way, walk in
it*, when you had been listening for thunder.

### Reconnect — re-rooted

Communion restored. The new thing is not just understood but inhabited, and you are
knit back into a body. Pentecost. The table. Peter, who denied Christ by a fire,
asked three times by another fire whether he loves Him, and put back to work.

The last phase is not the absence of scars. It is the discovery that the scars are
now part of what you have to give.

---

Six things to say about the map before you use it.

**It is a map, not a doctrine.** This matters enough to say plainly. Scripture
narrates these movements constantly — it is full of people who broke, wandered,
were re-formed and were brought home — but it nowhere legislates a four-stage
sequence, and nothing in the faith depends on this scheme being right. It is a
pastoral instrument, assembled from patterns other people noticed, offered because
it helps. If a chapter of it does not fit your life, your life is not the thing in
error.

**It is not a schedule.** Nobody moves through these in clean sequence at a
predictable rate. You will be reoriented in one area of your life while still
bleeding from a disconnect in another. You will think you have reached the fourth
phase and find yourself back in the second by Thursday. The phases are terrain, not
timetable.

**The phases can be counterfeited.** Every one of them has a shortcut that looks
identical from outside and leads nowhere. A break that is really just avoidance.
A wilderness that is really self-pity, being cultivated. A reorientation that is
really a new certainty adopted quickly to stop the pain. A reconnection that is
really a return to the old thing with nothing changed. Most failed upheavals fail
at one of those four doors.

**The order is not negotiable.** This is Bridges' insistent point and it is worth
the whole book: you cannot skip to the new beginning. The reason so much church
renewal fails, and so much personal reinvention fails, is that it begins at phase
three. There is no reorientation that has not been through disorientation. There is
no resurrection that skips Saturday.

**Not everyone reaches the fourth phase in this life.** I would rather say this now
than let you discover it as a private failure in year six. Some people live most of
their lives in Disorientation. Some upheavals do not resolve; some losses are not
restored on this side; some questions are carried to the grave unanswered, by
faithful people who prayed. Naomi got a grandson, and Job got a second family, and
those are in the book — and so is a psalm that ends in the dark with no turn at
all, and so is a prophet who died in Egypt against his will. If you are not
progressing, you are not doing this wrong. The phases describe a shape that
upheavals tend to have, not a debt God owes you within your lifetime.

**The map is not the point.** The map is a mercy for the middle. You will not need
it in the first hours of a break, and you will not need it once you are on the far
side. You will need it at three in the morning in the fourteenth month, when you
have lost all sense of whether anything is happening at all, and someone can hand
you a diagram and say: this. You are here. This is a place on the route.

## In You

Find yourself on it. Genuinely — put the book down for a moment and locate
yourself, because almost all the useless advice you have received in the last year
was advice for a different phase.

If you are in **Disconnect**, your work is to let the break be a real break, and to
count the cost honestly rather than either minimising it or dramatising it.

If you are in **Disorientation**, your work is to stay. Not to resolve it. To stay,
to keep the practices going even when they feel like nothing, to refuse both the
road back to Egypt and the temptation to force a premature answer. Most of what
this phase asks is simply endurance and honesty, and it will feel like doing
nothing, and it is not.

If you are in **Reorientation**, your work is patience with slowness and rigour
about foundations. The new thing is being built and you are going to want it
finished. Build it to bear weight.

If you are in **Reconnect**, your work is risk. You have to walk back into rooms
with people, having been changed, and let yourself be known in the new shape. Many
people who survive the wilderness beautifully stall here, because solitude has
become safer than communion.

And if you cannot tell which one you are in — that is itself information. That fog
is the signature of phase two, and if you are in phase two, the most important
sentence in this book is that it is a place on the route and not the end of the
road.

## In the Body

The map works at scale, and it clarifies why the Church argues with itself the way
it currently does.

Much of the anger inside Western Christianity right now is a phase disagreement
misread as a doctrinal one. A movement standing in Disconnect — convinced that
something must be broken with, and loudly breaking — will look to a movement
standing in Reconnect like faithless destruction. A body deep in Disorientation,
unable to answer basic questions it could answer in 1970, looks to a confident
outsider like a failure of nerve, when it may simply be honest about where it is. A
tradition that has skipped to Reorientation without doing the grief work of the
wilderness produces exactly what you would expect: brittle new certainties, held
loudly, that snap under the first real pressure.

There is also a genuine danger in the Church using this map badly. Institutions
love the language of transition because it makes decline sound like a plan. A
denomination that is simply dying can describe itself as being in the neutral zone
indefinitely, and a leadership that will not repent can call its refusal a
wilderness season. The map is a diagnostic, not an alibi. The test is whether
anything is actually being sifted, or whether the same things are being protected
under a new vocabulary.

Used honestly, though, the map does for the Church what Moses did on the ridge. It
says: this has a shape. The last sixty years were not sixty unrelated
catastrophes. The faith went from somewhere, is going through somewhere, and is
going to somewhere — and the wandering is not the destination even when it is all
the current generation will see.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Moses on the ridge is not remembering a route. He is remembering a companion. Every
verb in the sentence has the same subject: the LORD your God led you, humbled you,
fed you, kept your clothes from wearing out, brought you.

That is the one constant across all four phases, and it is why the map is worth
anything at all. What holds is not your ability to identify which phase you are in.
It is that the same God is present in every one of them — in the break, in the
trackless middle, in the slow rebuild, at the table — and He was the one thing
carried forward from each phase into the next.

He who began a good work in you will complete it. The map has a mapmaker, and He
has walked the whole route already.

## For Reflection

1. Which phase are you in? If you cannot tell, what does that suggest?
2. Think of advice you have been given recently that did not land. Was it good
   advice for a phase you are not in?
3. Where have you tried to jump straight to a new beginning without letting
   something end?
