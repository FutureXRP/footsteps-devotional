# Chapter Twenty-Six

## Sent Back With the Scar

He wants to get in the boat, and he is not allowed.

The man had been living among the tombs on the far side of the lake, in a condition
the account describes with unusual clinical detail: night and day among the graves,
crying out, cutting himself with stones, so violent that chains could not hold him.
Nobody had been able to help. The community's solution had been restraint, and the
restraint had failed, and so he had been left to the tombs.

And then Jesus crosses the lake, and the man is delivered, and the townspeople come
out and find him sitting, clothed, and in his right mind — and they are afraid, and
they ask Jesus to leave. Which He does.

As He is getting into the boat, the man begs to go with Him.

It is the most understandable request in the Gospels. The town is where the tombs
are. The people asking Jesus to leave are the people who chained him. Everyone in
that place knows what he was, and there is no version of staying that does not
involve being the man who used to live in the graveyard.

And Jesus says no.

Go to your house, to your friends, and tell them what great things the Lord has done
for you, and how He had mercy on you.

Not: come and be trained. Not: come away where it is safe. Go home. Go back to the
exact place, and to the people who watched, and tell them.

The man went and began to proclaim it in the Decapolis — the ten cities — and
everybody marvelled.

---

That is the last movement of the map, and it is the one that gives the whole thing a
purpose beyond your own survival.

Reconnect is not finished when you are re-rooted. It is finished when what happened
to you becomes available to somebody else, and the New Testament is remarkably
direct about the mechanism.

Paul, opening the most personal of his letters, having just been through something in
Asia so severe that he says he despaired even of life, describes what the comfort was
for. God comforts us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to comfort those
who are in any affliction, through the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted
by God.

It is a plain economy. What you received in the wilderness, you received in a
quantity larger than your own need, and the excess is for other people. Not
eventually, when you have fully recovered. The verb is present.

Which reframes the worst years of a person's life in a way nothing else does. You
learned a set of things in the dark that cannot be learned in daylight — how to sit
with someone without fixing them, what not to say, how long it actually takes, what
it is like to pray without any evidence, how to hear the sentence *I do not know if I
believe any of this any more* without flinching. Those competences are rare and they
are expensive and you did not want them, and there are people in your life who need
exactly one person who has them.

---

Two things about the scar, because it is the load-bearing image here.

**It is a scar, not a wound.** There is an enormous difference and the difference is
time and healing. A person who is still bleeding cannot minister out of it; they will
end up needing the person they are trying to help, and that is a particular kind of
harm that the caring professions have a whole vocabulary for. Paul writes his letter
about comfort after the affliction, not during. Peter is commissioned on the beach
after the resurrection, not in the courtyard. If your experience is still an open
wound, the right thing this year is to be comforted, not to comfort. That is not
selfishness; it is sequence.

**And it remains visible.** The risen Christ kept His wounds. That is the deepest
warrant for everything in this chapter — He did not come back unmarked, and when He
wanted to convince Thomas He did it by showing the marks. Your restoration will not
involve the erasure of your history, and it is not supposed to, because the marks
are how anyone knows it was really you and that the thing really happened.

---

What does the sending actually look like? Almost always smaller and closer than
people imagine.

The man was sent to his own house and his own friends, in his own ten towns. Not to
a platform. The commission at the end of Matthew is famously global — go and make
disciples of all nations — and the movement it launched has reached every country on
earth, and it began with eleven men who mostly worked locally.

And the New Testament's descriptions of what this looks like day to day are
consistently domestic: bear one another's burdens. Encourage one another. Confess
your sins to one another and pray for one another that you may be healed. Show
hospitality, without grumbling. Look after orphans and widows in their distress.
Visit the prisoner. Feed the hungry — and note that in the parable, the people
commended for doing it are surprised, because they had not experienced it as ministry
at all. When did we see you hungry? They had just been feeding people.

That is the shape of it. Not a role. A set of ordinary acts, performed by someone who
now has capacity they did not have before.

## In You

Someone in your life is where you were.

That is almost certainly true, and it is the practical question this chapter leaves
you with. Not a ministry, not a calling in the large sense — one person, currently in
a phase you have already walked through, who does not have anyone who has been
there.

You are unusually qualified for them and you are probably not going to volunteer,
for two reasons. The first is that you do not feel recovered enough, which is worth
examining honestly against the scar-not-wound test rather than assumed. The second is
that reaching toward someone in the middle of what nearly destroyed you means going
back to the tombs, and everything in you wants to get in the boat.

Go anyway, if the wound has closed. And go with the specific gift the wilderness gave
you, which is not answers. You do not have answers; you have something better and
rarer, which is the ability to be with someone in a place where there are no answers
without needing them to get out of it for your own comfort. Almost nobody can do
that. Everyone who has been through a long disorientation can.

Two practical constraints. Do not tell them your story as a template — the phases are
terrain, not timetable, and their route will not be yours. And do not need them to
recover. The moment your own equilibrium depends on their improvement, you have
started using them, and it will show.

The instruction to the man was to tell what had been done for him and how he had been
shown mercy. That is the whole brief. Report, do not prescribe.

## In the Body

The Body's sending, in this moment, has a particular and uncomfortable shape.

For most of the modern era the Western Church understood mission as something it did
outward and downward — from the centre to the periphery, from the strong to the
needy. That era is over, and the numbers ended it. The churches with the most vitality
in world Christianity are largely in Africa, Asia and Latin America, and they are
sending missionaries to Europe and North America, where they mostly find that the
historic churches do not quite know what to do with them.

That reversal is the Body's version of being sent back with the scar, and it will
require a humility that Western Christianity has not had to exercise before: to be
the mission field, to be taught, to receive from Christians whose grandparents were
converted by Western missionaries and who now understand things about suffering,
prayer, spiritual power and holding faith without cultural support that the West
comprehensively forgot.

And there is a second sending, closer to home. A Church that has been through the
last fifty years — that has lost its position, been publicly humiliated by its own
scandals, and had every institutional certainty shaken — is, if it can bear it, in a
better position to speak to a culture full of people who have also lost their
footing than it was when it was confident and respectable. A Body that has been
humbled has something to offer that a Body at the centre of things never did.

The condition is the same as the personal one: it has to be a scar and not an open
wound. A Church still bleeding from its own failures and still defending them is in
no position to minister to anyone. A Church that has actually repented, actually
made restitution where it can, and can speak about its own failures without
flinching, has a credibility that no amount of institutional strength ever purchased.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

He kept the wounds.

Everything else about the resurrection body is beyond our understanding — it passes
through doors, it is not always recognised, it eats fish. The one continuity the
accounts insist on is the marks. He came back still carrying what was done to Him,
and He used them as evidence.

What cannot be shaken is that nothing you went through is wasted. Not redeemed in
the sense that it was secretly good — much of it was not good, and this book has
refused that lie throughout. Redeemed in the sense that it has been taken up,
carried into the new life rather than left behind, and made useful in a way that
neither you nor anyone who hurt you intended.

The tombs are still there. The man went back and told them, and ten cities heard
about it, and nobody remembers his name.

## For Reflection

1. Who in your life is where you were? Name them.
2. Is your experience a scar or still an open wound? Answer honestly — the sequence
   matters more than the willingness.
3. What did the wilderness teach you that cannot be learned any other way? Who needs
   exactly that?
