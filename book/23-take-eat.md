# Chapter Twenty-Three

## Take, Eat

It is the last night, and everything is about to come apart, and He knows it.

Within hours one of the men at this table will sell Him for money. Another, the
confident one, will deny three times that he ever knew Him. The rest will run into
the dark. He is aware of all of it as He sits down — the accounts are explicit that
He knew — and He does not confront them with it or postpone the meal until they can
be trusted.

He takes bread. He gives thanks. He breaks it and puts it into their hands.

Take, eat. This is my body, which is broken for you. Do this in memory of me.

Then the cup, passed hand to hand around a table that is hours away from being
emptied by a cross, including into the hands of the man who has already made
arrangements.

There is something deliberate in what He chose to leave them. Not a doctrine. Not a
constitution or a rule of order or a defensible set of positions. A meal, and an
instruction to keep eating it, and the memory attached.

---

Reconnection needs something physical, and this is it.

Everything in the previous phase happened largely in the mind — sorting foundations,
recovering identity, correcting a scoreboard. Reorientation is intellectual work
even when it is painful. And a person can complete all of it and still not be
reconnected to anything, because understanding is not belonging.

The table is where the faith becomes physical again. Something is put into your
hands. You eat it. It goes into your body. You do it in a room with other people who
are doing the same thing, at the same moment, with the same bread.

And Paul draws the conclusion that is easy to miss: because there is one loaf, we,
who are many, are one body; for we all partake of the one loaf. The unity is not
achieved by agreement. It is enacted by eating. The many become one by sharing a
single thing.

Which means the table does two kinds of reconnecting at once, and they are the two
this book has been separating for twenty-one chapters. It re-roots you to Christ
given for you. And it re-roots you to the people beside you, because you cannot take
the one loaf privately.

---

Now the other half of reconnection, which is harder than the meal.

Peter had denied Him beside a charcoal fire in a courtyard, three times, with
increasing vehemence, and the account says that as the third denial left his mouth
the Lord turned and looked at him. That look is one sentence in the text and it
undid a man completely; he went out and wept bitterly.

After the resurrection, on a beach, there is another charcoal fire — the same rare
word, and it is not an accident. Jesus has made breakfast. And He asks Peter, three
times, whether he loves Him.

Three denials. Three questions. The account says Peter was grieved that He asked the
third time, and of course he was; he could feel what was being done. But notice what
is not happening. There is no interrogation about the courtyard, no requirement that
Peter explain himself, no probationary period. There is a question about love, asked
three times, and after each answer a commission: feed my lambs, tend my sheep, feed
my sheep.

He is not merely forgiven. He is put back to work, publicly, in front of the others,
by a man who knows exactly what he did.

---

That pattern — restoration that includes usefulness — runs through everything in
this phase, and it is the answer to the fear that keeps people out of the room.

The fear is: they know what I did, or what happened to me, and I will always be the
person that happened to. And the Gospel's repeated answer is that God's restorations
are not conditional readmissions. Mephibosheth, lame in both feet, grandson of the
king David replaced, is brought to the palace and given a permanent place at the
king's own table — and the text notes, twice, that he ate at the table continually,
and that he was lame in both feet. The disability was not fixed. The seat was
permanent anyway.

And then there is the hardest piece, which cannot be avoided in a chapter about
reconnection.

Jacob spends twenty years away because his brother swore to kill him, and comes back
knowing Esau is riding out with four hundred men. He sends gifts ahead in waves. He
divides his family. He wrestles all night with someone he cannot overcome and will
not release, and comes away with a new name and a permanent limp.

And then Esau runs to meet him and embraces him and they both weep, and Jacob says
one of the strangest and most beautiful things in Genesis: I have seen your face, as
one sees the face of God, and you were pleased with me.

Reconciliation, when it happens, has that quality. It is not the erasure of twenty
years. Jacob still limps. But something is genuinely restored, and it is more than
either of them had a right to expect.

Two qualifications, because this teaching has been used to hurt people. Forgiveness
is commanded and is between you and God; reconciliation requires two parties and
sometimes cannot be had. And Paul's own instruction is conditional — if it is
possible, as much as it is up to you, be at peace with all men — which concedes that
it is sometimes neither possible nor up to you. Nothing here requires you to return
to someone who is still dangerous. The limp is not obligatory proof of anything.

## In You

Go to the table, and go to it before you feel ready, because that is the only way
anyone has ever gone.

The most common reason people stay away is the sense that they should sort something
out first — settle the doubts, resolve the anger, become someone who deserves to be
there. And the account of the first one demolishes that. Judas was at the table.
Peter, hours from denying Him, was handed bread by the man he would deny. Not one of
them had sorted anything out; several of them were about to fail catastrophically.

The bread was broken for you before you sorted anything out. That is not a technical
point; it is the whole architecture. If the meal required a settled mind, He would
have waited until after Pentecost to institute it, and He did not. He instituted it
on the worst night of their lives.

So take it with the doubts intact. Take it angry, if that is where you are. Take it
having been away for six years. The instruction was to do it in memory of Him, which
means the thing being remembered is His act, not your condition.

And then, the harder application: is there someone you need to eat with?

There is usually one relationship that reconnection stalls on. Not necessarily
reconciliation in the full sense — that may be closed, and if the person is
dangerous it should stay closed. But often there is a lesser thing available and
undone: a message sent, an acknowledgement made, a small willingness to be in the
same room. Jacob sent gifts ahead for days before he ever saw his brother's face.
The approach can be slow. It usually should be.

## In the Body

The table is where the Body's divisions are most visible and most painful, which is
its own kind of argument.

The eucharistic table is the one place where the wounds of 1054 and 1517 are enacted
rather than merely discussed — where a Catholic and a Protestant standing side by
side in the same room cannot receive together, and where the fact of the split stops
being an abstraction. That is agonising. It is also, in a strange way, healthy:
whatever else the Church has managed to paper over, it has not managed to paper over
this. The division is felt at the exact point where unity is supposed to be enacted.

And there is a second division at the table, older than the Reformation and less
discussed. Paul's fiercest words about communion are not about doctrine at all. He
tells the Corinthians their gatherings are doing more harm than good, because when
they come together the wealthy eat their own food first and get drunk while the poor
— who could not leave work early — arrive to nothing. That is what he means by not
discerning the body. It is not a warning about insufficient reverence in the
individual soul. It is a rebuke to a church whose meal reproduced the class
structure of the city outside it.

Read that way, the question to the Body is uncomfortable and current. Does the
Church's table look like the society around it, sorted by income and ethnicity and
education — or does it look like Pentecost? In most Western cities the honest answer
is the former, and the segregation of congregations by class and race is one of the
plainest failures of reconnection in the modern Church.

The table is meant to be the place where that fails to hold. One loaf, and everyone
in the room eats from it.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Do this in memory of me, He said, and they have.

Every week for two thousand years, through the fall of Rome, through schism, through
the wars, in catacombs and cathedrals and prison camps and front rooms, in languages
that did not exist when He said it, on every continent — bread has been broken and a
cup has been passed, by people who mostly could not agree with each other about
anything else.

What cannot be shaken is the gift itself: a body given and a cup poured out, received
with empty hands, requiring nothing from you except that you take it. Your sense of
worthiness will rise and fall for the rest of your life. Your doubts may never fully
resolve. The bread does not consult either.

## For Reflection

1. What are you waiting to sort out before you go back to the table? Who at the
   first one had it sorted?
2. Is there someone you need to eat with? What is the slow first step — the gift
   sent ahead?
3. Look at the room you worship in. Does the table look like the city outside it, or
   like Pentecost?
