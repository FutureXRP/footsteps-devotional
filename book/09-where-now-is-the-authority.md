# PART TWO — DISORIENTATION

> *The wilderness in between. The old map is gone and no new one has come.*

This is the longest part of the book, because it is the longest part of the
journey and the one nobody warns you about. Six chapters on the country where
bearings are lost: what happens to authority, what exile does to a people, what to
do when God does not answer, what the mystics found in the dark, what it looks like
at the scale of the whole Church, and how to live in a place you cannot leave yet.

Nothing in this part resolves. That is not a failure of the writing.

# Chapter Nine

## Where Now Is the Authority?

The book of Judges ends without an ending.

It simply stops, after a run of atrocities that get worse as they go — a Levite's
concubine raped to death and dismembered, a civil war, a tribe nearly annihilated,
and a final episode in which women are abducted from a festival to solve a
demographic problem. There is no summary, no verdict, no prophet stepping forward
to interpret it. There is one sentence, repeated through the second half of the
book like a coroner's note, and it is the last line:

In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his
own eyes.

Notice what the sentence does not say. It does not say everyone did what was wrong.
It says everyone did what was right — *in his own eyes*. Nobody in Judges thinks
they are the villain. Each of them has a reason, a justification, a reading of the
situation in which their action is defensible. What has collapsed is not morality
in the abstract. It is any shared answer to the question of who gets to say.

That is the sound of Disorientation, and once you have heard it you will hear it in
your own century.

---

Here is what happens after a break, and it happens with the reliability of physics.

You leave, or you are thrown, and for a while the crisis itself provides all the
structure you need. There is a thing to survive. Adrenaline is a kind of clarity.

And then, some months in, the adrenaline goes, and you discover the actual problem:
the apparatus you used to make decisions is gone with the thing you left.

Because you did not just leave a church, or a marriage, or a job, or a set of
beliefs. You left an entire system for knowing what was true. Somebody's teaching
carried more weight than your own reading. Some book was where the argument
stopped. Some community's approval was the check on your judgement. Some voice — a
pastor, a father, a tradition, a movement — was the thing you measured yourself
against, and now that voice is either gone or discredited, and there is nothing in
the slot.

So you are standing in a wilderness with no king, doing what is right in your own
eyes, and the terrifying part is not that you have become wicked. It is that you
have become the final court of appeal on every question, and you know exactly how
qualified you are for that job.

---

Israel in the trackless waste had a particular provision for this, and it is worth
looking at because it is so unsatisfying.

They got a cloud. During the day a pillar of cloud, at night a pillar of fire, and
the entire navigational method was: when it moves, you move; when it stops, you
stop. Sometimes it stayed for two days. Sometimes a month. Sometimes a year. The
text is explicit that they did not know in advance and were not told.

That is not a map. A map lets you plan. A map lets you see the route, calculate the
distance, and know where you will be in a fortnight. What they were given instead
was presence plus one instruction at a time, which is enough to travel on and not
nearly enough to feel secure.

If you are in the wilderness looking for a map and finding only a cloud, you have
not been abandoned. You have been given the standard issue.

---

But we should be honest about why this phase has become so much harder in the last
generation, because it is not only a spiritual problem.

The old authorities have not merely become unfashionable. Many of them
disqualified themselves. A generation watched trusted pastors turn out to be
predators, and the institutions around them turn out to be more interested in
liability than in children. They watched theological certainty deployed to defend
things that were plainly indefensible. They watched the Bible used as a blunt
instrument against people they loved. When someone raised in that raises the
question of authority, they are not being rebellious. They are responding rationally
to evidence.

And at the same moment, the technology of the last twenty years put every possible
answer in front of everyone at once. Any position on any question is now three
seconds away, argued by someone articulate, with a following. The medieval peasant
had one voice; you have ten thousand, algorithmically sorted for engagement, which
means sorted for outrage. The problem is not a shortage of authorities. It is that
there are so many that the very idea of one has become hard to hold.

Judges got there without the internet. But it got there.

## In You

If you are in this, the fog itself is the diagnosis. You are not losing your mind.
You are in phase two.

The mistake almost everyone makes here is trying to solve it quickly, and there are
two ways to do that and both of them are traps.

The first is to go and find a new authority fast — a teacher, a system, a movement,
a podcast — and hand over the keys. It works, briefly. The fog lifts. And you have
not actually done the work; you have swapped one unexamined structure for another,
usually a more confident one, and confidence is precisely what you should distrust
most in this season. The people who sound most certain right now are selling relief
from the exact discomfort you are in, and they know it.

The second is to conclude that there is no authority but you, and to make a virtue
of it. That is Judges, and Judges is not a happy book. A self that has been badly
formed by a bad system does not become reliable simply by being made autonomous. It
becomes lonely, and it becomes unfalsifiable, and it drifts.

The way through is slower and less dramatic. It starts by separating things that
have been fused. The pastor who failed is not the same as the Scripture he
mishandled. The tradition that hurt you is not identical to the Christ it was
pointing at, badly. That untangling takes years and it cannot be rushed, and it is
the actual work of this phase.

And meanwhile, keep something. Not everything — you cannot hold everything up right
now. But keep one or two practices you no longer feel: some Scripture, some prayer,
some presence with other believers, whatever you can still do without lying. Those
are the cloud. They will not tell you where you are going. They will tell you that
you have not been left, and in this phase that is the whole job.

## In the Body

The Church as a whole is asking this question at full volume, and it is the marker
that identifies the present upheaval as one of the big ones.

In 500 the question was whether the faith could stand without the empire. In 1054 it
was one bishop or a college. In 1500 it was pope and council against Scripture and
conscience. Each time the answer that eventually emerged was not the old one
restored, and each time it took a century or more to settle.

So look at what is actually contested now. Not whether Scripture is authoritative —
a great many of the disputing parties affirm that loudly. But who interprets it,
and by what method, and what happens when the interpretations conflict. Whether the
tradition of the first five centuries is a binding inheritance or an interesting
archive. What weight experience carries, and whose. Whether a denomination can
require anything of a congregation that can leave by email. Whether a global Church
whose numerical centre is now in Africa and Asia will continue to accept
theological direction from a West that supplies a shrinking fraction of its
believers and none of its growth — a question the Anglican Communion has already
had answered, in public, whether or not it liked the answer.

That last one is the most consequential thing happening in world Christianity and
the least discussed in Western congregations. The authority question is being
settled demographically while everyone argues about it doctrinally.

None of this will resolve in our lifetimes, and Christians who need it to resolve
in order to be at peace are going to be a long time unhappy. The historical
consolation is thin but real: the Church has never once known, from inside a
rummage sale, what the answer was going to be. It has only ever known afterwards.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Paul wrote to a congregation that had fractured into fan clubs — I am of Paul, I
am of Apollos, I am of Cephas — which is an authority crisis in miniature, complete
with people who thought naming a better teacher settled the question.

His answer did not adjudicate between the teachers. He said that no one can lay any
other foundation than the one that is laid, which is Jesus Christ. Then he said
something more uncomfortable: everything built on that foundation gets tested by
fire, and some of it burns, and the builder is saved even as the work goes up in
smoke.

That is the thing that holds when authority is in question. Not your ability to
identify which voice is trustworthy — you may be genuinely unable to do that this
year. What cannot be shaken is that the foundation was laid by someone else, before
you arrived, and it is not being tested. The fire is testing what people built on
it. The rock underneath is the one thing in the whole scene that is not on trial.

## For Reflection

1. Whose voice used to be the place where the argument stopped for you? What is in
   that slot now?
2. Have you handed the keys to a new authority quickly, or declared yourself the
   only one? What did that cost?
3. Name one practice you can keep this month without lying. Can you keep it without
   requiring it to feel like anything?
