# Chapter Six

## The Break You Did Not Choose

He sees them from a long way off, and he is glad to see them.

That is what makes it unbearable. Joseph is seventeen, carrying a message from
their father, walking out across open country to find his brothers with the flocks,
and there is nothing in the story to suggest he arrived expecting anything but a
greeting. They see the coat first. They see it while he is still distant, the
absurd ornamented thing their father gave him and not them, and by the time he is
close enough to speak they have already decided.

They strip the coat off him. They throw him into a dry cistern — a stone-sided pit
in the ground, no water in it, no way out on your own. And then, with him down
there, they sit down and eat a meal. The text puts that detail in without comment
and it is the coldest sentence in Genesis.

A caravan comes past on the trade road, and it occurs to Judah that there is money
in this. So they haul him up and sell him for twenty pieces of silver, and a boy
who woke up that morning as his father's favourite goes south in a slave train.

Then thirteen years. Sold into a household. Falsely accused by a woman whose
advances he refused. Thrown into prison on the accusation. Forgotten there by a man
who promised to remember him — two whole years of being forgotten, which the text
also records without softening.

None of it was chosen. Not one hour of it. Abram walked out of Haran; Elisha burned
his own plough. Joseph was thrown, sold, accused, imprisoned and forgotten by other
people acting freely and wickedly.

---

Both kinds of break are in this book, and they must not be treated as the same
thing.

There is the disconnect you initiate, which is frightening but which leaves your
agency intact. And there is the disconnect done to you — the betrayal, the
diagnosis, the redundancy, the drunk driver, the parent who should have kept you
safe and did not. That break arrives with an extra wound the chosen kind does not
carry: the loss of the belief that you have any control over what happens to you at
all.

Almost all the shallow teaching about upheaval fails here. It generalises from the
chosen break — courage, obedience, stepping out — and hands it to someone who was
pushed. And the person who was pushed hears, underneath the encouragement, an
accusation: *if this is a journey, and journeys are chosen, what did I do?*

You did not do anything. Some pits are dug by other people.

---

So what does Scripture actually do with inflicted evil? It does something more
careful than either of the two things we usually do with it.

Twenty-two years later, Joseph is second in Egypt and his brothers are standing in
front of him terrified, certain he has been waiting his whole career for this. And
he says the sentence the whole Bible builds on for this question:

As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to save many
people alive.

Read what he does *not* say. He does not say it was not evil. He looks at them and
uses the word — you meant evil — and does not take it back or soften it or suggest
they had been unknowing instruments of a plan. Their intent was wicked. It remains
wicked. Twenty-two years and a national rescue have not laundered it into
something else.

And he does not say God did it. The verb is *meant*, used twice, of two different
parties with two different intentions running through the same event. They aimed it
at his destruction. God aimed it at the saving of many lives. Both aims were real,
and only one of them was good, and God's did not require Him to be the author of
theirs.

This is the distinction the whole chapter hangs on, and the Church has often stated
it badly and hurt people doing so. God is not the author of evil. He does not
inspire the cruelty, He does not need the sin, and when someone sinned against you
the guilt is entirely theirs and no fraction of it transfers upward. And yet
nothing reaches you from outside His hand. He permits, and He redeems, and He is
able to weave what He would never call good toward an outcome He calls very good.

He did not send the evil. He is Lord of the wreckage.

If you have been handed the flattened version of this — *everything happens for a
reason*, delivered at a funeral — you have been handed something that sounds like
Joseph and is not. Joseph never said the evil had a reason. He said God had a
purpose, running alongside their evil, in spite of it, and getting somewhere they
never intended.

---

One more thing about that pit, because it is easy to skip in a story that ends
well.

Joseph did not know. Not in the cistern, not in Potiphar's house, not in the two
forgotten years. Genesis 50 is available to us and was not available to him. He
lived every one of those days without the sentence that makes sense of them, and if
there is a hard mercy in his story it is that his faithfulness happened in the dark
and not in the light of the explanation.

You may not get the sentence. Many people do not, in this life. That is a real loss
and it should not be talked around. But notice that the meaning of Joseph's
suffering was not created by his understanding of it. It was true in the pit before
he could see it. Whatever God is doing with what was done to you is true now,
whether or not you are ever shown it.

## In You

If your break was inflicted, start here: it was not your fault, and God is not
asking you to file it under blessing.

There is a piety that rushes to redemption because it cannot tolerate sitting with
harm. It presses the wounded toward gratitude, and toward forgiveness on a
schedule, and it does this mostly to relieve its own discomfort. You do not have to
participate. The Psalms are full of people telling God exactly what was done to
them, in detail, with names, and asking Him to act on it — and God preserved those
prayers in the book. Your anger about what happened is not a spiritual problem to
be corrected before you can approach Him. It is often the first honest thing you
have brought Him in months.

Then, when you can, the harder work. Joseph named the evil as evil *and* refused to
let it become the whole story. Those are two separate acts and most of us can only
manage one at a time. Naming it protects you from the lie that you deserved it.
Refusing it the whole story protects you from the lie that it gets to define
everything that follows.

Notice also what he did in the pit and the prison, which is the only part of the
story you have any control over. He worked. He interpreted dreams for men who
forgot him. He administered a household and then a jail, faithfully, with no
evidence it was leading anywhere. That is what agency looks like when the big
things have been taken out of your hands — not the restoration of control, but the
small faithfulness that remains available inside a situation you did not choose.

And if you are nowhere near any of that yet — if you are still in the cistern
listening to the people who put you there having a meal — then the only thing this
chapter is for is to tell you that the pit is in the Bible. Your experience has a
precedent in the text and a God who sees you in it. That is not much. On some days
it is the difference between everything.

## In the Body

The Church has an inflicted-break problem that it has been very slow to name
honestly, and it is different from the losses it prefers to talk about.

Not all of the shaking of the Western Church in the last fifty years is God sifting
a body He loves. Some of it is harm the body did, to its own, and then concealed.
The abuse crises that have run through the Catholic Church, the Southern Baptist
Convention, Anglican dioceses, evangelical and charismatic networks and mission
agencies alike were not a cultural persecution and not a mysterious providence.
They were sin, done by people with authority to people without it, and then hidden
by institutions that judged their reputation more valuable than their children.

That must not be folded into the language of rummage sales and refining fire as
though it were weather. God did not send it. He was not the author of it. It has no
redemptive interpretation available to the institutions that did it, and any
attempt by them to supply one is a further offence.

What *is* true is Joseph's second clause, and it belongs only in the mouths of the
harmed, never in the mouths of those who harmed them. God is able to take even
this. The exposure that felt like the destruction of the Church's credibility has
been, in fact, the removal of something that could be shaken — a culture of
deference, an assumption that spiritual authority need not be accountable, a
theology of leadership that could not distinguish anointing from power. Those had
to go. That they went at such cost, and only when survivors forced them out, is the
body's shame and not its strategy.

The global picture holds the same double truth. Vast numbers of Christians in this
century are living the inflicted break — imprisoned in Eritrea, displaced across
the Sahel, driven out of towns their families had held since the first centuries of
the faith. The Church is not being purified *by* their persecutors. Their
persecutors are guilty. And the Church has, since Stephen, kept discovering that
what was scattered by violence went on preaching wherever it landed. Both.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

There is a hand in the weaving.

Not a hand that inflicted the wound — that must be said one more time, because a
wounded reader will hear it wrongly if it is not. Somebody else struck the blow and
it was theirs. But there is a hand underneath the wreckage, working with material
it never chose, and it does not stop working because the material is terrible.

That is what holds when a break was done to you. Not an explanation, which may
never come. Not the erasure of the harm, which is not on offer. What cannot be
shaken is a God who is present in the cistern and in the prison and in the two
forgotten years, and who is patiently turning even what was meant against you
toward the saving of lives — including, in time, your own.

The evil was real and it is passing. The weaving outlasts it.

## For Reflection

1. Was your break chosen or inflicted? How has advice for the other kind landed on
   you?
2. Can you name what was done to you as wrong, in plain words, without immediately
   reaching for a redemptive interpretation?
3. What small faithfulness is still available to you inside a situation you did not
   choose?
