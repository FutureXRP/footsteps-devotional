# Chapter Twelve

## The Dark Night

He wrote the poem in a prison cell, in the dark, from memory.

Juan de Yepes — John of the Cross — was a Carmelite friar who had joined Teresa of
Avila's reform of the order, and in 1577 his own brothers, from the unreformed
branch, abducted him. They locked him in a room in Toledo barely large enough to
lie down in, with a slit high in the wall for light. He was brought out regularly
to be flogged in front of the community. He was there nine months.

And in that cell, with no paper for most of it, he composed some of the finest
poetry in the Spanish language, about a soul slipping out of a sleeping house at
night to meet the one it loves.

Then he escaped, through a window, down a rope made of strips of blanket.

Later he wrote commentaries on those poems, and gave the world a phrase it has
been misusing ever since: **the dark night of the soul**.

---

Almost everybody who uses the phrase means depression, or a rough patch, or grief.
That is not what he meant, and the distinction is one of the most pastorally useful
things the Christian tradition has produced.

What John was describing is this. A person begins in faith and God gives them
consolations — a felt sweetness in prayer, a warmth in worship, a sense of presence,
tears at the right moments. These are real, and they are gifts, and they are given
to beginners the way a parent gives a child something sweet to get them to the
table.

And then, in some lives, they stop.

Prayer becomes arid. The Scripture that used to open goes flat. Worship produces
nothing. The person continues to do everything they were doing, and there is
nothing there, and no amount of effort or repentance or technique brings the
feeling back.

John's claim — and this is what makes him worth reading — is that in a great many
cases this is not regression. It is God removing the consolation because the person
had begun to love the consolation, and He wants to be loved Himself. The sweetness
was never the point. It was the invitation. And the withdrawal of it is not
abandonment but a more advanced kind of nearness, in which He can no longer be
detected by the instruments the soul had been using.

The night, he says, is the way the soul goes to God. And it is dark not because God
is absent but because He is now approaching in a way that overwhelms the faculties
— the way a light too strong for the eye registers as blackness.

---

Two enormous cautions, and John himself insisted on the first of them.

**The dark night is not depression, and confusing them has done real harm.**
Depression is an illness. It flattens everything, not just prayer — appetite, sleep,
work, love, the capacity for pleasure of any kind — and it responds to treatment,
and telling a depressed person that God is purifying them by withholding
consolation is spiritual malpractice that has kept people from doctors. John's own
tradition distinguished these. If you cannot get out of bed, if you have stopped
eating, if the thought of harming yourself has arrived, this chapter is not your
diagnosis. Get help. Getting help is not a failure of faith; it is what you would
tell anyone you loved to do.

**And the dark night is not the ordinary dryness of a distracted life.** Sometimes
prayer is dry because you are exhausted, or because you have not slept, or because
you are avoiding something you know you need to face. The tradition's test is
whether the person still wants God — whether, in the absence of all felt reward,
there remains a longing that has nothing to feed on. A soul that has simply drifted
does not ache. A soul in the night aches constantly and gets nothing for it.

---

Teresa of Avila, who was John's collaborator and by all accounts more practical
than he was, has the other half of this. Her image is a castle, a great interior
structure of many rooms, with God in the innermost chamber, and the soul travelling
inward through the rooms across a lifetime. What she insists on is that the outer
rooms are real rooms — that the beginner's noisy, distracted, consolation-hungry
prayer is not fake, it is early. Nobody starts in the centre.

She also had a sense of humour about the journey that the literature usually lacks.
Her reported remark to God after being thrown from a mule into a river — that if
this is how He treats His friends, it is no wonder He has so few — is almost
certainly polished by later tellers, but it survives because everyone who has been
in the middle rooms recognises the sentiment.

Between them, John and Teresa gave the Church something it badly needed: a
vocabulary in which the absence of feeling is a stage rather than a verdict.

---

And there is one more thing this century needs to hear from them, because we have
a word for this experience that they did not have.

We call it deconstruction. And what mostly happens under that word is that a person
who has been taught to identify the presence of God with a particular emotional
state, produced reliably by particular music in particular buildings, loses access
to that state — and reasonably concludes that they have lost God.

They have not necessarily lost God. They may have lost a delivery mechanism. And
the tradition would say, gently, that this is a normal and even hopeful stage,
horribly misread by a culture that has no category for it and by churches that
measure spiritual health by reported feeling.

That is not a dismissal of deconstruction, most of which is driven by legitimate
grievance and honest intellectual trouble rather than by dry prayer. But among
everyone currently in that country, some proportion are not losing their faith at
all. They are in a room John of the Cross described in a Spanish cell four hundred
and fifty years ago, and nobody ever told them the room existed.

## In You

If prayer has gone silent, the first question is not spiritual. It is: are you
well, and are you sleeping, and is there something in your life you are refusing to
look at? Rule those out honestly, because two of them respond to a doctor and one
to a confession, and none of them respond to endurance.

If they are ruled out, then here is the counsel the tradition gives, and it is
almost aggressively unspectacular.

**Do not stop.** Keep the practice. Pray the words when the words are nothing.
Read the passage when the passage is flat. Show up to the gathering and sing with a
closed throat. The whole test of this season is whether you will continue in the
absence of reward, and the only way to fail it is to stop.

**Do not chase the feeling.** You will be tempted to go somewhere it can be
manufactured — a conference, a more intense environment, whatever used to work. If
God has removed the consolation, restoring it artificially is working against the
thing being done in you.

**Lower the bar and keep it.** Not an hour. Five minutes. One psalm. The Lord's
Prayer said slowly once. The goal in the night is not depth; it is continuity.

**Tell someone.** The night is enormously worsened by the assumption that you are
the only one, and you are not — you are in the company of a Carmelite in a cell,
a psalmist who ended in darkness, and probably four people in your own
congregation who have never said so.

And hold this: your sense of God's presence is not a measurement of God's presence.
It is a measurement of your sense. The two have been confused in you, probably by
teaching you received in good faith, and pulling them apart is precisely what this
season is for.

## In the Body

Whole traditions go through this, and one is going through it now.

The Church in the West is experiencing something structurally similar to the night:
the withdrawal of the consolations it had come to expect. Cultural approval. Full
buildings. The sense of being at the centre of things. Growth that arrived without
having to be prayed for. Those were real, and for a long time they functioned as
confirmation — the body could tell it was doing well because it felt like it was
doing well.

All of it is being withdrawn, and the body is reacting exactly as an individual
does in the night. It is chasing the feeling: bigger events, better production,
harder attempts to manufacture the atmosphere of a period that is over. It is
concluding that the absence of the consolation means the absence of God. It is, in
places, going quiet and stopping.

John's counsel to a soul applies without much modification. Do not stop. Keep the
practices — the Scriptures read, the bread broken, the poor fed, the prayers said in
half-empty rooms — in the absence of any evidence that they are producing anything.
Do not chase the atmosphere of 1975. And do not mistake the loss of a delivery
mechanism for the loss of God.

There is corroboration for this in the parts of the Church that have never had the
consolations. The churches growing fastest in the world are largely poor, often
pressured, and were never given cultural approval to lose. They have been operating
without the sweetness the whole time. It has not slowed them down.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

He is not detectable and He has not left. Those two facts sit together in this
chapter and the whole of it depends on refusing to collapse them.

What cannot be shaken is a presence that does not depend on your perception of it.
The God who was in the cell in Toledo was as present during the floggings as during
the poem, and John's own testimony is that he was never nearer to Him than in the
months he could feel nothing at all.

Your instruments have gone dark. The room is still occupied.
