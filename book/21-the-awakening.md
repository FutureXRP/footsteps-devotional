# Chapter Twenty-One

## The Awakening

On the evening of 24 May 1738, an Anglican priest who had been a missionary failure
in Georgia went very unwillingly to a small meeting in Aldersgate Street in London.

John Wesley had done everything. Ordained. Oxford. A rigorous, methodical, disciplined
holiness that earned him and his brother a mocking nickname that stuck. He had
crossed the Atlantic to convert the Indians and had come home defeated, having
discovered in a storm at sea that a group of unremarkable German Moravians were not
afraid to die and he was.

At the meeting, someone was reading aloud from Luther's preface to the epistle to
the Romans. And about a quarter before nine, Wesley wrote later, while the reader was
describing the change which God works in the heart through faith in Christ, he felt
his heart strangely warmed. He felt he did trust in Christ, Christ alone, for
salvation — and an assurance was given him that his sins, even his, had been taken
away.

Nothing about his theology changed that night that had not been available to him for
years. What changed was that it became his.

Within two years he was preaching in fields to coal miners at Kingswood, because no
respectable pulpit would have the message, and the awakening that followed reshaped a
nation.

---

Reorientation, at the scale of a whole Church, has a name. It is awakening, and it
has happened often enough to have recognisable features.

**It begins in prayer that nobody is watching.** The Moravians who unnerved Wesley
in that storm came from Herrnhut, a village on Count Zinzendorf's estate in Saxony
full of refugees who had been fighting bitterly with one another until, in August
1727, something broke over them in a communion service. They began a prayer relay —
someone praying at every hour — and it ran, according to their own accounts, for over
a hundred years. Out of that village came the first large-scale Protestant missionary
movement, sent by a community of a few hundred people, decades before anyone else
thought of it.

**It is usually preached to people the establishment has written off.** Whitefield in
the fields. Wesley to the miners, whose faces, he noted, were streaked white where
the tears ran through the coal dust. The First Great Awakening ran through the
American colonies among ordinary settlers; Edwards, its most formidable mind, was
eventually dismissed by his own congregation at Northampton.

**It produces theology and it produces reform, in that order.** Edwards wrote about
the religious affections precisely because he had watched an awakening produce both
genuine transformation and a great deal of emotional froth, and he wanted to give
people tools to tell them apart — his conclusion being that the reliable evidence is
not intensity of feeling but a changed life. And the reform followed: Wilberforce
and the Clapham circle, converted in the evangelical revival, spent forty-six years
on the abolition of the slave trade and then on slavery itself, and Wilberforce died
three days after the bill passed. John Newton, who had captained slave ships and
later wrote the hymn everybody knows, lived to give evidence to Parliament about
what he had done.

**And it always overreaches.** Every awakening in history produced excess, division,
and people who mistook the movement for the kingdom. Edwards was writing his
diagnostic manual for a reason.

---

The modern reorientations are less visible and no less real, and they answer the
crisis described in Chapter Fourteen.

When the nineteenth century's intellectual pressure and then the trenches destroyed
the confident liberal Protestantism that had built on human progress, the response
was not a retreat into anti-intellectualism, at least not everywhere. Barth's
commentary on Romans landed, as one contemporary put it, like a bomb on the
theologians' playground. The Church recovered a God who speaks rather than a God who
is inferred, and that recovery armed the resistance at Barmen.

The Church also went global in the twentieth century in a way that is
epoch-defining and still underappreciated in Western congregations. In 1900 the
overwhelming majority of the world's Christians were European or North American. That
is no longer remotely true. The faith's centre of gravity has moved south and east,
and the growth is not a Western export any more — it is African, Latin American,
Asian, Pentecostal, and led by people the old missionary societies never appointed.

And there is the retrieval movement that Scot McKnight and others were describing
twenty years ago in the emerging Western church, and which has outlasted the label:
Christians reaching back past their immediate tradition to recover things it had
discarded. The daily office in evangelical households. Lent observed by people whose
grandparents thought it was superstition. Spiritual direction. Fasting. The desert
fathers read by people under thirty. Whatever else is happening, a generation that
was handed a thin faith is going looking for the thick one, and going backwards to
find it.

---

Two things must be said about awakening before anyone starts praying for one, and
both are in the record.

**It cannot be manufactured.** Every attempt to reproduce the conditions of a
previous revival has produced technique and not revival. Zechariah's word stands:
not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit. Aldersgate was a man going unwillingly
to a meeting he did not want to attend.

**And it always arrives with counterfeits attached.** Edwards, Wesley and every
serious observer since have had to spend enormous energy distinguishing the work
from the froth around it. The test they all landed on is the unglamorous one: a
changed life over time. Not the intensity of the meeting. Not the tears. Whether, two
years later, the man is still sober, the marriage is mended, the money is being given
away, the enemy is being forgiven.

## In the Body

So what should the Body actually do, standing where it stands?

The record suggests four things, none of them a strategy.

**Pray in a way nobody is watching.** Herrnhut is the most consistently reproduced
precondition in the whole history of awakening, and it is the least reproducible as
a programme, because a prayer relay announced with a launch event is not the same
thing as a village that could not stop.

**Preach to the people the institution has written off.** Every awakening went to
the fields. The current equivalents are not hard to identify — they are the people
Western Christianity has effectively conceded: young men, the poor in wealthy
countries, the digitally lonely, and the large populations of European cities where
the historic churches long ago stopped trying.

**Do the theological work seriously.** Barth, Edwards and Athanasius were not
anti-intellectual and the awakenings they served were not. A Church that responds to
its disorientation with slogans will get a movement with the lifespan of a slogan.

**And build the diagnostic in from the start.** Expect the froth. Do not be
scandalised by it, and do not defend it either. Edwards' test — a changed life over
time — is still the best one available, and a movement willing to apply it to itself
is one worth trusting.

## In You

Personal reorientation has an awakening shape too, and Wesley's account of that
Wednesday evening is precise about what it was.

He did not learn anything new. He had known the doctrine of justification by faith
for years; he had been taught it, could have examined a student on it, had crossed
an ocean on the strength of it. What happened at Aldersgate was that a thing he knew
moved about eighteen inches, from his head to somewhere it could actually hold his
weight.

That is what you are waiting for, and it is worth naming, because if you are looking
for new information you will keep reading and keep not being helped. The thing that
will eventually put your feet on the ground is almost certainly something you already
know and do not yet trust. Grace, probably. Most people's wilderness ends when a
sentence they could have recited at fifteen finally applies to them.

You cannot make that happen. Wesley did not go to Aldersgate to have it happen; he
went reluctantly. But notice what he was doing in the meantime, in the years of
failure: he kept showing up. He was in the room. That is the only part available to
you, and it is the part Chapter Fifteen already asked for — the floor, kept without
results.

And when it does come, apply Edwards' test to yourself before you tell anyone it is
a turning point. Not the intensity of the moment. Whether, in two years, the life is
different.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Malachi closes the Old Testament, and then there are four hundred years of silence
before anyone in Israel hears from God again — the longest disorientation in the
book, with the last page of Scripture already written and nobody knowing it.

And the last image he leaves them with is this: to you who fear my name, the sun of
righteousness will arise with healing in its wings.

The sun. Which is the one thing in the natural world that requires nothing from
anybody in order to come up. Nobody organised a dawn. Nobody's diligence produced
it, and nobody's failure delayed it, and the people asleep get exactly the same
morning as the people who stayed awake for it.

That is what cannot be shaken here. Not the movement, which will overreach. Not the
revival, which will have froth in it and will end. What remains is the God who rises
on His own initiative over people who have been in the dark for four centuries and
had stopped expecting anything — and who arrives, when He arrives, with healing.

## For Reflection

1. What do you already know that has not yet moved the eighteen inches?
2. Where are you still showing up without results? What would it cost to keep doing
   that for another year?
3. Apply Edwards' test to something you called a turning point two years ago. What
   is actually different?
