# PART ZERO — THE MAP

> *Before the shaking, the shape of it.*

Three chapters, and then the journey. The first names the promise that hangs over
every upheaval in this book. The second names the pattern — why this keeps
happening, to souls and to the Church, on a rhythm you can almost set a watch by.
The third hands you the map itself: four phases, one shape, and where you probably
are on it right now.

---

# Chapter One

## What Can Be Shaken

At Sinai the mountain moved.

That is not a figure of speech in the text. The people stood at the foot of it and
the ground under them shuddered, and there was fire on the summit and a darkness
and a trumpet blast that grew louder rather than fading, the way a sound does when
something is coming closer. And they begged for it to stop. They sent Moses up and
asked that God not speak to them directly again, because a voice that shakes a
mountain is not a voice you can survive at close range.

Centuries later a writer sat down to explain to a group of exhausted Jewish
Christians why their world was coming apart, and he reached back for that mountain.
He quoted the prophet Haggai, where God says: yet once more I will shake not only
the earth, but also the heavens.

And then he did something remarkable. He stopped, and read the grammar.

This phrase, he says — *yet once more* — signifies the removing of those things
that are shaken, as of things that have been made, so that those things which
cannot be shaken may remain.

Sit with what he has just done. He has taken the most frightening image in the
religious imagination, the earth moving under your feet, and told you what it is
*for*. The shaking is not destruction. The shaking is a sieve. God shakes in order
to remove the things that can be removed — the made things, the assembled things,
the scaffolding — so that what is actually load-bearing is left standing clear and
visible and alone.

He does not shake what He means to destroy. He shakes what He means to keep.

That sentence is going to carry this whole book, so let me be honest about where it
comes from. It is not a quotation. Hebrews says that what can be shaken is removed
so that what cannot be shaken may remain; it is describing one great final shaking,
and it says nothing directly about your redundancy or your diagnosis or the state of
your denomination. The sentence is what I take the passage to mean when it is
brought to bear on an ordinary life — a principle drawn out of the text, not a
promise the text makes about every hard year you will have.

I think it is a fair reading, and the whole book is an argument that it is. But you
should know you are being handed an interpretation and not a proof text, and you
should feel free to hold it the way you would hold any interpretation: firmly enough
to lean on, loosely enough to be corrected.

---

There is a particular sound a house makes in a windstorm. Anyone who has lived
through one knows it: a groaning and a rattling and, somewhere out in the dark, the
crack of something giving way. In the morning you go out and find the yard covered
in branches, and every one of them is dead wood. The storm did not kill them. The
storm found them. They had been dead on the tree for a year and the tree had gone
on carrying them, and it took a night of violence to reveal what had already been
true.

That is closer to what happens in an upheaval than the language of catastrophe
usually allows. What falls in the shaking was, in almost every case, already
falling. The certainty that shattered had been hollow for years. The institution
that collapsed had been running on inherited momentum since before you were born.
The version of faith that could not survive the diagnosis had been thin for a long
time and nobody had leaned on it hard enough to find out.

This is not a comfort you can offer to someone in the first hours. It is
insufferable in the first hours. But it is true, and later it becomes the
difference between a person who is destroyed by their upheaval and a person who is
sifted by it.

---

Here is the thing about a sieve that the metaphor of destruction misses entirely.

A sieve is used by someone who wants something. Nobody shakes a pan of river silt
because they hate silt. They shake it because there is gold in there and the only
way to find it is to let everything that is not gold fall through. The violence of
the motion is entirely in service of the thing being kept. If you filmed only the
shaking, and never showed the hand or the gold, it would look like rage. It is the
opposite of rage. It is the most focused kind of wanting there is.

The refiner in Malachi does the same thing and the prophet notices the posture. He
does not stand back from the fire. He sits down in front of it. He sits because
this takes time, and because he is watching for something — the moment the surface
goes clear and he can see his own face in the metal. The fire is not the point. The
face is the point. The fire is simply the only thing that will bring the impurities
up where they can be lifted off.

You have probably been in a season where you felt God had walked away from the
furnace and left you in it. Malachi says the opposite. He has pulled up a chair.

## In You

Something in your life is being shaken right now, or it has been, or it will be.
That is not a threat. It is simply the shape of a life lived in a world that is
itself being made new, and the sooner you stop treating each upheaval as an
anomaly, the sooner you can start reading it.

So hold whatever is shaking in you up against this promise and ask the truer
question. Not *how do I make this stop*, which is the first and most human
question and the one that will get you nowhere. Ask instead: *what is being
revealed here that was always going to last?*

Because here is what I suspect is happening, if your faith is what is moving. You
are not losing it. You are losing the scaffolding that was built around it —
borrowed certainties, inherited assumptions, the emotional habits of a religious
culture, the props you sincerely mistook for the structure. Scaffolding is not a
lie. Scaffolding is how buildings get built. But it was never meant to be
permanent, and there comes a day when it has to come down, and the coming down
always looks like collapse to someone standing on it.

Ask what you actually lost this year. Name it specifically. Was it God, or was it
your picture of God? Was it faith, or the certainty that used to come bundled with
it? Was it your family, or your idea of what your family was? Very often the thing
that shattered turns out to have been a photograph of the thing, and the thing
itself is still there, unphotographed, waiting to be met without the frame.

And one more, gently, because a wounded person needs this said carefully and
almost never gets it that way. Two things are true, and the whole book depends on
not letting them blur.

The first is that some hardship comes from God's own hand, and Scripture is not
shy about it. A door closed. A thorn given and not removed. The discipline
Hebrews describes a few verses before our passage, which it compares to a father's
correction and calls painful at the time. Those are real, they are His, and the
older writers had a good phrase for them — a severe mercy, the kind of wound a
surgeon makes.

The second is that what another person did to you in sin is not in that category,
and must never be filed there. He did not send it. He did not need it. The guilt
belongs to the person who chose it, it stays with them, and no amount of talk
about divine purpose transfers a gram of it to heaven's account.

Hold both and you can say the sentence this book will keep saying. He is not the
author of the evil. He is the Lord of the wreckage — able to take what He would
never have chosen and work it toward what cannot be shaken. Not because the evil
was secretly good. Because He is greater than it, and He has not left the room.

## In the Body

The Church in the West has spent roughly sixty years being shaken, and has spent
most of that time treating it as an emergency to be managed.

The numbers are not in dispute. Across Western Europe, North America, Australia,
the historic mainline and increasingly the evangelical churches too, attendance and
affiliation have fallen through a generation and kept falling. Institutions that
seemed permanent in 1960 — denominational machinery, seminaries, publishing houses,
the assumption that a nation's public life would be conducted in Christian
vocabulary — have thinned or gone. The Christendom arrangement, where the faith
supplied a civilisation's default settings, is over in the places where it was
strongest and longest.

The reflex has been to fix it. Better programmes, better music, better branding,
better arguments, a return to the fifties, a leap to the future. Every one of those
efforts assumes the shaking is the problem.

But run the sieve over the last sixty years and look at what has actually fallen
through. Cultural Christianity fell — the affiliation that cost nothing and
therefore meant nothing. The assumption of respectability fell. The confusion of
the gospel with a national identity fell, and is still falling, loudly. Structures
that had outlived their purpose and were being kept alive by budget rather than
mission fell. Deference to religious authority that had never earned scrutiny fell,
and where that authority had been abused, its fall was not a tragedy but an
overdue judgement.

And in the same sixty years the faith has grown at a rate the West has not seen in
centuries — in sub-Saharan Africa, in China, in Latin America, in Korea, in Iran.
There are more Christians alive now than at any point in history. The centre of
gravity of world Christianity has moved south and east. Something is being removed.
Something is very obviously not being removed.

That is not decline. That is a sieve, and it is being worked by a hand that wants
something.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

The made things go. That is what the writer of Hebrews says, precisely and almost
casually: *as of things that have been made*. Everything assembled, everything
constructed, everything with a founding date and a building fund and a set of
assumptions — all of it is, by definition, shakeable.

What remains is not a better-built version of the same. What remains is the thing
that was never made in the first place: the God who spoke at the mountain, and the
kingdom He is handing over, which is described as one that cannot be moved.

You are not being asked to hold on. You are being handed something that holds.

## For Reflection

1. Name one thing you have lost in the last five years. Was it the structure, or
   was it the scaffolding around the structure? How would you know the difference?
2. If the shaking in your life were a sieve rather than a wrecking ball, what do
   you think the hand working it is trying to find?
3. Where have you spent energy trying to make the shaking stop, that might have
   been better spent asking what it is revealing?
