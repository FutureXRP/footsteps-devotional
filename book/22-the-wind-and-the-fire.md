# PART FOUR — RECONNECT

> *Re-rooted. The new thing is inhabited, and communion is restored on the far side.*

The last phase is not the absence of scars. It is the discovery that a person who
has been through this has something to give that they did not have before, and that
it can only be given inside a body. Five chapters: the Spirit given, the table, the
old wound released, what it means to be planted, and being sent back with the scar.

---

# Chapter Twenty-Two

## The Wind and the Fire

There were about a hundred and twenty of them in an upstairs room, and they had been
told to wait.

That is worth sitting with, because it is not what anyone would have designed. The
resurrection had happened. The most extraordinary event in history had occurred and
they had eaten breakfast with the man it happened to, and His instruction was: do
not leave Jerusalem. Wait for the promise. They waited ten days, in a city where the
authorities had killed their teacher seven weeks earlier, doing nothing, which after
everything must have been almost unbearable.

Then the day of Pentecost came, and they were all together in one place.

Suddenly there came from the sky a sound like the rushing of a mighty wind, and it
filled all the house where they were sitting. Tongues like fire appeared and
distributed themselves, and one sat on each of them. And they were all filled with
the Holy Spirit and began to speak with other languages, as the Spirit gave them the
ability.

Note the distribution. Tongues like fire, sitting on each of them. Not one flame over
the leader, not a pillar over the building. Individually, on every person in the
room, including the women named in the previous chapter and the hundred and twenty
whose names nobody recorded.

Whatever else Pentecost is, it is the moment the presence of God stopped being
located in a building and started being located in people, plurally, without a
hierarchy of access.

---

And then the crowd, and the languages, and Peter standing up.

Jerusalem at Pentecost was full of Jews from across the known world — the list in
Acts 2 runs from Parthia and Media to Egypt, Libya, Crete and Rome — and every one
of them heard the mighty works of God in their own native language.

Take the trouble to notice what that reverses. In Genesis, at Babel, a unified
humanity building a tower to make a name for itself has its language confused, and
scatters. At Pentecost the scattering is not undone — nobody starts speaking one
imperial tongue — but the barrier is overcome from the other direction. The gospel
does not require you to learn the language of the centre. It arrives in yours.

That principle has driven every genuine missionary movement since and is the reason
the Bible has been translated into more languages than any book in history. It is
also the reason the faith survives the collapse of the cultures it passes through:
it was never welded to one.

---

Now the part of this that belongs to Reconnect specifically.

After the break, after the wilderness, after the slow rebuilding of bearings, the
last movement is being knit back into a body — and the New Testament's language for
that is startlingly physical.

Paul's image is not that the church is like a body. It is that you are members of
one another. Not members of an organisation; members of each other, the way a hand
belongs to an arm. And he presses it into the places it gets uncomfortable: the eye
cannot say to the hand, I have no need of you. The parts that seem weaker are
necessary. If one member suffers, all the members suffer with it.

And the first thing the newly reconnected community in Acts does is astonishingly
ordinary. They continued steadfastly in the apostles' teaching and fellowship, in the
breaking of bread and prayers. Four things. Teaching, one another's company, meals,
prayer. Then, later in the chapter, they were selling their possessions and
distributing to anyone who had need, and breaking bread at home, and eating with
gladness and singleness of heart.

The most powerful outpouring of the Spirit in history produced a community that
mostly ate together and shared money. That is what reconnection looks like when it
lands.

---

One honest note about the wait.

They were told to stay in the city and do nothing for ten days, and the Spirit came
in His own time, not theirs. If you have reached the point in your own upheaval
where the bearings have returned and the identity has been recovered and you are
ready to be part of something again — and nothing is available, and the door has not
opened, and you are sitting in an upstairs room in a city that hurt you — that is a
recognised position. It is where the Church itself started.

Waiting is not the failure of this phase. It is frequently its first act.

## In You

Reconnection is where a great many people who survived the wilderness beautifully
come to a stop, and the reason is simple: solitude became safe.

In the wilderness you learned to need very little from people. That was a genuine
skill and it kept you alive. But it hardens, and after a few years the capacity for
self-sufficiency that saved you becomes the thing preventing the last phase from
happening. You can pray alone now. You can read alone. You have a faith that does
not require anyone, and it feels like maturity, and it is at least partly a scar.

Getting back in is risky in a way the earlier phases were not. In Disconnect you
lost things. In Disorientation you endured. Reconnect asks you to walk back into a
room with people — possibly people like the ones who hurt you — and let yourself be
known in your new shape, which means being available to be wounded again.

So do it small and do it slowly. Not a return to full institutional participation.
One table. Two or three people who know the actual story, with whom you can be
honest about where you have been without having to summarise it into something
respectable. The New Testament's picture is a household, not an auditorium, and the
practices that rebuild belonging are the four ordinary ones: something taught,
somebody's company, a meal, a prayer.

And be realistic about fit. If your break was from a particular kind of community,
you may not be able to go back to that kind, and that is not a moral failure. There
are many rooms. What there is not, in the New Testament, is a version of this faith
conducted alone. Every image is corporate — body, household, temple built of living
stones, a city. You were not designed to finish this by yourself, and the isolation
that feels like peace right now is, in the long run, another form of the wilderness.

## In the Body

The Body's reconnection question is the largest one in front of world Christianity,
and it is not primarily about the West.

There are more Christians alive than at any point in history, spread across more
cultures, speaking more languages, and more thoroughly fragmented into separate
bodies than at any time since Pentecost. Estimates of distinct denominations run
into the tens of thousands. The wounds of 1054 and 1517 are unhealed. And on top of
those, the newer fractures — the splits of the last decade, running through
denominations and congregations and families.

Jesus prayed, on the last night, that they may all be one, and gave the reason: that
the world may believe. He tied the credibility of the message to the visible unity
of the messengers, which is either the most inconvenient sentence in the Gospels or
the most important, and probably both.

Nothing in the present moment suggests institutional reunion is coming. But
something else is happening that is worth naming, because it is genuinely new.
Christians are increasingly encountering one another across lines that were sealed
for centuries — Pentecostals reading the desert fathers, evangelicals discovering
the daily office, Catholics and Protestants working side by side in the same slums,
persecuted churches whose members do not have the luxury of asking each other's
tradition before helping. The fragmentation of the institutions has, oddly,
coincided with a great deal of traffic between them.

That is not unity. It might be the beginning of the recognition that has to precede
it. And the direction of travel is worth noticing: the Body is being reconnected
from underneath, at the level of ordinary believers sharing practices and burdens,
rather than from above by agreement. Which is more or less how Acts 2 did it.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

He did not leave them a structure. He left them Himself, in a form that could not be
confiscated.

That is the thing about Pentecost that outlasts everything else. The temple could be
destroyed and was, forty years later. The apostles could be killed and were. The
buildings could be burned, the institutions corrupted, the councils divided, the
whole visible apparatus of the faith shaken to pieces repeatedly across twenty
centuries — and none of that reached the arrangement made in the upstairs room,
because the arrangement was a Spirit distributed onto individual people who could be
scattered and could not be centralised.

What cannot be shaken is the indwelling. Not the health of any institution, not your ability
to find a community, not the state of the institutions. The fire sat on each of
them, and it has not been withdrawn.

## For Reflection

1. Has solitude become safe? What did the wilderness teach you not to need?
2. What would one small table look like — two or three people who know the actual
   story?
3. Which of the four ordinary practices — teaching, company, a meal, prayer — is
   most missing from your life right now?
