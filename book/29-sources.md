# Sources and Further Reading

This book is not a work of scholarship and does not pretend to be. But it leans on
other people's thinking throughout, and where it does, they are named in the text.
This page gathers them in one place — partly so the credit is properly recorded,
and partly because the reader who wants to go further should not have to hunt back
through the chapters for a name half-remembered.

Where a living or in-copyright author's framework is used, it is paraphrased and
attributed, never quoted.

---

## The two engines

**Phyllis Tickle,** *The Great Emergence: How Christianity Is Changing and Why*
(Baker, 2008). The five-hundred-year rummage sale, which Tickle credits to the
Anglican bishop **Mark Dyer**, and the observation that each upheaval turns on the
question of authority. Chapter 2 depends on this book. Tickle's thesis is
contested by historians, and rightly treated here as an interpretive lens rather
than a law.

**Bill Hybels,** *Holy Discontent* (Zondervan, 2007). The idea that a specific,
permanent inability to accept a specific wrong is often a calling. Chapter 4
discusses both the idea and the author's subsequent fall.

## The shape of transition

**Walter Brueggemann,** *The Message of the Psalms* (Augsburg, 1984) and
*Spirituality of the Psalms* (Fortress, 2002). Psalms of orientation,
disorientation and new orientation — the framework nearest to this book's spine.

**William Bridges,** *Transitions* (1980) and *Managing Transitions* (1991). Every
transition begins with an ending, passes through a neutral zone, and only then
reaches a new beginning. The insistence that the ending comes first is his, and
Chapter 3 owes it the most.

## The dark night

**John of the Cross,** *The Dark Night of the Soul* and *The Ascent of Mount
Carmel* (c. 1578–85). Public domain; several good modern translations.

**Teresa of Ávila,** *The Interior Castle* (1577) and *The Book of Her Life*.
Public domain.

Chapter 13's insistence that the dark night is not depression follows the
tradition's own distinction. It is not a clinical judgement, and nothing in that
chapter should be read as one.

## The Church in its upheavals

**Augustine,** *The City of God* (413–426) and *Confessions* (c. 400). The two
cities, and the restless heart. **Jerome's** letters record the shock of 410.

**Benedict,** *The Rule* (c. 530).

**Athanasius,** *On the Incarnation* (c. 318). The Nicene and Chalcedonian
definitions are in any standard collection of the creeds.

**Julian of Norwich,** *Revelations of Divine Love* (c. 1395). **Thomas à Kempis,**
*The Imitation of Christ* (c. 1418). **Blaise Pascal,** *Pensées* (1670). All
public domain.

**Martin Luther:** the Ninety-Five Theses (1517), the preface to Romans, and the
Worms testimony (1521). **William Tyndale's** plough-boy remark is reported by
Foxe. Readers wanting a reliable modern narrative of the period should go to a
working historian rather than to this book.

**John Wesley's** *Journal* for 24 May 1738 (Aldersgate). **Jonathan Edwards,**
*A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections* (1746) — the source of the test this
book keeps returning to: not the intensity of the experience but a changed life
over time. **John Newton's** *Authentic Narrative* (1764).

**Karl Barth,** *The Epistle to the Romans* (2nd edn, 1922); the Barmen Declaration
(1934), which he largely drafted. **Dietrich Bonhoeffer,** *The Cost of
Discipleship* (1937) — costly and cheap grace — and *Letters and Papers from
Prison*. Bonhoeffer was hanged at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945.

**Scot McKnight,** *Five Streams of the Emerging Church* (Christianity Today,
February 2007). Chapter 2's account of the currents running through the emerging
Western church.

## Scripture

All Scripture is from the **World English Bible**, which is in the public domain.
The divine name is rendered *the LORD* throughout.

## A note on the claims in this book

The historical and demographic statements in these pages — the scale of the global
Church, the movement of Christianity's centre of gravity toward Africa and Asia,
the decline of religious affiliation across the West, the abuse crises and their
institutional handling — are drawn from the general body of reporting and research
on those subjects and are given here in round terms, without figures I have not
verified.

Readers who want the numbers should go to the standing sources rather than trust a
book like this one for them: the *World Christian Encyclopaedia* and the Center for
the Study of Global Christianity for global demographics; Pew Research Center and
the British Social Attitudes survey for Western affiliation trends; and, for the
abuse crises, the published independent reviews and inquiry reports themselves,
which are a harder and more necessary read than any summary.

Where this book says *something like* or *roughly* or *by most estimates*, it means
it. Where it has been more confident than the evidence warrants, the fault is mine
and I would rather be told.
