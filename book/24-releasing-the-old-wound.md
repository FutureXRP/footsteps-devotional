# Chapter Twenty-Four

## Releasing the Old Wound

Peter thinks he is being generous.

He comes to Jesus with a number already in his hand, which is how you can tell he
has been working on this. Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I
forgive him? Until seven times?

The rabbinic convention of the day, as far as we can reconstruct it, suggested three.
Peter has more than doubled it. He is expecting to be commended.

Jesus says: I don't tell you until seven times, but until seventy times seven.

Which is not a higher number so much as the abolition of counting. The moment you
are keeping a tally, you have not forgiven anything; you have deferred a judgement
and are tracking it. Four hundred and ninety is a number nobody can hold in their
head, and that is the point.

And then, because Peter is not the only one who needed it, He tells a story.

A servant owes his king ten thousand talents. The figure is deliberately absurd — a
talent was something like twenty years of a labourer's wages, and there are ten
thousand of them. It is not a debt; it is a national budget. The man cannot pay,
begs for patience, and the king does something he did not ask for: he does not
reschedule the debt, he cancels it.

The man goes out and finds a fellow servant who owes him a hundred denarii — about
three months' wages, a real amount, the kind of sum you could genuinely resent — and
takes him by the throat.

The contrast is not that the second debt was nothing. It was substantial. It was
simply not in the same universe as the one that had just been written off.

---

Now, this teaching has been used to injure people, and a chapter on it has to say so
before it says anything else.

Forgiveness has been demanded of abused people by the institutions that failed them,
on a timetable, as a condition of being considered spiritually healthy. It has been
used to end conversations that needed to continue, to protect the powerful from
consequence, and to send people back into danger. That is not what the parable is
about, and the misuse is severe enough that some readers will have had to brace
themselves at the chapter title.

So, four things that forgiveness is not.

**It is not saying it was acceptable.** Joseph forgave and still said *you meant
evil against me*, in those words, to their faces. Naming the wrong accurately is a
precondition of forgiving it, not a violation of it. You cannot release something
you have not first identified.

**It is not the removal of consequences.** Forgiveness is personal; justice is
public. A person can be forgiven and still be reported, prosecuted, removed from
office, and kept away from the people they harmed. The Church has confused these two
catastrophically and repeatedly, and every abuse scandal of the last thirty years
has that confusion somewhere in it.

**It is not reconciliation.** Reconciliation takes two people and requires
repentance from one of them. Forgiveness is something you can do alone, about
someone who is dead, or unrepentant, or dangerous. Paul's own formula concedes the
limit — if it is possible, as much as it depends on you, live at peace — which
plainly allows that sometimes it is not possible and does not depend on you.

**And it is not a feeling that arrives on schedule.** It is a decision that has to be
made repeatedly, often about the same offence, sometimes for years. That is not
failure. That is what seventy times seven describes.

---

With those in place, here is why this chapter is in the Reconnect section rather
than anywhere else.

Unforgiveness is the most common reason people stall at the last phase.

You can do everything else. You can survive the break, endure the wilderness,
rebuild your bearings, recover your identity, find a table. And if there is a person
you are still holding, the reconnection stays partial, because a grievance is a
relationship — an intense, ongoing, one-sided relationship that requires constant
maintenance and gives nothing back.

That is the practical case, and it is why the tradition has always insisted this is
for your sake at least as much as theirs. The person who wronged you is very often
unaware, uninterested, or dead. The maintenance is all yours. You are the only one
paying the storage costs on something you did not choose to own.

And Jesus attaches a warning to the parable that is genuinely frightening and should
not be softened: the unforgiving servant is handed over to the tormentors. Whatever
else that means, it describes something observable. People who cannot release an
injury are tormented by it, daily, for decades, and the torment is administered
from inside.

---

The relief comes from the direction the parable points, which is not toward the
offence at all.

The servant's problem was not that he was cruel. His problem was that he had not
grasped the size of what had been done for him. If ten thousand talents had actually
landed on him — if he had walked out of that room understanding what had just
happened — the hundred denarii would have been unrecognisable as a grievance.

Which means the route to forgiving is almost never a direct assault on the
resentment. It is the slow enlargement of your sense of what you have been forgiven.
People who cannot forgive are not, in general, people who need to try harder. They
are people for whom grace is a doctrine rather than an experience, and the two
produce entirely different capacities.

## In You

Name the person. You know who it is; there is usually one, occasionally two, and
almost everyone reading this has already thought of them.

Then do the work in the right order, because the order is what makes it survivable.

**First, say what was actually done.** In plain words, without minimising, to God and
probably to one other human being. The psalms are full of this and God kept every
one of them. You cannot release something you are still describing euphemistically.

**Then separate the four things above.** Ask specifically: am I refusing to forgive,
or am I refusing to reconcile, or am I refusing to drop a consequence, or am I
waiting for a feeling? Those are four different refusals and only one of them is the
thing being asked of you. An enormous amount of the guilt people carry here
evaporates when they realise they have been trying to do something that was never
required.

**Then make the decision, and expect to make it again.** Not once, decisively, with
a sense of release. Most people make it, feel the resentment return within a week,
and conclude it did not work. It worked. Seventy times seven is not hyperbole about
a generous person; it is a realistic description of what releasing a serious injury
takes.

**And go at the enlargement rather than the grievance.** Spend the time on the ten
thousand talents. What has actually been forgiven you — not in theory, in specifics?
That is slower and less satisfying than confronting the resentment head-on, and it
is the only thing that has ever worked.

One more, gently. If the wound is severe — abuse, violence, the loss of a child to
someone's negligence — this is not a chapter you should attempt alone or quickly,
and there is no shame in needing years and help. Nothing here is a schedule. The
instruction to forgive is not an instruction to hurry.

## In the Body

The Church's unfinished forgiveness is a thousand years old and counting, and it is
the most visible obstacle to its witness.

The mutual excommunications of 1054 were formally lifted in 1965 — nine hundred and
eleven years later — and the two halves of the Church still do not share a table.
The wounds of the sixteenth century are less raw but not healed. And on top of those
sit the newer ones: the denominations that split in the last decade and cannot now be
in a room together, the congregations that divided over a decision in 2021 and have
not spoken since, the families inside the Church split along political lines.

There is also a category the Body has barely begun and which is more important than
any of the above: the forgiveness that the Church must ask for rather than extend.
Abuse survivors. The peoples the faith arrived among alongside conquest. The
communities the Church stood against when it should have stood with them. In every
one of those cases the Body is not the injured party, and the appropriate act is not
magnanimity but repentance, restitution and the patient acceptance that forgiveness
belongs to the injured and cannot be requested on a timetable.

That distinction matters enormously and the Body keeps getting it backwards. An
institution calling for reconciliation while resisting accountability is the
unforgiving servant with the roles reversed — demanding the hundred denarii be
written off while never acknowledging the ten thousand talents it owes.

Where the Church has done this properly — where it has told the truth about its own
record, made restitution it could not afford, and waited without demanding
absolution — it has recovered credibility that no amount of strategy could buy.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

The debt was cancelled before anybody deserved it, and nothing since has reopened
it.

That is the ground under this whole chapter. Not your success at forgiving, which
will be partial and slow. Not the other person's repentance, which may never come.
What holds is a settlement made at the cross about a sum nobody could pay, entered
in full, and not subject to review.

Peter, who asked about seven times, would later deny Him three times in a courtyard
and be handed breakfast on a beach. He asked the question as a man managing a
budget, and he found out what he was actually standing inside.

You are not being asked to generate forgiveness out of your own reserves. You are
being asked to pass on a fraction of something you have already been given in an
amount you have not finished counting.

## For Reflection

1. Name the person. What, exactly, was done — in plain words, without softening?
2. Which of the four are you actually refusing: forgiveness, reconciliation, a
   consequence, or a feeling? Only one of them is being asked of you.
3. What has been forgiven you? Be specific rather than general. How long is that
   list?
