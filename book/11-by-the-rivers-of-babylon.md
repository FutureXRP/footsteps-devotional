# Chapter Eleven

## By the Rivers of Babylon

They sat down by the canals and could not sing.

Babylon was not a wasteland. That is the first thing to correct. It was the most
advanced city on earth — irrigation works, walls you could drive a chariot along,
a functioning economy, gardens famous across the world. The exiles were not in a
desert. Many of them, within a generation, were doing well.

That is what makes Psalm 137 so devastating. The problem was never the conditions.

By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down. Yes, we wept, when we remembered Zion.
On the willows in that land we hung up our harps. For there, those who led us
captive asked us for songs, and those who tormented us demanded songs of joy: Sing
us one of the songs of Zion!

And then the question the whole exile turns on:

How can we sing the LORD's song in a foreign land?

It is not rhetorical self-pity. It is a genuine theological emergency. Their songs
were temple songs, tied to a building that was now rubble, sung by a people
defined by a land they were no longer in, addressed to a God whose reputation in
the ancient world had just been publicly destroyed. In the logic of that era, a
defeated nation had a defeated god. The Babylonians were not being cruel by
accident when they asked for a song of Zion. They were asking the losers to perform
their own humiliation.

---

Everything a person can build an identity on, they had lost, and it is worth
listing them because your own losses will map onto this list more closely than you
expect.

**The land.** The place that told them who they were, marched out of in a column.

**The king.** The Davidic line, with its promise of permanence, ended in a scene
the historians report without flinching: Zedekiah watched his sons killed, and then
his eyes were put out, so that the last thing he ever saw was that.

**The temple.** Not a building they were fond of. The address where God had said He
would be. Burned.

Take those three away and the question is not *how do we cope*. The question is
whether anything they had believed was true. If God's promises were attached to a
land, a line and a building, and all three are gone, then either God has failed or
God was never what they thought He was.

That is where a deconstruction actually happens. Not in an argument. In a burnt-out
capital, where the categories you used to think with have stopped working.

---

Into that, Jeremiah sends a letter, and it may be the least welcome piece of
pastoral advice in Scripture.

The exiles were being told by other prophets — popular ones — that this would be
brief. Two years, hold on, do not settle. Jeremiah writes to say it will be
seventy, which is to say that most of the adults reading the letter will die there.

And then he tells them what to do with the seventy years. Build houses and live in
them. Plant gardens and eat their fruit. Marry, have children, arrange marriages
for those children, multiply and do not decrease. And then the sentence that must
have stopped the room: seek the peace of the city where I have caused you to be
carried away captive, and pray to the LORD for it; for in its peace you will have
peace.

Pray for Babylon. Not endure Babylon. Not resist Babylon. Seek its welfare, because
your welfare is now inside its welfare.

Understand what he is refusing. He is refusing both of the available strategies. He
will not let them treat exile as temporary — no camping in the departure lounge for
seventy years, no life on hold. And he will not let them assimilate — the letter
assumes they remain distinctly the LORD's people, praying to the LORD for a pagan
city. He is telling them to live a full and fruitful life in a place they did not
choose and cannot leave, without pretending it is home and without pretending it is
hell.

That is the single most useful instruction in this book for anyone in a long
disorientation. Plant a garden. You are going to be here a while.

---

There is a corrective the exile made that could not have been made any other way,
and it is one of the great turns in the history of faith.

In Babylon there was no temple, no sacrifice, no priesthood functioning as it had.
And out of that deprivation came the practices that carried Judaism and then
Christianity ever after: the gathering of people around a text rather than an
altar. The reading and exposition of Scripture. Prayer as the offering when no
offering was possible. Something like the synagogue, born in the absence of the
temple, and portable in a way the temple never was.

Ezekiel, in Babylon, sees the glory of God by a canal in Chaldea — which was
theologically impossible under the old assumptions and is the whole point. God was
not confined to the address He had given. The exiles discovered, by losing the
building, that they had never been dealing with a local deity, and the faith became
something that could travel.

They did not learn that in Jerusalem. They could not have. Some things are only
visible from Babylon.

## In You

You may be in a Babylon — a situation you did not choose, cannot leave, and did not
expect to last this long. An illness that has stopped being an episode and become a
life. A city you moved to for someone else. A marriage that is not ending and is
not what you hoped. A season of faith in which the songs you used to sing have gone
silent in your mouth.

Two instincts will present themselves, and Jeremiah blocks both.

The first is to live provisionally. To not unpack. To decline invitations, defer
decisions, put things off until this is over, and treat the present years as a
waiting room rather than as your life. People do this for a decade. It is a slow
way of not being present for your own existence, and if the exile is long you will
have missed the only years you were given.

The second is to conclude that since you are here, you might as well become
Babylonian — to let the place set your values because resisting is exhausting.

Between them is the letter. Build a house. Plant a garden. Make something that
takes time and assumes a future in the very place you did not want to be. Not
because you have made peace with the situation, but because the God who sent the
letter is the God who has caused you to be here, and He has not put your life on
hold even though you have.

And about the songs. If you cannot sing right now, do not force it. Notice what the
psalm does: it is itself a song, sung by people who said they could not sing. The
complaint became the music. That is the discovery of this phase — that lament is
not the absence of worship but a form of it, and that God accepted a psalm which
ends in one of the ugliest wishes in Scripture and put it in the book without
sanding it down. He can take what you actually feel. He has printed worse.

## In the Body

Western Christianity is in Babylon, and its central mistake has been reading the
seventy years as two.

For most of a century the operating assumption has been that this is a downturn.
A dip. Something a better strategy will reverse — the right programme, the right
leader, the right cultural moment, the right election. And so an enormous amount of
the Church's energy has gone into the departure lounge: holding structures together
in the expectation of a return to conditions that are not coming back.

Jeremiah's letter suggests a different posture, and the parts of the Church that
have adopted it are the ones showing life. Stop asking how to get back to
Jerusalem. Ask what to build here.

And notice what the first exile produced, because it is the best argument for hope
in this section. Under conditions of total institutional loss, the people of God
invented the portable forms that have carried the faith ever since. The synagogue,
the primacy of the text, the gathered congregation, prayer as the central offering
— all of it is exile technology. The Church's own forms have a similar history:
the monastic rule came out of the fall of Rome, not out of its glory. The
vernacular Bible came out of a crisis of authority. The modern missionary movement
came out of a Church that had lost its national confidence.

There is no reason to think this exile will be different, except that nobody
currently alive gets to see what it produces. The Christians in Babylon who did the
building mostly died in Babylon. Their grandchildren went home carrying something
better than what had burned.

Meanwhile, the Body should notice that most of it is not in exile at all. The
Church in Nigeria, Brazil, Korea, China and Indonesia is not asking how to sing in a
foreign land; it is singing. Western Christianity's crisis is real and it is
provincial. The song did not stop. It moved.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Ezekiel saw the glory of the LORD by a canal in Babylon.

That vision is the answer to Psalm 137's question, arriving in the same generation
that asked it. How can we sing the LORD's song in a foreign land? Because the LORD
is in the foreign land. He did not stay in the ruins of the building. He went with
the column of prisoners, and He was seen four hundred miles from His own address by
a priest who no longer had a temple to serve in.

What cannot be shaken is the presence that travels. Everything they had used to
locate God — the land, the line, the building — turned out to be a made thing, and
every made thing can be shaken. What remained was the God who had never actually
been contained by any of them, and who has been meeting exiles in unlikely
geographies ever since.

You have not been left behind at the old address. He is not there either.

## For Reflection

1. What have you been treating as temporary that has, in fact, become your life?
2. What would it look like to plant a garden here — to build something that assumes
   a future in the place you did not choose?
3. What did you lose that you had confused with God Himself?
