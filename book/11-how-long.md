# Chapter Eleven

## How Long?

Habakkuk does not ease into his book. He opens it with a shout.

How long, LORD, will I cry, and you will not hear? I cry out to you Violence! and
you will not save. Why do you show me iniquity, and look at perversity?

There is no preamble, no formula of approach, no throat-clearing about God's
greatness before the complaint. A prophet is looking at violence and corruption in
his own nation, looking up at a heaven that appears to be doing nothing whatever
about it, and demanding to know why. And he does not whisper it apologetically. He
hurls it.

The first remarkable thing is that this is in the Bible. The second is that God
answers. The third is that the answer is far worse than the silence.

God says He is raising up the Chaldeans — a bitter and hasty nation, ruthless,
whose horses are swifter than leopards — and that they are the answer to the
injustice Habakkuk complained about. In other words: I am dealing with it, and I am
dealing with it by means of something more terrible than the thing you asked me
about.

Habakkuk's second complaint is therefore even better than his first. You are of
purer eyes than to behold evil, he says — how can you look on those who deal
treacherously, and hold your peace when the wicked swallows up the man more
righteous than he?

And then he does the thing that makes him the patron of everyone stuck in this
phase. He says: I will stand at my watch, and set myself on the ramparts, and will
look out to see what He will say to me.

He does not leave. He does not resolve it. He goes up on the wall and waits.

---

Here is what has to be said plainly, because a great deal of Christian
encouragement is dishonest about it: sometimes God does not answer, and sometimes
when He answers He does not explain.

Paul asked three times for the thorn to be removed. He never tells us what it was —
an illness, an opponent, a chronic torment of some kind — and the reticence is
itself instructive, because it makes the passage fit everyone. He prayed
specifically, repeatedly, for a specific removal. The answer was no. What he got
instead was a sentence about grace being sufficient and strength being made perfect
in weakness, which is a magnificent thing to receive and is not what he asked for.
The thorn stayed. It was still there when he wrote the letter and there is no
record of it ever leaving.

Job asked for a hearing and got a whirlwind full of questions about wild goats and
the storehouses of snow, and not one word about the actual thing — not one sentence
explaining the wager, the losses, the children. He is given God and denied the
explanation, and the strange testimony of the book is that he found this sufficient,
which is either the deepest thing in Scripture or the hardest, and is probably both.

The psalmist of Psalm 88, alone in the whole psalter, ends in the dark. He works
through his complaint and reaches the last line and there is no turn, no
nevertheless, no rescue. The final word of the psalm is darkness. Somebody in the
worshipping community of Israel wrote a prayer that does not resolve, and the
editors of the psalter kept it.

If your prayer has found no sunrise, there is a psalm that ends where you are.

---

But look again at what God actually gave Habakkuk on the ramparts, because it is
the most useful thing in the chapter.

The vision is yet for the appointed time, He says. It hurries toward the end, and
won't prove false. Though it takes time, wait for it; because it will surely come.
It won't delay.

He does not give a date. He gives an appointment. There is a fixed hour, set by
God, and the waiting has an end that is already determined even though it has not
been disclosed. That is a different thing from a timetable, and it is what the phase
actually runs on.

The difference matters more than it sounds. A timetable lets you count down, which
is a way of managing the wait by shrinking it. An appointment lets you wait, which
means enduring an unknown duration on the strength of someone else's reliability.
The first is a mechanism. The second is a relationship, and it is the only one on
offer.

And then, at the end, Habakkuk sings. It is one of the most defiant things in
Scripture, and every clause of it is a subtraction. Though the fig tree doesn't
flourish, nor fruit be in the vines, though the labour of the olive fails, the
fields yield no food, the flocks are cut off from the fold, and there is no herd in
the stalls — yet I will rejoice in the LORD. I will be joyful in the God of my
salvation.

That is a man singing in a stripped landscape, with the invasion still coming, with
nothing whatever having improved between chapter one and chapter three except him.

## In You

You have prayed for something and it has not happened, and you have started to
wonder what that says about you.

Here is what it does not say. It does not say your faith is insufficient. The
apostle Paul had a thorn that stayed, and if a deficit of faith is the explanation
for unanswered prayer then it is difficult to see who qualifies. It does not say
you asked wrongly, necessarily, or that there is a technique you have missed. The
market in techniques for making God answer is old and profitable and it preys
precisely on people at this point in the journey.

What the waiting does is separate two things that were fused: your love for God and
your expectation of what He would do. While the two are joined you cannot tell them
apart, and neither can you. The wait pulls them apart, painfully, and what is left
after the expectation has been stripped is the answer to a question you would never
have asked voluntarily — do I want Him, or do I want the outcome I was using Him
to reach?

That is a brutal question and you do not have to answer it today. But it is the
question this phase is asking, and it is why Habakkuk's ending is so significant.
Every fig tree in his song is an unmet expectation. He lists them all, admits none
of them arrived, and worships anyway, which means he has found something to hold on
to that was not any of the things he asked for.

Meanwhile, permission: go up on the ramparts and complain. Loudly, specifically,
without the preamble. God is not fragile and He has demonstrated an unusual
tolerance for being shouted at by people who have not stopped talking to Him. What
He seems unable to work with is the polite distance we adopt when we have decided
He is not going to answer and have stopped bringing it up.

## In the Body

The Church's how-long question is currently pointed at its own decline, and it has
been asked for two generations without an answer.

How long does a body wait while its numbers fall? At what point does faithful
endurance become denial? Congregations across the West are asking this in the most
concrete possible terms — at what point do fifteen people in a building for four
hundred stop being a remnant and start being an ending. There is no formula for it,
and pretending there is has produced a great deal of cruelty in both directions:
churches shamed for closing, and churches kept on life support long past the point
where the ministry was to the building rather than to anyone alive.

There is a second how-long, older and heavier, and it belongs to the martyrs under
the altar in Revelation, who ask how long before their blood is answered for. It is
being asked now by Christians in northern Nigeria, in Eritrean shipping containers,
in provinces of China where the surveillance is total. They are not asking about
attendance figures. They are asking the Habakkuk question in its original form, and
the Western Church's version of it should be held next to theirs with some humility.

And the answer given in Revelation is the same one given on the ramparts: an
appointed time, and a robe, and the instruction to rest a little longer. Not an
explanation. Not a date. The Church has never been given a date, has repeatedly
invented them, and has been wrong every single time — which is itself a two-thousand
year demonstration that the appointment is not ours to schedule.

What the Body is offered in the meantime is Habakkuk's posture. Stand on the wall.
Keep watching. Say the complaint out loud rather than managing the decline in
silence. The prophet who shouted at God is the one who was still there to hear the
answer.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

The appointed time itself.

That is the thing in this chapter that does not move. Not the fig tree, not the
vines, not the flocks — Habakkuk watched every one of those go and said so in the
song. What held was a horizon God had already fixed, which will surely come and
will not delay past its hour, whether or not anyone waiting can see it from here.

The wait is long. The silence is heavy. Neither of them has any power over the
hour. It was set before you started waiting and it is not being revised by how the
waiting is going.

## For Reflection

1. What have you stopped bringing to God because you decided He was not going to
   answer?
2. Can you separate your love for God from your expectation of what He would do?
   What is left?
3. Habakkuk listed every unmet expectation by name before he worshipped. What is on
   your list?
