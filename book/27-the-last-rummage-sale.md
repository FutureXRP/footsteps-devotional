# PART FIVE — WHAT CANNOT BE SHAKEN

> *The unshakable kingdom, and a word for anyone still in the middle of it.*

Two chapters. The first is about the last upheaval, which is the end of all of
them. The second is for the reader who has read this whole book from inside a
shaking that has not stopped.

---

# Chapter Twenty-Seven

## The Last Rummage Sale

An old man on a prison island, at the end of a very long life, is shown the end of
everything.

Patmos is a small rocky place in the Aegean and John is there, by his own account,
because of the word of God and the testimony of Jesus. Everything he had known is
gone. Jerusalem has been destroyed. The apostles are dead — executed, most of them.
The churches he writes to in the first chapters are variously compromised,
persecuted, lukewarm, and in at least one case dead while thinking itself alive. The
faith is illegal, small, and under an empire that has no intention of stopping.

And what he is shown is not an escape. That is the thing people get wrong about
Revelation, having been handed a version of it that is essentially about evacuation.

I saw a new heaven and a new earth, he writes.

Not a new heaven only. Earth is in the sentence. And then the direction of travel,
which is the opposite of what most Christians assume: I saw the holy city, New
Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God.

It comes down. Nobody goes up. The movement in the last chapters of the Bible is
God relocating to be with people, permanently, on a renewed earth — and the voice
from the throne says exactly that: Behold, God's dwelling is with people, and He
will dwell with them, and they will be His people.

---

Read against everything this book has covered, the final vision is the last rummage
sale — and it has three features that distinguish it from all the others.

**It is total.** Behold, I am making all things new. Not all new things — the Greek
does not say He is discarding this creation and starting over. He is making the
things that exist new, which is what happened to a body in a garden tomb: the same
one, transformed, still bearing the marks. The pattern of resurrection is applied to
everything.

Which means the material world is not a temporary stage set. It is being renewed,
and so the work done in it is not wasted. Paul's long argument about resurrection
ends with a practical instruction that seems, until you notice the logic, like a
non-sequitur: therefore be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the Lord's
work, because you know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord. The renewal of
all things is the reason the labour is not wasted.

**It is the end of all upheaval.** The one thing the vision insists on, over and
over, is the absence of the things that shake. No more death. No more mourning, or
crying, or pain — for the first things have passed away. No more sea, in the
imagery of the ancient world, which meant chaos and the deep that swallows. No more
night.

And the tenderest sentence in Scripture: He will wipe away every tear from their
eyes.

Notice the intimacy of the verb. Not: there will be no more tears, as a policy. He
wipes them, individually, from faces, which is a gesture between two people who are
close enough to touch. Every tear implies that each one was seen and counted. What
is being described is not the cancellation of your grief but its personal attention.

**And it restores what was lost at the beginning.** The tree of life, which was
fenced off in Genesis 3, appears again on the last page, on both sides of the river,
with its leaves for the healing of the nations. The story does not end where it
started. It ends further on — a garden becomes a city, and the fruit is available
again.

---

There is one more thing to say about that city, because it settles the question this
book has been asking from Chapter Two.

There is no temple in it. John notes it with visible surprise: I saw no temple in
it, for the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb are its temple. And no sun or moon,
because the glory of God illuminates it, and the Lamb is its lamp.

Every religious structure ever built exists to mediate access. Temples, priesthoods,
buildings, institutions, denominations, systems — all of them are scaffolding around
a problem, which is that God is holy and we are not and something has to stand
between. And in the end there is no scaffolding, because there is no problem. They
will see His face.

The made things are gone, exactly as Hebrews said they would be. What is left is
the thing they were all pointing at.

## In the Body

The Church's hope has been distorted in two directions and both of them are
corrected here.

The first distortion is escapism: the reading of Revelation as an evacuation plan,
in which this world is a write-off and the Christian's task is to survive it and
leave. That reading has consequences. It produces disengagement from creation,
indifference to justice, and a version of the faith that has nothing to say about
the material conditions in which most people live. If the world is being burned
rather than renewed, then nothing done in it matters much.

But the city comes down. The nations bring their glory into it. The earth is
renewed rather than replaced. Which means every piece of work the Church does that
belongs to the coming order — every reconciliation, every hungry person fed, every
beauty made, every prisoner visited — is not a holding action. It is an early
instance of the thing that is going to be true permanently.

The second distortion is the opposite: the loss of the hope altogether. Large parts
of Western Christianity became embarrassed about the end. It sounded unsophisticated
and had been abused by people making predictions, and so it quietly disappeared from
the preaching, and what was left was a faith about coping and meaning and community
in the present, which is a small thing to have exchanged it for.

The martyrs did not have that faith. The persecuted church does not have it now, and
this is worth saying plainly: the Christians currently paying the highest price for
the faith are overwhelmingly the ones who talk most about the return of Christ and
the world to come. That is not naivety. A hope that is genuinely about the future is
the only thing that lets a person endure a present that is not going to improve.

The Body is in the middle of a rummage sale that nobody alive will see the end of.
The reason that is bearable is that there is a final one, and it is not a rummage
sale at all — it is a wedding.

## In You

If you are still in the middle of your own shaking, the vision offers two things and
withholds a third.

It offers a horizon. Not a date — the Church has invented dates repeatedly and has
been wrong every time — but a certainty that the shaking has a terminus and that the
terminus is not oblivion. There will be an end. It has been described, in some
detail, and the description is of a place with no more night in it.

It offers the wiping of the tears, individually. That is worth taking personally,
because you have probably wept about things nobody knows about, in rooms by
yourself, and there is a strong temptation to believe those went unrecorded. The
image says otherwise. Every tear. Wiped by hand.

And what it withholds is the explanation. There is no scene in Revelation in which
God sits down and takes His people through why each thing was permitted. Job did not
get that either. What is given instead is His face — they shall see His face — and
the consistent testimony of everyone in Scripture who got that far is that the face
turned out to be the answer rather than a substitute for one.

So the practical use of the last chapter is not comfort in the abstract. It is a
recalibration of weight. Paul, who had been beaten, shipwrecked, imprisoned and left
for dead, said he reckoned that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy
to be compared with the glory which will be revealed toward us. That is a man doing
arithmetic, not a man minimising. He had done the sum, with a full accounting of the
suffering side, and reported the result.

You do not have to feel that today. It is not a feeling. It is a fact about
proportion that you can hold on to on days when nothing in you agrees with it.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Daniel, in exile, interpreting a dream about a succession of empires — gold, silver,
bronze, iron, every one of them apparently permanent to the people living under it —
comes to the end of the sequence and says that in the days of those kings the God of
heaven will set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed.

Every empire in that dream is gone. Nobody has paid tribute to Babylon in
twenty-five centuries.

And the writer of Hebrews, after the passage about the shaking, draws the conclusion
the whole book has been building toward: therefore, receiving a kingdom that can't
be shaken, let us have grace, by which we serve God acceptably.

Receiving. Present tense. Not achieving, not defending, not building — receiving,
which is what you do with something handed to you.

That kingdom is what remains when the sieve has finished. It was never made, so it
cannot be unmade. It was not built by anyone in this book, so nobody in this book
can bring it down. And the shaking that has cost you so much is, in the end, the
process by which everything that is not it gets separated out, so that the thing you
are left holding is the one thing that was never going to fall.

## For Reflection

1. Have you been carrying an escape hope or a renewal hope? What difference would
   the second make to what you do on Monday?
2. What have you wept about that nobody knows? Can you believe that it was counted?
3. Do the arithmetic Paul did — a full accounting of the suffering, and the glory
   set against it. What do you get?
