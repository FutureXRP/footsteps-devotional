# PART ONE — DISCONNECT

> *The break. The old is left behind, and there is no road back.*

Every upheaval begins the same way: something that was joined comes apart. A person
walks out. A hand lets go. A hammer hits a door. This part is about how breaks
start, what they cost, and the difference between the ones that are obedience and
the ones that are only escape.

---

# Chapter Four

## The Fire in the Bones

He has decided to quit.

Jeremiah has been at this for years and it has cost him everything a man can be
charged. He has been beaten and put in the stocks overnight by a temple official.
He is mocked in the street; his own name has become a joke, because he keeps
shouting about violence and destruction and the destruction keeps not arriving on
schedule. He has no wife and no children, by God's own instruction, so there is not
even a house to go home to. And the message he carries is one nobody wants and
nobody believes.

So he does what any sane person does. He resolves to stop. I will not make mention
of Him, he says, nor speak any more in His name.

And then this, one of the most honest sentences in Scripture:

Then in my heart it is as if there were a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I
am weary with holding it in, and I cannot.

He tried. That is the part people miss. He is not describing enthusiasm. He is
describing a man who genuinely attempted to shut his mouth and physically could
not manage it. The fire was not something he generated. It was something he was
carrying, and it burned worse when contained.

---

Almost every upheaval begins at a fire like that.

Bill Hybels wrote a book about this in 2007 and called it *holy discontent*. His
argument, in short, was that God gives certain people a specific and permanent
inability to accept a specific wrong thing — and that this intolerable thing, the
one that makes you slam your hand on the table, is often the clearest indication of
what you were made for. He liked to reach for the old cartoon sailor for the
sound of it: *that's all I can stands, I can't stands no more.* The Popeye moment.
The point at which putting up with it stops being possible.

I am going to name the obvious thing rather than walk past it, because this book
has asked you to look at the Church's failures squarely and it would be cowardly to
make an exception here. Hybels resigned from the church he founded in 2018, after
allegations of sexual misconduct going back decades. He denied them. An independent
review commissioned by the church concluded the following year that the allegations
were credible; the entire elder board and the two pastors named to succeed him
resigned. He is, in other words, precisely the kind of trusted leader whose fall is
part of why the authority question in this book is being asked at all.

So why keep the phrase? Because the phrase was never his. He named something the
prophets had been doing for twenty-seven centuries, and the naming was good, and it
does not become false because the man who named it failed. This is the same
discipline the whole book runs on: the pastor who failed is not identical to the
truth he mishandled. If we could only use ideas whose authors finished well, we
would lose most of the Church's vocabulary — and, as it happens, most of the
Psalms.

But there is a sharper reason to leave it standing, and it is the point of this
chapter. A fire in the bones is not self-authenticating. It does not certify the
man carrying it. Someone can name holy discontent accurately, teach it well, build
something enormous on it, and still fail the tests that are about to follow — and
the tests are the reason this chapter does not end with the invitation to go and
burn something down.

Hold the idea. Test the fire. Never confuse the two.

Because the thing itself is everywhere in the prophets, and they carry it without
help. Amos was a
herdsman and a tender of sycamore figs, not a professional anything, and what got
him out of Tekoa was that he had seen people sold for a pair of sandals and could
not unsee it. Nehemiah heard a traveller's report about a wall in a city he had
never lived in and sat down and wept and then fasted for days. Moses saw an
Egyptian beating a Hebrew and something in him detonated — badly, in that instance,
and forty years early, but the fire was real.

Jesus, in the temple courts, made a whip out of cords. Take a moment with that
detail: making a whip out of cords is not a flash of temper. It takes minutes. He
was angry the whole time He was braiding it. The tables went over because the house
of prayer had been turned into a market and He could not stand in the middle of it
and do nothing.

---

But not every fire is holy, and this is where the chapter has to slow down, because
the language of righteous anger is the most abused language in the Church.

Numbers 11 puts two things side by side deliberately. The people complain, and fire
falls among them in judgement. Moses complains — bitterly, at length, saying he
would rather die than carry this people another mile — and God responds by giving
him seventy elders to help. Same posture, from outside. Utterly different thing.

The difference is not intensity and it is not politeness. It is direction. Holy
discontent runs *toward* the thing that is wrong and takes responsibility for it.
Grumbling runs away from a cost and takes responsibility for nothing. The murmurers
in the wilderness wanted Egypt back; they were not angry about injustice, they were
angry about the menu. Moses was in agony over a burden he was still carrying while
he complained.

Four tests, and they are worth applying before you let a fire move you.

**What is it about?** Holy discontent is almost always about someone else's harm.
The counterfeit is almost always about your own comfort, dressed up in the
vocabulary of principle.

**Does it cost you?** Amos went to Bethel, where the priest of the royal shrine
told him to get out and prophesy somewhere he could earn a living. A discontent
that positions you well, wins you an audience, and costs you nothing should be
examined with real suspicion.

**Does it persist when nobody is watching?** Jeremiah's fire burned hardest when he
was alone, trying to quit. Outrage that requires an audience to stay lit is not a
fire in the bones. It is a performance with a fuel supply.

**Does it grieve?** This is the quietest of the four and the most revealing. Holy
discontent mourns the thing it is angry about. Jesus wept over Jerusalem in the
same week He overturned the tables. Anger that has no sorrow underneath it has
usually stopped being about the wrong and started being about the self.

## In You

There is probably something you cannot stand. Name it precisely.

Not the general list of things that are wrong with the world — you have opinions
about those, and opinions are cheap. I mean the specific thing that makes your
chest tighten. The thing you bring up at dinner parties without meaning to. The
situation you cannot walk past. Kids without fathers. Women spoken to the way your
mother was spoken to. The particular loneliness of old men. Addiction. Whatever it
is, it has been there a long time, and you may have been apologising for it.

Stop apologising for it. Test it against the four questions, honestly, and if it
survives them, treat it as information rather than as a personality flaw.

Because here is what usually happens instead. Most people spend years managing
their fire. Turning it down. Explaining to themselves why now is not the time,
why they are not qualified, why someone better will handle it. And it works, mostly
— you can hold it in for a decade. What you cannot do is hold it in without cost.
Jeremiah's word for it is *weary*. I am weary with holding it in. That particular
tiredness, the exhaustion of suppressing something rather than of doing something,
is one of the most common and least diagnosed conditions among people who love God.

One caution, because this is where people get hurt. A fire in the bones is a
reason to move; it is not a licence to detonate. Moses acted on his the first time
and killed a man and lost forty years. The discontent was real. The timing and the
method were catastrophic. If the fire in you has been burning a while, the next
step is almost never the dramatic one — it is telling one trusted person, and
letting them test it with you.

## In the Body

The Church's great renewals almost all trace back to somebody who could not stand
something any longer.

A German monk could not stand the traffic in forgiveness. A young Englishman could
not stand that the plough boy could not read the Scriptures in his own language,
and was strangled and burned for fixing it. Wilberforce could not stand the slave
trade and spent forty-six years of parliamentary life on the single question. A
generation of believers could not stand that the gospel had been carried to their
own nation and no further, and the modern missionary movement came out of that
refusal.

None of them were institutionally authorised to be discontent. That is the pattern
and it should make the Church nervous, because it means the fire that renews the
body almost never originates at its centre. It comes from a herdsman in Tekoa, a
cupbearer in Susa, a friar in Assisi who strips in the town square, a monk in a
provincial university. The centre's usual first response is to tell them to go
prophesy somewhere else.

Which is the challenge to the Church now, in the middle of its rummage sale. There
are people inside it at this moment who cannot stand something: the way abuse was
handled and in places is still being handled, the fusion of the gospel with
partisan identity, the treatment of the poor as a programme rather than as the Lord
in disguise, the loneliness that a culture has manufactured and the Church has
largely failed to answer. Some of that discontent is grumbling and will burn itself
out on the internet. Some of it is a fire in the bones, and the body's own future
depends on whether it can tell the difference and whether it can bear to be told
the truth by people it did not appoint.

The historic failure mode is not that the Church lacks prophets. It is that it
identifies them a century late, when the cost of listening has passed.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Jeremiah's ministry, judged by any visible standard, failed. The city fell. The
temple burned. The people were marched to Babylon. He was carried off to Egypt
against his will by the last of his own countrymen, and tradition says he died
there. Nobody listened.

And the fire was still not his. That is the thing that holds here. He did not light
it, he could not put it out, and its worth was never dependent on the response it
got. What cannot be shaken is the word itself — the thing God puts in a person that
outlives the person's reputation, results and lifetime. Jeremiah's word is still
being read twenty-six centuries after the audience that ignored it turned to dust.

The fire in your bones does not belong to you either. That is bad news for your
comfort and very good news for your fear of failure.

## For Reflection

1. What is the specific thing you cannot stand? How long have you been apologising
   for caring about it?
2. Run your discontent through the four tests — whose harm, what cost, does it
   persist alone, does it grieve. What did that reveal?
3. Are you weary from doing something, or weary from holding something in?
