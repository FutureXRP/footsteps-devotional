# Chapter Fourteen

## The Long Wreckage

In 410 the city fell, and the news travelled faster than anyone could absorb it.

Rome had not been taken by a foreign enemy in eight hundred years. It was not even
the capital any more, administratively, and the sack itself lasted three days and
was comparatively restrained by the standards of the age. None of that mattered.
The unthinkable had been thought. Jerome, working on his translation in Bethlehem,
wrote that his voice failed him and sobs broke his words: the city which had taken
the whole world was itself taken.

And immediately the accusation started, because it always does. Rome had abandoned
its old gods for the Christian one, and now Rome had fallen. The correlation was
obvious to everyone who wanted it to be.

Augustine, in Hippo, spent the next thirteen years writing the answer. *The City of
God* is enormous and digressive and it argues, at its core, one thing that the
Church has needed in every upheaval since: there are two cities, one earthly and
one eternal, they are intermixed in this age, and the Christian's citizenship is
not in the one that just fell. Rome was never the point. Rome's fall is a tragedy
and it is not a theological catastrophe, because the kingdom of God was never
underwritten by the Roman state.

He died in 430 with a Vandal army besieging his own city. He did not get to see
whether he was right.

---

The Church's disorientations are long. That is the first thing to take from this
chapter, and it is the hardest for people who want the present crisis resolved
inside their own attention span.

After Rome, the West spent centuries in genuine wreckage. Literacy collapsed.
Cities emptied. The administrative machinery that had moved grain and law and
letters across a continent simply stopped, and what remained was local, poor, and
violent. The faith survived this in a form nobody would have predicted in 400: in
monasteries. Benedict wrote a rule for a small community in the sixth century — a
practical document, more concerned with when to sleep and how much wine and who
does the washing up than with visions — and that unglamorous rule became the
infrastructure that carried Scripture, literacy, agriculture and prayer through the
dark.

It took roughly five hundred years for Europe to have a functioning intellectual
culture again. Five hundred years. Nobody who lived through the disorientation saw
the reorientation.

---

Then there is the disorientation the Church inflicts on itself, which is a
different and uglier category.

The Great Schism of 1054 is usually dated to a scene in which a papal legate laid a
bull of excommunication on the altar of Hagia Sophia and walked out, and the
patriarch excommunicated him back. The technical issue was a clause in the creed
and the question of papal authority. The actual causes were four centuries of
divergence, mutual incomprehension, and politics. The wound has never healed.

The Western Schism, three centuries later, was worse in a different way, because it
was pure farce with real consequences. From 1378 there were two popes, one in Rome
and one in Avignon, each excommunicating the other's followers, each with the
backing of different European powers. An attempt to fix it at Pisa in 1409 produced
a third. For nearly forty years the Church had multiple men each claiming to be the
one voice of God on earth, and every believer in Europe had to decide which — or
notice that the question had become unanswerable.

You cannot overstate what that did. Whatever the medieval Church's authority had
rested on, it did not survive three popes. Wycliffe and Hus are unthinkable without
it. So, a century later, is Luther.

And then the wars of religion, which is where the Church's disorientation stopped
being conceptual and started killing people at scale. From 1524 to 1648 Europe bled
over these questions. The Peasants' War. The French wars of religion and the St
Bartholomew's Day massacre. The Thirty Years War, which killed perhaps a fifth of
the population of the German lands, and in some regions a third — Christians
slaughtering Christians over the interpretation of a text about loving enemies.

This must be said without euphemism. That was not God shaking His Church. That was
the Church's sin, and the guilt for it belongs to the people who did it. Any account
of upheaval that files those decades under refining fire is lying about the dead.

---

The modern disorientation is quieter and in some ways deeper, because it went after
the foundations rather than the institutions.

Through the nineteenth century, three things arrived more or less at once. Geology
and then Darwin made the age and the origin of the world into open questions in a
way that unsettled a literalism much of the Church had assumed without ever having
had to defend. Historical criticism took the biblical text apart with the tools of
ordinary scholarship, and whatever one concludes about the results, the experience
for a believer was of watching the Bible put under a knife. And a broader
intellectual current — call it the secular turn — made unbelief, for the first time
in Western history, an ordinary and respectable position rather than an eccentric
one.

Some of the Church's responses were disastrous. Panic hardened into positions that
did not need defending and could not be held. Some of the responses were
magnificent and are the subject of a later chapter.

But the experience, for millions of ordinary believers across a century, was
disorientation in its exact sense: the map their grandparents used had stopped
corresponding to the terrain, and no new map had been issued.

Then the twentieth century arrived and did something worse than argue. The Somme.
The camps. A war in which the most educated, most Christianised nations on earth
industrialised murder. Every optimistic account of human progress and every
comfortable liberal theology built on it died in those years, and much of European
Christianity has never recovered its confidence.

## In the Body

So where does that leave the Body now, five decades into its own disorientation?

It leaves it in respectable company, and it removes several excuses.

The first thing the history removes is the assumption that this is unusual. The
Church has been here before, repeatedly, and has never once known from inside what
was coming. The monasteries were not a strategy anyone chose; a rule about
mealtimes turned out to be a civilisation's ark. Nobody in 1400, looking at three
popes, could have told you that within a century the Bible would be in every
European vernacular. The pattern is not that the Church correctly anticipates its
renewal. The pattern is that renewal arrives from a direction nobody was watching,
usually through somebody small.

The second thing it removes is the fantasy that the Church is only ever a victim of
its upheavals. Some of what is being shaken now is being shaken because the body
did it. The abuse and its concealment. The fusion of the gospel with partisan
politics, in several countries, at enormous cost to its credibility with everyone
who is not already inside. The centuries in which the faith travelled with empire
and could not tell the difference. Those are not persecutions and they are not
mysteries. They are sins, and the appropriate response to them is not analysis but
repentance.

And the third thing the history offers is patience, which is not much comfort but is
honest. If the last three of these took a century or more to resolve, the current
one will not be finished by anyone reading this. The Christians who did the most
useful work in each previous disorientation were largely people who accepted that
they would not see the outcome — Augustine dying in a besieged city, Benedict
writing house rules, Wycliffe's bones in a river — and did the next faithful thing
anyway.

## In You

You are also in a long one, and the history above is offered to you for exactly one
reason: to lengthen your sense of time.

The wilderness feels aberrant. It feels like an interruption in a life that was
otherwise proceeding normally, and that framing is what makes it unbearable,
because every day it continues is another day of something that should not be
happening. Fourteen months into a disorientation, most people are not primarily
suffering from their circumstances. They are suffering from the belief that this
should have finished by now.

Israel was in the wilderness for forty years. The exile ran seventy. John was in a
cell for nine months and the Church took a century to sort out most of its great
crises. There is nothing in Scripture or in the record of the Church that supports
the expectation of a quick resolution, and the expectation itself is doing you
harm.

So set the clock differently. Not to endure indefinitely in grim resignation, but
to stop measuring each week against a deadline you invented. Ask what a faithful
life looks like at month thirty, rather than what will end this by month six. That
one adjustment converts a large amount of despair into ordinary difficulty, which
is survivable.

And take the specific comfort of Augustine, who did the most important work of his
life while the walls he lived behind were being surrounded. The wreckage does not
disqualify you from doing something worth doing. It never has. Most of what the
Church has to show for itself was made by people in the middle of something they
could not fix.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Two cities, intermixed, and only one of them was ever going to last.

That was Augustine's whole answer to 410 and it is still the answer. What fell in
Rome, in 1054, in 1378, in 1648, in the trenches, was in every case the earthly
city — an arrangement, a settlement, a structure with a founding date. Each time,
people who had confused it with the kingdom of God experienced its fall as the end
of the world.

The other city has no founding date and no walls to breach. It has been growing
through every one of those catastrophes, mostly invisibly, made of people whose
names are not in the histories. It was not shaken in 410. It has not been shaken
since.

You hold citizenship in a city that has never fallen. That is not a metaphor for
optimism. It is the sober fact that has kept Christians steady in every one of
these centuries, and it is available now.

## For Reflection

1. What are you measuring your current season against? Where did that deadline come
   from?
2. Which of your losses belong to the earthly city, and which — honestly — belong
   to the eternal one?
3. What would faithfulness look like at month thirty, rather than escape by month
   six?
