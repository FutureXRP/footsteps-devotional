# Chapter Two

## The Rummage Sale

The image belongs to an Anglican bishop named Mark Dyer, and it was passed on and
made famous by the American writer Phyllis Tickle, who built a book around it in
2008 called *The Great Emergence*.

Every five hundred years or so, Dyer observed, the Church holds a giant rummage
sale.

Picture what that actually means. Not a fire. Not a demolition. A rummage sale is
what a family does when the attic has become impassable — when generations of
accumulated things, each of which mattered enormously to somebody once, have
silted up the house until nobody can move. So everything comes out onto the lawn.
Some of it gets sold. Some of it gets thrown out. Some of it, examined in daylight
for the first time in a century, turns out to be the good silver, and goes back
inside, cleaned.

Tickle's point, and it is a historian's point rather than a prophet's, is that the
Church appears to do this on a rhythm. Roughly every half-millennium the
accumulated forms of the faith harden past the point of usefulness, the shell
cracks, and the whole thing is emptied out onto the lawn in a period of enormous
disruption. And then — this is the part that matters — the faith comes out the
other side more vital, and more widespread, than it went in.

She counted four of these.

---

**Around the year 500,** the Roman world came apart. The imperial order that had
carried the faith across a continent simply stopped functioning in the West.
Augustine died with Vandals at the gates of his city. And out of that wreckage came
Benedict, and a small rule for a small community, and the monastic movement that
would carry literacy, agriculture, Scripture and prayer through the collapse and
into whatever came next. The Church did not survive the fall of Rome by being
strong. It survived by having been emptied of its dependence on Rome.

**Around 1054,** the Great Schism split East from West. Mutual excommunications
exchanged in Constantinople, a thousand years of consequence, and a wound in the
body of Christ that has still not healed. It was also the moment the two halves of
the Church each became fully themselves. The recovery of a distinctly Western
theology and a distinctly Eastern one both date from this rupture.

**Around 1500,** the Reformation. A monk with a hammer and a list of grievances, a
new printing technology, a continent's worth of stored resentment, and within a
generation the unified religious order of Europe was gone forever. Wars followed
that emptied whole regions of central Europe. And so did the
vernacular Bible, the recovery of justification by faith, congregational singing,
universal literacy as a religious project, and the Catholic reform that came out of
Trent.

**Around 2000,** the present one. Which does not yet have a settled name, because
nobody has yet stood on its far side.

---

Now, two cautions, because this idea is powerful enough to be misused and usually
is.

The first: this is a historical observation, not a law of nature and certainly not
a prophecy. Nothing in Scripture sets the Church on a five-hundred-year clock. The
dates are approximate, the boundaries are argued over by every historian who has
looked at them, and you could tell the same story with different hinges. It is a
lens. Held loosely it explains a great deal. Held tightly it becomes astrology.

The second, and more important: a rummage sale costs something. It is easy to read
the pattern from a comfortable distance and find it reassuring — *ah, this has
happened before, it will all be fine*. Tell that to the radicals executed by
fellow reformers. Tell it to the villages emptied by the Thirty Years War. The
Church came out of each of these more vital, yes. Real people were destroyed on the
way, and some of the destruction was done by Christians to Christians, and God was
not its author. Renewal on the far side does not retroactively sanctify the
casualties. Both things stay true.

---

But there is one question Tickle noticed at the centre of every one of these
upheavals, and it is the question that makes the pattern more than a curiosity.

Each time, the thing that actually cracks is the answer to: **where now is the
authority?**

In 500, the answer had been the imperial order and the structures it sustained.
When the empire went, the question was live again, and the answer that emerged was
the See of Rome, and the Rule, and the councils. In 1054, the fight was precisely
about that answer — one bishop or a college of them. In 1500, Luther's real
detonation was not indulgences; it was his answer at Worms, that a conscience bound
by Scripture outranked both a pope and a council. Where now is the authority? In
Scripture, he said, and the whole order of Europe cracked along that line.

And now. Where now is the authority? Not the denomination — most people under forty
could not name theirs. Not the pastor, not automatically, not after the last twenty
years of abuse and cover-up made deference look like negligence. Not the tradition,
for a generation that inherited none. Not the academy. Not, for many, the Bible
itself in the way their grandparents held it, because the question of who gets to
interpret it turned out to be as contested as the text.

That question is the sound a rummage sale makes. If you hear it in your own life —
*whose voice do I actually trust now* — you are not in a private crisis. You are
standing inside a five-hundred-year one, holding your own small piece of it.

## In You

You have probably had your own rummage sale, or you are in one, and if you are it
almost certainly began the way they all begin: with things you had inherited
without inspecting.

Everyone gets handed an attic. Beliefs you absorbed before you could evaluate them.
Practices you kept because they were what your people did. Political assumptions
that arrived stapled to the gospel and were never separated out. A picture of God
assembled from a particular pulpit in a particular decade. Emotional reflexes
around guilt and worth and belonging that have your family's fingerprints all over
them.

None of that is contemptible. Most of it was given in love. But an attic is not a
foundation, and there comes a point where you cannot move in your own house.

The mercy of a personal rummage sale is that everything comes out onto the lawn
where you can finally see it in daylight. And the terror of it is exactly the same:
everything is out on the lawn, all at once, and for a while you cannot tell the
junk from the silver. That is why this phase feels like losing your faith. From
inside it, an inventory is indistinguishable from a loss.

So slow the sale down. The instinct in a crisis is to throw out the whole attic in
one weekend, and people who do that spend the following decade trying to buy pieces
of it back. Paul's counsel is better and harder: test everything; hold fast to what
is good. Sift, do not smash. Some of what you inherited is genuinely the good
silver, and it will look like junk to you right now precisely because it arrived in
the same box as the junk. That is not a reason to bin it. It is a reason to clean
it and look again.

## In the Body

The Church is in one of these now, and has been for perhaps fifty years, and the
noise level is roughly what the pattern would predict.

Everything is on the lawn. Denominational identity, which organised Western
Christianity for four centuries, means little to anyone under forty and is
dissolving into networks and affinities that cross the old lines freely. Buildings
built for a Christendom crowd stand three-quarters empty on Sunday and are being
sold across Europe at a rate nobody planned for. Authority is contested at every
level, and the contest was made vastly sharper by the sexual abuse crises that
crossed Catholic, Protestant, evangelical and charismatic bodies alike, and by
Christians discovering that their institutions had chosen self-protection over
children.

And in the same decades: the extraordinary growth of Pentecostal and charismatic
Christianity into the largest single movement in the faith's history. The African
church, now numbering in the hundreds of millions, sending missionaries back to
Europe. The Chinese church, grown under pressure the West cannot imagine. A serious
retrieval of ancient practice — liturgy, fasting, the daily office, spiritual
direction — by young Christians whose parents fled all of it. Scot McKnight,
writing in 2007, tried to name the streams running through the emerging Western
church, and whatever one makes of the movement he was describing, the instinct was
right: what you can see in a rummage sale is not one new thing but several currents
running at once, and it is far too early to say which will be flowing in a century.

Nobody alive gets to see the far side of this one. That is the honest position.
Luther did not see the far side of his. What the pattern offers is not prediction
but nerve: the Church has been emptied onto the lawn before, it has always looked
terminal from inside, and it has never yet been terminal.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Notice what does not go on the lawn.

In 500 the empire went; the Church did not. In 1054 the unity of the visible Church
went; Christ's ownership of both halves did not. In 1500 the medieval order went;
the gospel it had been carrying, imperfectly, came out the other side clearer than
it went in.

Paul, writing to a congregation tearing itself apart over which teacher to follow,
said the one thing that has held through all four sales: no one can lay any other
foundation than the one that is laid, which is Jesus Christ. Everything built on
top of that foundation is inspected by fire eventually, and some of it burns. The
foundation is not what burns. It is what the fire is standing on.

## For Reflection

1. What did you inherit in your faith without ever inspecting it? Which pieces have
   you never once taken out into daylight?
2. Where now is the authority, for you? Answer honestly rather than correctly —
   whose voice do you actually trust when something is at stake?
3. What have you thrown out in a hurry that you now suspect was the good silver?
