# Chapter Seven

## Turn Again

He is on the road with letters in his bag.

Saul of Tarsus has legal authorisation to go to Damascus, find members of the Way in
the synagogues there, and bring them back to Jerusalem in chains — men and women,
the account specifies. He is not a bystander who disapproves. He had stood holding
the coats while Stephen was stoned, and Luke says he was consenting to his death,
and he has since been going house to house dragging people out. He is, by his own
later description, the chief of sinners, and he means it technically rather than
rhetorically.

And near Damascus, about noon, a light.

He falls to the ground and hears his own name twice — Saul, Saul, why do you
persecute me? He asks who is speaking, which is the only sane question available,
and gets the answer that reorganises his entire life in one sentence: I am Jesus,
whom you are persecuting.

Not *my followers*. Me.

He gets up blind. They have to lead him by the hand into the city, this man who had
arrived with warrants, and he sits in a house on Straight Street for three days
without eating or drinking. Then a believer named Ananias — who argues with God
about the assignment, reasonably, having heard what this man does to people like him
— comes and puts his hands on him and calls him brother.

---

Every conversion is a disconnect, and this is the one most people in the Church have
actually experienced.

Not the dramatic version, necessarily. Most conversions are undramatic and some are
so gradual that nobody can date them. But the structure is the same: something is
broken with, and the person on the other side of it does not simply hold new
opinions. They have a new centre of gravity, and the old life becomes, in a real
sense, someone else's.

Paul's own accounting is stark. Reading his list in Philippians, you notice it is not
a list of sins. It is a list of achievements — circumcised the eighth day, of the
tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews, as touching the law a Pharisee, blameless.
Every item is a genuine credential, earned, and the sort of thing his community
would have admired. And he says: what things were gains to me, these have I counted
loss for Christ.

The break was not with his failures. It was with his successes. That is a harder
disconnect and a much less discussed one.

---

There is a smaller and more cheerful version in Jericho, and it belongs here as a
corrective, because the Damascus road can make conversion sound like something that
only happens to people being knocked over.

Zacchaeus is a chief tax collector, which means wealthy and thoroughly despised — a
collaborator who has grown rich skimming from his own people. He is also short, and
the crowd is not going to let him through, so he does something no man of his
standing would do in that culture: he runs ahead and climbs a tree.

Jesus stops under it, looks up, and invites Himself to lunch.

And Zacchaeus comes down joyfully, and by the end of the meal has announced that he
is giving half his goods to the poor and repaying anyone he defrauded four times
over — which is the restitution the law required for stolen livestock, applied
voluntarily to his entire career.

Notice the sequence, because it matters. He was received first and reformed second.
The crowd's complaint was that Jesus had gone to be the guest of a sinner, and they
were right about the facts and wrong about everything else. Nothing was required of
Zacchaeus before the meal. The restitution came out of the meal.

And notice that it is joyful. Not every break is a wound. Some people arrive at the
Disconnect phase with enormous relief, having been carrying something they did not
know they could put down.

---

Then there is the ongoing kind, which is the one this chapter most wants to leave
you with.

Peter, preaching in Jerusalem, tells a crowd to repent and turn again, that their
sins may be blotted out, so that there may come times of refreshing from the
presence of the Lord. The word behind repentance is about the mind being changed and
the direction being reversed — it is a navigational term before it is an emotional
one.

And Luther's very first thesis, before he said a word about indulgences, made the
same point about scale: when our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said Repent, He willed
that the whole life of believers should be repentance.

The whole life. Not an event at the beginning that gets referred back to for the
next forty years. A continuous willingness to be turned, which means that Disconnect
is not only the first phase of one large upheaval in your life; it is a small
recurring motion inside all four.

That is worth saying because a great many people carry a single conversion story
like a certificate and have not been turned about anything since. The certificate is
real. It is also, in Luther's reading, the first instance of something that was
supposed to keep happening.

---

One necessary caution. Not every dramatic religious experience is a conversion, and
the tradition has always known this.

Jesus warned about seed that springs up quickly in shallow soil, with joy — that
detail is in the parable — and withers when the sun comes up because it has no root.
Enthusiasm at the beginning is not evidence of anything by itself. Edwards spent his
career on this exact problem during the awakenings, and the test he arrived at is
still the best one: not the intensity of the experience but a changed life over
time.

Saul's evidence was not the light on the road. It was thirty years of being beaten,
shipwrecked, imprisoned and eventually executed for the thing he had been trying to
destroy.

## In You

If you have never made this break, the invitation is exactly as it was under the
tree: you are received first.

That is the part almost everybody gets backwards, having absorbed the idea that some
cleaning up is required before you are eligible. Zacchaeus did not clean up before
lunch. Saul was still legally authorised to arrest Christians when Christ addressed
him by name. Ananias called him brother three days later, and the man had not yet
done a single thing to deserve the word.

If you made it long ago, the question is different, and it is Luther's. What have you
been turned about lately? A faith with one conversion in it and no subsequent
reversals is a faith that has quietly stopped listening — not necessarily out of
hardness, but because being corrected is uncomfortable and it is possible to arrange
a life in which nobody ever does it.

And there is Paul's specific question, which is the sharpest one in this chapter.
His break was with his credentials. What in your life is a genuine gain — something
you earned, that people admire, that has become part of how you understand yourself
— that has quietly moved into the position only Christ should hold? Not a sin. A
success. Those are much harder to identify, because nothing about them feels wrong,
and much harder to put down, because putting them down looks to everyone else like
throwing away something good.

Finally: expect the three blind days. Saul's conversion did not resolve into clarity
immediately; it produced a period of complete disorientation in a house on a
street, being fed by other people. If your break has left you unable to see what
happens next, that is not a sign it did not take. It is in the account.

## In the Body

Conversion is where the Body's authority question becomes intensely practical,
because whatever else a church is, it is the community that decides who is in.

Ananias is the model and the story does not flatter the Church. He objected. He had
heard about this man and how much harm he had done to the saints in Jerusalem, and
his objection was not faithless — it was informed, and any of us would have raised
it. He went anyway, and the first word out of his mouth was brother.

That scene has to be reread by Western Christianity in a season when it has become
extraordinarily good at deciding who is out. The lines are drawn now over politics,
sexuality, vaccination, tradition, tone — and the drawing is done fast, publicly, and
with a confidence that Ananias, who had actual evidence, did not permit himself.

The Body also has to reckon with a related failure, which is the conversion that
never becomes discipleship. Vast numbers of people across the modern Western Church
were counted as converts on the strength of a decision made at an event, and then
handed nothing — no formation, no community, no rule of life, no one to call brother
— and the shallow-soil parable describes precisely what happened next. That is not a
failure of those people's sincerity. It is a failure of the Body to do what Ananias
did, which was to show up at the house afterwards.

And the healthiest thing happening in this area is worth noting. Across traditions,
churches are rediscovering catechesis — the slow, unfashionable, months-long
formation of new believers that the early Church took for granted and the modern
Church largely abandoned in favour of the decision. That is a recovery of something
that was always there, and it is exactly what a body in a rummage sale ought to be
pulling back off the lawn.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.

Sit with the grammar of that one more time, because it is the sentence that holds
this chapter up. Saul had not touched Him. Christ had ascended; the people Saul was
dragging out of houses were ordinary believers in Damascus and Jerusalem, and He
identified with them so completely that what was done to them was done to Him.

What cannot be shaken is that identification. It was true before Saul knew it, it
was true of people whose names nobody recorded, and it is the reason a persecutor
could become the apostle to the nations without any intermediate step of proving
himself.

You are not received on the strength of what you have made of yourself. You are
received into a body He refuses to be separated from.

## For Reflection

1. What have you been turned about recently? If nothing, when did the turning stop?
2. Paul's break was with his gains, not his sins. What genuine success has moved into
   a place it should not hold?
3. Who is someone you have decided is out? What would it cost you to say brother
   first, as Ananias did, with the evidence still in front of you?
