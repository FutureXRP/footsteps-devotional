# Chapter Five

## Leave Your Country

He is seventy-five years old.

That is the detail the story refuses to let you skip, and it is worth stopping on
before anything else happens. Abram is not a young man with options. He is settled
in Haran, in a household with servants and flocks and an established position, at
an age when a man is meant to be handing things over rather than packing them. And
God speaks, and what He says is a demolition delivered in three widening blows.

Get out of your country. Leave the land, the culture, the language, the only
geography you have ever understood.

And from your relatives. The extended clan, the network of obligation and
protection that in that world was the entire difference between a life and a
death.

And from your father's house. The last circle. The household itself. The name you
answer to.

Then, finally: *to a land that I will show you*.

Not *to Canaan*. Not a map, not a route, not a destination he could look up and
weigh. The order comes in the wrong sequence for anyone who wants to make a
reasonable decision. You leave first. The showing comes later.

And Abram went, as the LORD had spoken to him.

---

There is a farmer in another book who understands this instinctively.

Elijah finds Elisha ploughing with twelve yoke of oxen — an enormous operation,
which means Elisha is not a labourer but a wealthy man's son with a future. The
prophet walks past and throws his cloak over him and keeps walking. That is the
whole call. No speech.

Elisha asks to kiss his parents goodbye, and then does something nobody asked him
to do. He takes the yoke of oxen he was working with and kills them, and builds a
fire out of the plough and the equipment, and cooks the meat over it, and feeds it
to the people.

Think about what he has just destroyed. The oxen were the engine. The plough was
the tool. Together they were his livelihood, his inheritance, his fallback. He
could have sold them, put the money aside, kept the option open. Instead he burns
the option. He makes going back not difficult but impossible, and then throws a
party with the proceeds.

That is a specific spiritual instinct and it is worth naming: he understood that a
retained option is a slow leak. Half the people who never complete a break are not
stopped by the difficulty of the new thing. They are stopped by the survival of the
old one, sitting in a field, still viable, still whispering.

---

But before this becomes a sermon about burning things, three qualifications, and
they matter more than the drama.

**Abram's call was unique.** He is being made into a nation, and the promise
attached to his departure is not repeatable — through you all the families of the
earth will be blessed. Not every believer gets a call to leave a country, and a
book like this one can do real damage if it implies that faithfulness requires a
dramatic exit. The principle transfers; the particulars do not. What transfers is
that God's call routinely comes before the details, and that obedience is measured
by what you actually let go of.

**Some breaks are disobedience.** Jonah also got on a boat. He also left home, also
at God's word, also without knowing exactly what was coming — and he was running in
the opposite direction. The presence of upheaval proves nothing about the presence
of a call. Plenty of dramatic departures are simply a man leaving a hard thing and
calling it a wilderness.

**The cost is real and it lands on other people.** Abram's departure took Sarai out
of her home too, and Lot, and a household of servants who did not get a vision.
Follow the thread of that family and you find the ache of it never fully resolves:
these all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them and
embraced them from afar. He got the going. He did not get the arriving.

Jesus never pretended otherwise. He told a crowd that was swelling around Him to go
and sit down and calculate the cost first, like a man pricing out a tower before he
lays a foundation, because the alternative is a half-built ruin that everyone walks
past and mocks. That is not recruitment language. It is the opposite. He seems to
have preferred a smaller number of people who knew the bill.

---

And yet. Notice who does not make the break, and what it costs him.

A young man runs up to Jesus in the road and kneels — he is eager, he is sincere,
he has kept the commandments since he was a boy, and Jesus looks at him and loves
him. That clause is in the text and it changes the whole scene. This is not a trap
being sprung. This is love naming the one thing.

Sell what you have. Come, follow me.

And he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions.

He is the only person in the Gospels who is invited to follow and simply declines.
And the tragedy is not that he was greedy. The tragedy is that he was *sad*. He
knew. He walked away knowing exactly what he was walking away from, holding on to
something he could not put down, and the last we see of him is a grieving back.

Every break not made has that shape. It is not a triumphant refusal. It is a sad
one.

## In You

There is something you are being asked to leave, and there is a good chance you
already know what it is, and there is an even better chance you have been
negotiating about it for years.

Notice the sequence in Abram's call, because it explains the paralysis. The showing
comes after the going. You have been waiting for clarity before you move, and God's
pattern is very often to give the move and withhold the map — not to be difficult,
but because a certainty you can inspect in advance requires no trust at all, and
trust is the actual thing being built.

So the question to sit with is not *where is this going*. You are not going to get
that. The question is: **what am I keeping viable?**

Because that is where breaks actually die. Not in the leaving, which people manage
with surprising courage, but in the quiet preservation of the option. The
relationship that ended, with the messages still saved. The job you resigned from,
with the door kept warm. The old way of thinking about yourself that you have
renounced out loud and returned to privately every time you are tired. The oxen are
still in the field. You told everyone you had left.

Elisha's fire is not a demand that you be reckless. It is a diagnosis. What is
still standing behind you that makes going back possible on a bad night?

And if what you need to break with is not a thing but a pattern — a way of numbing,
a way of managing people, a way of speaking to yourself — then the burning is not
dramatic at all. It looks like a conversation with one person, and a decision made
again on Tuesday, and again on Wednesday. Most real disconnects are unphotogenic.

## In the Body

The Church, corporately, has had to leave a country too, and much of the last half
century of Western Christian anxiety is the grief of an emigration nobody chose.

For sixteen hundred years, in the West, the faith had a homeland. Not a territory
exactly, but an arrangement: a culture whose calendar, laws, art, education and
public vocabulary all assumed Christianity. To be a Christian in that world was to
be at home. The Church did not need to explain itself from first principles because
it was one of the first principles.

That country is gone. Not going — gone, in most of Western Europe, and going fast
elsewhere. And the Church has spent decades in the posture of a household that
keeps insisting the old address is still valid: campaigning to restore what was
default, mistaking cultural nostalgia for faithfulness, treating loss of privilege
as persecution.

The New Testament, written by people who never had that homeland, is remarkably
unbothered. Peter addresses his readers as sojourners and pilgrims — resident
foreigners, people with a legal status but not a birthright, at home and not at
home simultaneously. He is not consoling them for a demotion. It is simply what
they were, and it is what the Church has been for most of its history and in most
of the world today. The Chinese church has never had a Christendom. The African
church that is now sending missionaries to Europe grew up without one. The
persecuted church has never once confused the gospel with the establishment.

Which means the Body's Disconnect is not primarily a loss to be mourned but a
possession to be sold. The question in front of Western Christianity is Elisha's
question: are the oxen still in the field? Is there still an assumption, somewhere
in the institutional memory, that the culture will eventually come back and that
the job is to hold on until it does?

Because a Church that is genuinely willing to be a pilgrim people can move.
And a Church that is still keeping the old country viable will spend its remaining
strength defending an address it no longer lives at.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

Abram left everything that gives a person a location — land, kin, name — and what
he received in exchange was not a place. It was a promise, and a companion.

Hebrews says what he was actually looking for: a city which has foundations, whose
builder and maker is God. He never saw it. He lived in tents for a hundred years in
the land he had been promised, which is to say he was a permanent guest in his own
inheritance, and he died holding a word rather than a deed.

What cannot be shaken is that word. Everything Abram let go of was made — a city
built by hands, a household assembled by generations, a name that a few centuries
would erase anyway. The thing he received was not made, and it is still standing,
and it is the only reason anyone remembers the name of a seventy-five-year-old
man who walked out of Haran.

You are not being asked to leave something for nothing. You are being asked to
trade the made for the unmade.

## For Reflection

1. What are you keeping viable? Name the oxen still standing in the field.
2. Where are you waiting for the showing before you will do the going?
3. Is there a break you have declined and grieved? What did you walk away holding?
