# Chapter Eighteen

## Rebuilding the Wall

He is a cupbearer in a Persian palace, which is a better job than it sounds — you
taste the king's wine for poison, which means you have his ear and his trust — and
his brother arrives from Judah with news.

Nehemiah asks how things are. And the answer is: the survivors there are in great
affliction and reproach; the wall of Jerusalem is broken down, and its gates are
burned with fire.

He sat down and wept, and mourned certain days; and he fasted and prayed before the
God of heaven.

Certain days. The text does not specify, and the vagueness is the point — it was
long enough to be worth mentioning and not worth counting. He did not immediately
launch a project. A man in a position to actually do something about it sat in
grief for an unspecified period first, and every rebuild in Scripture that works has
that pause in it somewhere.

Then he went to work, and the book of Nehemiah becomes the most practical
description of reconstruction in the Bible.

---

Here is what he actually did, and it is deeply unspiritual in the best way.

**He asked for what he needed, specifically.** He went to the king and requested a
leave of absence, letters of safe passage for the journey, and a written order to
the keeper of the royal forest for timber. He had thought about the timber. People
in reorientation often pray for restoration in general and never ask anyone for the
specific things restoration would actually require.

**He inspected the damage at night, privately, before telling anyone his plan.** He
rode out around the broken walls in the dark with a few men and told nobody what
God had put in his heart to do. He wanted to know the true extent of it before there
was an audience, and before other people's opinions had shaped his assessment.

**He divided the work.** Chapter three of Nehemiah is a list, and it is one of the
most quietly beautiful chapters in the Old Testament once you see what it is doing.
The priests built the Sheep Gate. The goldsmiths and the perfumers worked on a
section. Shallum's daughters worked on his. And most people worked on the stretch of
wall in front of their own house.

That last detail is the whole method. Nobody rebuilt Jerusalem. Everybody rebuilt
the bit in front of them, and Jerusalem got rebuilt.

**He worked with the opposition still active.** Sanballat and Tobiah mocked, then
threatened, then plotted. Nehemiah's response was to post a guard and have half the
workers hold weapons while the other half built — each builder with a sword at his
side and a trowel in his hand. He did not wait for safety. He built in conditions
that had not improved.

**He refused the meeting.** Four times his opponents invited him to talk on the
plain of Ono, and four times he sent back the same message: I am doing a great work,
so that I can't come down. Why should the work cease while I leave it and come down
to you?

That sentence is worth memorising by anyone in reconstruction. There will be
invitations to come down and litigate. They will be framed as reasonable, as
dialogue, as your obligation. Some of them are; most, at this stage, are not.

---

But the wall is only half of it, and the other half is the harder half.

Ezra reads the law aloud to the assembled people — from early morning until midday,
with Levites moving through the crowd explaining the sense of it — and the people
weep. They weep because they are hearing what they had lost, and hearing it clearly
enough to understand how far they had drifted.

And they are told, gently, to stop. This day is holy to the LORD your God. Don't
mourn, nor weep. Go your way, eat the fat, drink the sweet, and send portions to
him for whom nothing is prepared — for the joy of the LORD is your strength.

There is a moment in every reconstruction where grief has done its work and
becomes an obstacle, and knowing when that moment has arrived is one of the great
pastoral difficulties. Too early and it is bullying. Too late and a person or a
people has organised their whole life around a loss.

And there is the scene at the foundation of the second temple, which may be the most
emotionally accurate verse in Scripture about rebuilding. The old men who had seen
the first house wept with a loud voice when the foundation of this one was laid, and
many shouted aloud for joy — and the people could not distinguish the noise of the
shout of joy from the noise of the weeping, because the people shouted with a loud
shout, and the noise was heard far away.

Nobody could tell the sounds apart. That is what reorientation actually sounds
like: a new thing genuinely worth celebrating, going up on the site of something
that will never come back, and both facts being expressed simultaneously by
different people who cannot hear each other properly.

If your rebuild feels like joy and grief at the same volume, you are not confused.
You are at the foundation.

---

One more, and it is the corrective to everything practical above.

Zechariah, speaking to the same rebuilding effort, is shown a lampstand fed by two
olive trees that pour oil into it directly — no one tending it, no one refilling it.
And the word that comes with it is: not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit,
says the LORD of Armies.

Nehemiah organised brilliantly and the wall went up in fifty-two days. Zechariah's
vision says the oil was never coming from the organising. Both are in the book. The
Bible is remarkably relaxed about holding competent planning and total dependence
together, and it is mostly the rest of us who insist on choosing.

## In You

Rebuild the bit in front of your house.

That is the single most useful instruction in this chapter, and it is aimed at the
paralysis that hits people when they finally have energy again and survey the total
extent of what needs restoring. The whole wall is down. Your health, your finances,
your marriage, your work, your prayer life, your capacity to trust anybody. You
cannot rebuild that. Nobody could.

You can rebuild one section. Pick the one in front of you — the nearest, most
obvious, least glamorous thing — and work on that, and let the rest of the wall be
somebody else's section or next year's problem.

**Inspect the damage honestly first, at night, alone.** Before you announce anything
or ask anyone's opinion. Most people either overestimate the damage from inside a
bad season or minimise it out of shame, and neither is a foundation to build on.

**Ask for the specific thing.** Not prayer for restoration in general. The timber.
The appointment with the doctor. The money. The conversation with the person. Write
down what this actually requires and then request those items from the people who
have them.

**Expect the sword-and-trowel conditions.** You are not going to get a period of
calm in which to rebuild. The opposition — external, or the internal voice, or the
circumstances that have not resolved — will still be active. Build anyway, with one
hand, more slowly than you would like.

**And decline the meeting.** There will be someone wanting to relitigate the past
just as you are starting to build. Sometimes that conversation is necessary and you
should have it. Often it is Ono, and the honest answer is that you are doing a great
work and cannot come down.

## In the Body

The Body rebuilds the same way and keeps trying to do it the other way.

The institutional instinct, faced with decline, is to plan the whole wall: a
denominational strategy, a national initiative, a decade of mission with a logo. And
the record of the last fifty years of those is not encouraging. What has actually
produced durable renewal, in every century one looks at, is closer to Nehemiah's
chapter three — a great many people working on the section in front of their own
house, without central coordination, most of them unrecorded.

Benedict wrote a rule for one house. Wesley organised small groups of a dozen
people who met to ask each other how their souls were doing. Herrnhut was a village
of refugees who started a prayer meeting that ran, in relay, for a hundred years.
None of these were programmes. All of them were somebody's section of wall.

The Body also needs Ezra's discipline about grief, and currently gets it wrong in
both directions. Some parts of Western Christianity have not grieved at all — have
gone straight from denial to strategy without ever admitting what has been lost, and
their plans have a brittle, unreal quality as a result. Other parts have made the
lament permanent, and can produce sophisticated accounts of their own decline
indefinitely while doing nothing. There is a day when the reading of the law
produces weeping and a moment, later that same day, when the instruction is to go
and eat the fat and drink the sweet and send portions to those who have nothing.

And the foundation scene should be read aloud in every denomination that is
downsizing. What is being built now is smaller than what was there before. The
people who remember the first house are genuinely grieving and are not being
sentimental — it really was bigger. The people shouting for joy are not being naive
— something really is being built. Both noises are appropriate. Neither party should
try to silence the other, and the sound of the two together carries a long way.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

The oil.

Nehemiah's wall was breached again within centuries. The second temple was
magnificently expanded by Herod and then destroyed by Titus in 70 AD, and the
foundation those old men wept over is a retaining wall people now pray at. Every
structure in this chapter is gone.

What cannot be shaken is what Zechariah was shown: a lamp fed directly, without
human refilling, by a source that does not depend on the strength of the builders or
the survival of the building. Not by might. Not by power.

Build the section in front of you. Build it well, with proper timber, and accept
that it too will need rebuilding by someone else. The oil was never coming from the
wall.
