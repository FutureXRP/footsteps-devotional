# Chapter Eight

## The Door at Wittenberg

The door was a noticeboard.

It is worth deflating the scene before building it back up, because the picture
most people carry — a defiant monk hammering revolution into oak while a crowd
watches — is largely a later painting. The north door of the Castle Church at
Wittenberg was where university notices went. Posting theses there was the ordinary
way an academic proposed a disputation. Luther wrote his in Latin, the language of
scholars and not of the market square, and he sent a copy to his archbishop the
same day with a respectful letter. Whether he used a hammer at all is disputed by
serious historians.

He was not trying to break the Church. He was trying to argue about a fundraising
practice.

That is what makes it the perfect illustration of how disconnects actually begin.
Almost nobody sets out to detonate their world. They set out to raise a question
that turns out to be load-bearing.

---

The practice was this. A Dominican named Johann Tetzel was working the territory
near Wittenberg selling indulgences — remissions of temporal punishment for sin —
to fund, among other things, the rebuilding of St Peter's in Rome. The sales pitch
had become grotesque. Popular report has it running as a jingle about a coin in the
box and a soul springing out of purgatory, and whether or not Tetzel used those
exact words, that was the transaction people believed they were making. Peasants
who could not afford bread were buying their mothers out of the fire.

Luther was a parish priest as well as a professor. He heard about it in the
confessional, from his own people, who were arriving with receipts instead of
repentance.

So he wrote ninety-five theses. Read them and you will be struck by how moderate
they are. Several defend the pope, on the assumption that if he knew what was being
done in his name he would stop it. The first one is not about money at all: when
our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said Repent, He willed that the whole life of
believers should be repentance.

But underneath the ninety-five is a single question with a very long fuse. If the
Church is selling something, on what authority does it sell it? And that question,
pulled on hard enough, becomes the question: **where now is the authority?**

Four years later Luther was standing in front of the Emperor at Worms with his
books on a table, being asked to recant them. He asked for a night to think. He
came back and made his answer, and the part of it that mattered was not the famous
line about standing but the sentence before: unless he was convinced by Scripture
and plain reason, he could not and would not recant, because it is neither safe nor
right to go against conscience.

A monk had just told an emperor and a pope, in public, that a text and a conscience
outranked them both. The unified religious order of Europe did not survive that
sentence.

---

The Church's breaks always run along that fault line, and once you see it you see
it everywhere.

Wycliffe, a century and a half earlier, had done the same thing from Oxford, and
the English Bible that came out of his circle was so threatening that decades after
his death his bones were dug up and burned and thrown in a river — a gesture that
tells you exactly how much authority was felt to be at stake. Hus preached
Wycliffe's ideas in Prague, was given a safe conduct to the Council of Constance,
and was burned anyway; the safe conduct was ruled not to apply to heretics.
Tyndale, who wanted a boy behind a plough to know more Scripture than a priest, was
strangled and burned at Vilvoorde, and his last recorded prayer was that God would
open the King of England's eyes.

None of these men began as revolutionaries. Every one of them began with a
question about who gets to say.

And the breaks kept multiplying, which is the part triumphalist accounts skip. The
Reformation did not produce one new church. It produced Lutheran and Reformed and
Anglican and Anabaptist and a dozen radical movements, and they persecuted one
another. The Anabaptists, who committed the offence of concluding that baptism
should follow belief, were drowned by other Protestants — a joke about their own
convictions being made of their executions. Within a century Europe was at war over
this, and the Thirty Years War killed a fifth of the population of the German
lands, and in some regions a third.

The Church came out of it with the vernacular Scriptures, congregational singing,
the recovery of grace, mass literacy as a religious project, and the Catholic reform
that Trent set in motion. It also came out of it soaked in blood, most of it
Christian blood spilled by Christians.

Both of those sentences are the record. A book that gives you only the first is
selling you something.

---

There is one more figure worth putting beside Luther, because he shows a different
way through.

Fifteen years before Luther, a wealthy young man in Assisi had a break with his
father that has never been improved on for sheer theatre. Francis had been giving
away his father's cloth to the poor; his father dragged him before the bishop in
the town square to renounce his inheritance. Francis renounced it, and then removed
his clothes and handed those back too, and stood naked in front of the town and
said he now had only one Father.

Francis broke with the Church's wealth without breaking with the Church. He took
the poverty of Christ so literally that the contrast preached itself, and the
institution — to its considerable credit — made room for him rather than burning
him. Which raises a question the Church has never fully answered. Why Francis
absorbed and Hus burned. Some of the difference is the men. Much of it is the
century, and how frightened the institution was that year.

## In the Body

The Body's disconnects are the loudest events in Christian history and the least
understood, because they are almost always narrated afterwards by the winners.

Here is what the record actually suggests. First, that the break is usually about
authority even when it presents as being about something else. Indulgences,
vestments, the filioque clause, a hymnal, a building fund — pull on any Church
split and you eventually find two parties who disagree about who gets to decide.
Second, that the institution's first instinct is nearly always suppression, and
that this instinct has been wrong more often than it has been right. Third, that
splits cost far more than the people making them anticipate, and that nobody in
1517 imagined 1618.

Which lands the Body in an uncomfortable place, currently. Western Christianity is
fragmenting again — denominations splitting over sexuality and gender, congregations
detaching from bodies they had belonged to for a century, whole networks forming
and dissolving inside a decade, and millions simply leaving without joining
anything. Some of that is holy discontent doing what it has always done. Some of it
is the sheer availability of separation in a consumer culture, where leaving costs
nothing and therefore means nothing.

The test the history offers is not comfortable for either side. It is not *was I
right*. Luther was right about indulgences and it did not prevent the Thirty Years
War. The test is closer to Francis: is this break making the gospel more visible, or
only making a party more distinct? And: have I counted a cost that will be paid by
people who are not in this room?

## In You

Your break may have had a Wittenberg door — a small, reasonable act that turned out
to be enormous.

That is how it usually goes. Not a decision to leave, but a question asked once in
a meeting. A refusal to sign something. A conversation where, for the first time,
you said what you actually thought about the thing everyone had agreed not to
discuss. You were not trying to end anything. You were trying to raise a point,
and the room went quiet, and you found out that the point was load-bearing.

If that has happened to you, three things.

First, the cost is usually social, and nobody prepares you for it. Luther lost
Rome; most people lose a lunch table. The people you assumed would be with you turn
out to be elsewhere, and the silence from those who agree with you privately is a
particular kind of lonely.

Second, be very careful about your conscience, in both directions. Luther's stand
rested on Scripture *and* plain reason — he did not appeal to a private impression.
Conscience unhooked from the text is just preference with a raised voice, and there
has never been a decade with more people burning things down on the strength of
their own certainty. And yet a conscience genuinely bound by the Word is the one
thing you must not violate, whatever it costs you, because a person who overrides
that has nothing left to stand on later.

Third, and hardest: your break will divide people who love you. Jesus said, plainly
and without softening it, that He came to bring not peace but a sword, and He
described the split running right through a household. He was not endorsing family
conflict. He was refusing to let anyone claim they had not been told. If your
disconnect has cost you your family's approval, you are not necessarily doing it
wrong. You may be doing exactly what He said it would look like.

## What Cannot Be Shaken

The line Luther could not get out of his head, the one that undid him in his tower
study before it ever undid Europe, was seven words from Habakkuk by way of Romans:
the righteous shall live by faith.

He had read it a hundred times. What broke open was that God's righteousness was
not a standard he had to reach but a gift he had to receive — that the verdict came
first and the life followed, rather than the reverse. Everything else, the door and
the Diet and the four centuries, was consequence.

That is what held. Not the movement, which fractured. Not the institutions, which
have all been shaken since and some of which are gone. What cannot be shaken is a
righteousness that is given rather than achieved, standing outside you, unaffected
by your performance, and therefore not available for anyone to sell you.

Nobody has ever needed to buy what God was already handing out.

## For Reflection

1. Was there a door in your story — a small act that turned out to be enormous? Did
   you know at the time?
2. When you have broken with something, was it about the stated issue, or about who
   got to decide?
3. Is there a conflict you are in where you are more concerned with being right
   than with the gospel being visible?
