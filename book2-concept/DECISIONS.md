# Decisions after outside review

Three independent reads. Recorded here so we stop re-litigating settled points.

**Verdicts:** conditional yes (write the hardest section first as a test) · no
(publish the non-fiction instead) · yes, and possibly the better book.

Two of three say write it. The one *no* concedes the premise is "the strongest
thing in the proposal" and rates the prose "better than most work in the
category" — its objection is not the idea, it's the risk profile. That objection
turns out to have a cheap answer, below.

---

## Where I was wrong

Recorded plainly because it matters for how much weight to give my judgement
later.

**The ending.** I recommended (b) — she left by a road he never knew about. All
three picked **(a)**, independently, and all three rejected (b) for the same
reason: it cheats the reader of the scene the back half promised and converts
Grieve into a mystery-device. One noted (b) "protects the character from the full
cost of the Waste," which is exactly the softening this book exists to refuse. I
was wrong and the consensus is right.

**The flash-forward.** I wrote it and rated it. One reviewer caught what I
missed: *it answers question 8*. "Next to a woman who had refused to come" places
him at a table beside Grieve eleven years out — it forecloses (b) and (c) on page
one. I put a spoiler for my own open question into the sample I was using to ask
the question.

**The retrospective narrator — the most important note in all three reviews.**
Three instances in 800 words: *would be grateful for later*, *understood later*,
*would carry the sentence for eleven years*. The diagnosis is exact and I did not
see it: this is the non-fiction's reflex — reading the map backwards from the
ridge, literally Chapter 3 — smuggled into fiction, where it is an anti-tension
device. Every time the narrator promises that present pain will resolve, the
scene is anaesthetised and nothing is allowed to hurt *now*.

That is consolation. It is precisely the consolation the non-fiction spent 53,000
words refusing. **Rationed to twice in the whole book, and never in the middle
third.**

---

## Locked — unanimous, not reopening

1. **Ending (a).** She is still there. She still will not come. Nobody is
   rescued.
2. **The flash-forward is cut.**
3. **The Caravan stays cut** — three pages, watched from a rise. New rule: **he
   must not understand what he sees.** The moment he interprets it, it is an
   essay.
4. **The premise is genuinely distinct from Bunyan.** Bunyan's register is peril;
   this one's is grief. A reader feels it by page two.
5. **Em-dash dialogue stays** — with the rule that it must never force
   compensating attribution. If "he said / she said" starts appearing to
   disambiguate, the device has started charging rent.
6. **Hide the architecture.** No country names as section headings. Borders
   crossed without announcement, ideally without the pilgrim noticing. At least
   one deliberate violation of sequence — he should stumble back into the Waste
   from the Slow Country. The novel should lie about the diagram in the same ways
   the diagram admits to lying.
7. **Never market it as "a modern Pilgrim's Progress."** That invites judgement on
   Bunyan's terms. The lineage belongs in interviews, not on the cover.

## Sharpened by review

**Grieve has to be right too.** The strongest single insight across the three
reads. In the non-fiction's own terms she is the Jeremiah 29 exile — building the
house and planting the garden in the country she cannot leave — and *that is
obedience*. He is the Exodus crosser. Two faithful responses to one catastrophe.
If the Waste is simply a bad place and her staying is simply stuckness, the engine
degrades into a rescue plot. If her reasons are genuinely good, the question stops
being *will he save her* and becomes **which of them is faithful** — answer: both
— and that can drive two hundred pages.

**The return scene, rebuilt from the reviews.** He goes back to rescue her and
discovers she does not need rescuing. The garden is larger. The wall is finished.
There is another exhausted traveller eating at her table. She is genuinely glad to
see him — and he realises that while he has spent two hundred pages wondering
whether he abandoned her, **she has not spent one of those years waiting for him
to come back.** So the return is not about her at all. It completes something in
him. Then he leaves, and she stays, warmly. No villain, no apostasy, no
punishment.

Fold in one thing from the rejected (b): some small sign she has a road of her own
that is not his to see. One packed crate. One direction she looks.

**Write everyone toward Grieve, not toward the keeper.** The keeper's speech is
the essayist's voice wearing a coat, and it survives *once* because Steadfast
understands none of it. That is the one free wisdom-speech in the book. Nothing
Grieve says is quotable out of context and everything she says is devastating in
it. That is the target.

## Two open problems

**The Surgeon.** Flagged by two reviewers for different reasons, which strengthens
it. One: he is Allegory Character™ — a mysterious symbolic act in chapter six
existing to be explained in chapter thirty-seven. Two, and far more serious: a
God-adjacent figure performing an unconsented act in the dark will be read by some
of the most vulnerable readers — the ones this book explicitly courts — as their
own violation given divine sanction. Allegory removes the ability to add the
careful fencing paragraph the non-fiction needed. **The fiction currently has less
pastoral care than the non-fiction at exactly the point that matters most.**

My call: **cut the Surgeon as designed.** If the dark-night material needs a
figure, whatever is removed must be legible afterward as something already dead,
the act must have immediate narrative consequence rather than deferred meaning,
and the scene gets tested on readers with the relevant history before it is final.

**The title.** *The Road That Moved* is refuted by the keeper on page two — "It is
not the road that moved." Either that is deliberate irony (the title is the
pilgrim's misreading, corrected by the whole book) or it is an accident. It needs
to be a decision, because browsers will not get the joke and finishers will wonder
whether the author did. Alternatives worth weighing: **Old Grass**, **The Country
Between**, **The Country Turned**.

## Rejected

**"Write a realistic novel where the four phases are lived but never named."** One
reviewer's alternative. I would not. It discards the single element all three
praised — the concrete image of a road that is simply gone — in favour of a
harder, more generic book. The allegory is not the liability; the tidiness is, and
tidiness has fixes.

**Moving the flash-forward later.** Cut it, don't relocate it.

---

## The plan: write the Waste first

This is the best strategic advice in the three reviews and it dissolves the *no*
verdict's strongest objection — that there are 53,000 words of proven non-fiction
and 800 words of proven fiction, and **novels do not fail in their openings, they
fail in their middles**, which is the part no sample tests.

So: don't draft Part One. **Draft the Grieve section**, 15–20,000 words, the
hardest part of the book, under three constraints:

1. No more than **one** retrospective flash-forward in the whole section.
2. Nobody but the keeper speaks in aphorism.
3. Every scene must generate the **next scene**, not the next meaning.

If it holds, the book exists and we have its spine. If it doesn't, we found out at
18,000 words instead of 60,000 — and what's in hand may be a standalone novella,
*The Waste*, with the non-fiction unharmed behind it.

## The opening, with the notes applied

Cut: the narrator's nudge, the gratitude gloss, the whole flash-forward, and the
explanatory middle of the keeper's speech. Ends where the third reviewer said it
should — on the line that makes you turn the page because you do not understand
it.

> The road was gone in the morning.
>
> Not blocked. Steadfast had seen the road blocked, twice, and both times there
> had been something to look at — a slide of rock, a fallen tree, a place where a
> river had decided differently. This was not that. He came out of the lodging
> with his pack on and his boots laced and looked at the place where the road had
> been for the nineteen years he had walked it, and there was grass there. Old
> grass. Grass that had been growing a long time.
>
> He stood in the doorway.
>
> Then he walked out to where it should have been and put his boot on the ground
> and pressed, as though the road were under the turf and could be found by
> pushing. He went twenty paces north and tried again. He went forty paces south.
> He got down and put his hand flat on the grass and felt nothing but grass, and
> cold, and the small movement of something living underneath.
>
> The keeper of the lodging came out and stood with him and said nothing.
>
> — It was here, Steadfast said.
>
> — I know it was.
>
> — I walked it in.
>
> — I know you did. I watched you come.
>
> — Then where is it.
>
> The keeper looked out at the grass with the expression of a man reading
> something in a language he only half has. — I have seen this four times, he
> said. Not here. Elsewhere. It is not the road that moved.
>
> Steadfast waited.
>
> — It is the country, the keeper said. The country has turned underneath it.
> There is no walking back to a thing that is still there.

*Shorter by a third. Nothing is promised. Nothing is explained. The last line is
the one the reader carries.*
