# Review brief: a proposed allegorical novel

**I am considering writing this book and I want to know whether I should not.**

Please read the proposal below and answer the questions at the end. I am not
looking for encouragement — I have plenty. I am looking for the reasons this
fails, the places where I am fooling myself, and an honest read on whether the
prose is actually good or merely competent. If your overall verdict is that this
should not be written, say so plainly and early.

Assume I can take criticism and that vague praise is worse than useless to me.

---

## 1. Background

I have a finished non-fiction manuscript, ~53,000 words, called *The Upheaval*.
Its argument is that every major upheaval — in a person's life, and in the
Christian church as an institution — moves through four phases:

- **Disconnect** — the break. The old is left behind or taken away.
- **Disorientation** — the wilderness in between. The old map is gone, no new one
  has arrived. The longest phase.
- **Reorientation** — new bearings slowly form. Not the old thing restored.
- **Reconnect** — re-rooted. Communion restored on the far side.

It's built on Hebrews 12:26–27 (what can be shaken is removed so that what cannot
be shaken may remain), and it draws on Phyllis Tickle's observation that the
church has a major upheaval roughly every 500 years, Walter Brueggemann's
orientation/disorientation/new-orientation reading of the Psalms, and William
Bridges' work on transitions. It is careful, pastoral, and deliberately refuses
easy consolation — it repeatedly insists God is not the author of evil, that
renewal doesn't retroactively justify the casualties, and that some people never
reach the fourth phase in this life.

Reviewers rated it strong but flagged that its formal structure (every chapter
splits into a personal application and an institutional one) becomes predictable,
and that at 50k words of exposition it's a hard sell commercially.

**The proposal: write the same map as an allegorical novel instead** — in the
lineage of Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress*, which is public domain. Total fiction.
The non-fiction becomes a companion volume rather than the main event.

---

## 2. The premise

*Pilgrim's Progress* is a conversion story. Christian flees the City of
Destruction with a burden on his back and walks to the Celestial City. The road
exists the whole time; his problem is that he keeps straying off it.

This book starts elsewhere.

**The pilgrim has been walking the King's Road for nineteen years and is good at
it.** He knows the route. He has helped others onto it. And one morning he comes
out of his lodging and the road is not there — not blocked, not washed out,
*gone*, with old grass growing where it was.

The claimed distinction: in Bunyan the pilgrim strays from the road; here the road
itself is what gets shaken, and the man standing on it did nothing wrong.

Working title: **The Road That Moved.**

---

## 3. Structure

Four countries, matching the four phases:

| Country | Phase | Companion |
|---|---|---|
| **The Sundering** | Disconnect | Alone — by choice, badly. He refuses three offers of company. |
| **The Waste** | Disorientation | **Grieve.** The longest section. |
| **The Slow Country** | Reorientation | Someone younger, where he was. He becomes the one walking behind. |
| **The Table Lands** | Reconnect | A company, re-formed rather than restored. |

Beneath all four: **the Unshaken** — not a destination but bedrock, the thing he
keeps hitting whenever anything else gives way.

**The name arc.** He begins as **Steadfast** — a name he earned and is quietly
proud of, which becomes unbearable in the second country (a man called Steadfast
who cannot stand, asked his name by strangers). He starts giving his name as
**Mara**. He is renamed at the end; he does not choose it, and it is not
Steadfast.

**Grieve is the plot.** She has been in the Waste eleven years. She has a garden
there, which should not be possible, and a fire, and a door that shuts, in a
country with no houses. She is competent, funny, unsentimental, and the best
company he has had since the road went. She is never leaving, her reasons are
good, and she is genuinely not certain he is right to go. He stays longer than he
means to. Then he leaves, and she won't come — and the question of whether he goes
back for her is meant to carry the remaining two hundred pages.

**Other figures** (people first, meanings second):
- **The Cartographer** — sells maps of the Waste at a fair price. Warm, generous,
  and every map is confidently wrong. He believes them.
- **The man walking the other way** — cheerful, going back, describing the old
  country in loving detail, every detail slightly off. The pilgrim can't tell
  whether the man is lying or whether his own memory is.
- **The boy with the plumb line** — one page, measuring something small on a site
  nobody is visiting.
- **The Surgeon** — appears once, in the dark, does something the pilgrim does not
  consent to and does not understand, unexplained for two hundred pages.

**Scope:** 55–65,000 words, single POV, ~40 short scene-length chapters.

**Voice decision:** no archaism whatsoever. Bunyan wrote in the plain vernacular
of tinkers and housewives — he was being clear to people who didn't read books,
not quaint — so modern plain prose is argued to be *more* faithful to him than
thee-and-doth pastiche. The allegory is never explained on the page.

**One structural cut already made:** an earlier version had a second parallel
plot — a two-thousand-year-old Caravan halted on the plain and forced to unpack
every crate it owns, mirroring the church's institutional upheaval alongside the
man's personal one. That was cut. It now appears for three pages, watched from a
rise, and is never returned to. The institutional story would become a separate
book if this one works.

## 4. Prose samples

These are the whole point. Please react to these specifically. If the prose isn't
good enough, nothing above matters.

### Sample A — the opening

> The road was gone in the morning.
>
> Not blocked. Steadfast had seen the road blocked, twice, and both times there
> had been something to look at — a slide of rock, a fallen tree, a place where a
> river had decided differently. This was not that. He came out of the lodging
> with his pack on and his boots laced and looked at the place where the road had
> been for the nineteen years he had walked it, and there was grass there. Old
> grass. Grass that had been growing a long time.
>
> He stood in the doorway a while.
>
> Then he did the thing anyone does. He walked out to where it should have been
> and put his boot on the ground and pressed, as though the road were under the

> He went forty paces south. He got down and put his hand flat on the grass and
> felt nothing but grass, and cold, and the small movement of something living
> underneath.
>
> The keeper of the lodging came out and stood with him and did not say anything
> for a long time, which Steadfast would be grateful for later and was not
> grateful for then.
>

>
> — I know it was.
>
> — I walked it in.
>
> — I know you did. I watched you come.

>

## 5. Risks I have already identified

Listed so you don't spend your answer on them. Go past these.

- Modern *Pilgrim's Progress* imitations are mostly bad, and the label is
  tarnished.
- Allegory tends to produce characters who are walking labels rather than people.
- Allegory without genuine story tension is a sermon in costume.
- A book that explains its own symbolism is dead on arrival.
- Christian fiction as a category has a serious quality-perception problem.

## 6. Questions

Answer as many as you have something real to say about. Skip the rest.

**On the premise**

1. Is "the road disappears out from under a faithful long-time walker" genuinely
   different from *Pilgrim's Progress*, or is that a distinction only the author
   finds interesting? Would a reader feel the difference or just think *this is a
   Pilgrim's Progress knockoff*?
2. Is the premise strong enough to carry 60,000 words, or is it a brilliant
   opening chapter attached to a book that has nowhere to go?

**On the prose**

3. Judge the two samples as prose, not as concept. Are they good? Be specific
   about lines that work and lines that don't.
4. The dialogue uses em-dashes instead of quotation marks. Does that read as
   deliberate and clean, or as affectation?
5. Sample A ends by flash-forwarding eleven years and naming three things the
   reader doesn't understand yet. Does that earn attention or spend it?
6. Is the voice actually free of the things it claims to avoid — archaism,
   preachiness, over-explanation? Point at any place it slips.

**On structure**

7. Grieve has to carry roughly a third of the book, and the question *does he go
   back for her* is meant to be the engine driving the last half. On the evidence
   of Sample B, can she? Is that a real engine or a device?
8. **The ending with her.** He returns in the fourth country. Which of these is
   the right book, and why?
   - (a) She is still there and still will not come. Warm, final, nobody is
     rescued.
   - (b) She left two years after him by a road he never knew about, and he never
     finds out where she went.
   - (c) She died in the Waste.
   Please pick one and argue it before considering the others.
9. Was cutting the parallel institutional plot (the Caravan) right? It was the
   most structurally unusual thing in the design and arguably the only thing no
   other book in this tradition has done. Does keeping it as a three-page image
   preserve anything, or is that just a scar?
10. Four countries mapped to four phases is very tidy. Does the tidiness become a
    problem — will a reader feel the scaffolding?

**On the whole thing**

11. Who actually buys this book? Be concrete. If the honest answer is "people who
    already read Christian allegory," say so.
12. What is the single most likely reason this book fails?
13. **Make the strongest possible case that this book should not be written**, and
    that the author should do something else with the material instead. What is
    the something else?
